# Act 1 Scene 2 (Part Two) — Caesar Returns, Casca Reports, Cassius Plots

*This chapter takes you from the moment Caesar's procession comes back on stage to the end of Scene 2, when Cassius is left alone and tells us what he really intends. You will learn to read three things a school guide usually skims: how Brutus and Caesar each read faces; how the most important political event of the day — the offer of a crown — reaches us only through Casca's sneering prose; and how Cassius's closing soliloquy quietly rewrites everything you heard him say in Part One. Every important passage is taught line by line, and the chapter ends with two master tables, an exam section that is honest about the ICSE syllabus, and a one-minute revision sheet.*

## Where we are when Caesar comes back

At the end of Part One (Chapter 04), Brutus has promised only to think. He has told Cassius to "chew upon this", he has said he would rather be a villager than live as a Roman under the conditions Caesar is about to impose, and Cassius has answered with a sentence of studied modesty: "I am glad that my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus." Then the stage direction says that Caesar and his train enter. The games are over. The men who have been standing to one side, talking about Caesar, now watch him walk past.

The second half of the scene is built in three movements, and it will help you to hold them in your head as a shape before we go line by line.

```chain
Caesar's procession returns → Brutus reads the faces (anger, pallor, fury)
↓
Caesar reads Cassius ("lean and hungry") → Antony reassures him → Caesar admits a deaf ear
↓
The procession leaves; Casca stays → the crown offered three times, told in sour prose
↓
Casca leaves; Brutus and Cassius judge him → Brutus makes an appointment for tomorrow
↓
Brutus leaves → Cassius alone: the forged letters and the plan to "shake" Caesar
```

Notice what Shakespeare has done with the stage. In Part One, Caesar was present but Brutus and Cassius talked about him from the edge of the crowd. Now the positions reverse: Caesar walks through, notices Cassius, and talks about him. The watchers are watched. That symmetry is not an accident, and by the end of this chapter you will be able to explain why it matters.

::: level LEVEL 1 — What happened?
Caesar returns from the Lupercalia games with a face like thunder. Brutus, who has been talking privately with Cassius, notices that Caesar looks angry, that his followers look as if they have been scolded, that Calpurnia is pale and that Cicero has the fierce look he wears when he loses an argument in the Senate. Caesar, passing, tells Antony that he distrusts thin, thoughtful men like Cassius and prefers contented, well-fed ones; Antony says Cassius is a noble, well-disposed Roman; Caesar insists he fears nobody, then asks Antony to come to his right side because his left ear is deaf. The procession leaves. Casca, pulled back by Brutus, reports in blunt prose that Antony offered Caesar a crown three times, that Caesar refused it each time (though, in Casca's opinion, he wanted it), that the crowd cheered every refusal, and that Caesar then fainted in the marketplace. Casca adds that Cicero spoke Greek, and that the two tribunes from Scene 1 have been silenced for stripping decorations from Caesar's statues. Casca leaves. Brutus and Cassius agree to meet tomorrow. Alone, Cassius reveals that he will throw forged letters through Brutus's windows to push him towards the conspiracy.
:::

## Brutus reads the returning procession

Before the procession arrives, Cassius gives Brutus a small instruction that quietly shapes everything that follows:

> **Cassius:** As they pass by, pluck Casca by the sleeve, / And he will, after his sour fashion, tell you / What hath proceeded worthy note today.

Two things are worth pausing on. First, Cassius already knows Casca's temperament — his "sour fashion" (bitter, sarcastic manner) — which tells you that Cassius has been studying men for some time; he knows who will talk and how. Second, Shakespeare is preparing you to receive the most important event of the day (the crown) through a witness who is pre-labelled as cynical. Keep that in mind; we will return to it in the first PROFESSOR'S INSIGHT.

### "The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow" — reading faces from a distance

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow, / And all the rest look like a chidden train. / Calpurnia's cheek is pale, and Cicero
> Looks with such ferret and such fiery eyes / As we have seen him in the Capitol, / Being crossed in conference by some senators.

**Simple English:** Look, Cassius — there is a red flush of anger on Caesar's forehead, and everyone around him looks like a group of people who have just been told off. Calpurnia's face is white, and Cicero's eyes have that sharp, angry, ferret-like glare he gets in the Senate when other senators contradict him in debate.

**Difficult words:** *spot* — here, a flush or patch of colour (of anger) on the skin; *doth glow* — is burning, is bright (the anger is visible as heat); *brow* — forehead; *chidden* — scolded, rebuked (from the old verb *chide*); *train* — the followers or retinue who walk behind a great man; *ferret* — a small, fierce hunting animal with reddish, glittering eyes; used here as an adjective, 'ferret-like'; *fiery* — blazing, furious; *the Capitol* — the Capitoline Hill, where Shakespeare (following his sources loosely) imagines the Senate meeting; *crossed* — contradicted, opposed; *in conference* — in debate or discussion.

**Literal meaning:** Brutus describes the physical appearance of five things in six lines: Caesar's forehead, the followers' collective expression, Calpurnia's colour, Cicero's eyes, and a remembered occasion in the Senate when Cicero looked the same way.

**Subtext:** Something has gone badly wrong at the games, and Brutus can read the results on every face before anyone has said a word. The people who were meant to be celebrating look punished. This tells him — and us — that the shouting we heard was not a simple triumph for Caesar.

**Hidden meaning:** The whole group's mood is set by one man's mood. When Caesar is angry, "all the rest" look scolded. That is a picture of a court, not of a republic of equals. Brutus does not say this; he simply observes it, and the observation does part of Cassius's work for him. Shakespeare lets Brutus's own eyes confirm what Cassius has been arguing: Rome has become a place organised around a single temper.

**Character psychology:** Brutus here is an acute observer — calm, exact, attentive to colour and detail. This matters because a lazy reading of Brutus makes him an unworldly dreamer. He is not; he sees clearly. His tragedy is not that he fails to perceive but that he will later reason himself past what he perceives. Notice too the fairness of his attention: he looks at Calpurnia's suffering as well as Caesar's anger.

**Political meaning:** Cicero is the great senatorial voice of the old Republic, and Brutus has seen him "crossed" — contradicted — by other senators. The scene thus reminds us that the Senate is not a united body of freedom-lovers; it contains men who oppose Cicero, presumably Caesar's supporters. The Republic is already divided from within, which is exactly the condition that lets a strongman rise.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Cicero (106–43 BC) was Rome's greatest orator and a lifelong defender of the senatorial Republic; he was not a member of the conspiracy and was killed on the orders of the triumvirs in December 43 BC. Calpurnia married Caesar in 59 BC and was childless; Plutarch records her ominous dream on the night before the Ides. Shakespeare's picture of the Senate meeting on the Capitol is his own simplification: historically the Senate met in various buildings, and Caesar was killed in the Curia of Pompey, not on the Capitol.

**Literary devices:** *Metaphor* — anger is an "angry spot" that "doth glow", as if a coal were burning under the skin. *Simile* — the followers "look like a chidden train". *Animal imagery* — Cicero's "ferret" eyes: small, red, restless, predatory; the ferret is a hunter that goes down holes, and Cicero is a debater who pursues an opponent. *Alliteration* — "ferret and such fiery" drives the phrase forward. *Colour contrast* — the red glow on Caesar's brow set against Calpurnia's pale cheek.

**Effect:** In six lines the audience is told, without a single word of dialogue from the procession, that the celebration has soured. Suspense is created: we want to know what happened, and Shakespeare will make us wait for Casca to tell us.

**Dramatic significance:** This speech turns the stage into a picture we must interpret. Brutus is doing what the whole play asks its audience to do — reading signs. It also sets up the next exchange, in which Caesar, in turn, reads Cassius's face and body.

**Foreshadowing:** Calpurnia's pallor anticipates her fear in Act 2 Scene 2, when her dream and the omens make her beg Caesar to stay at home. The "angry spot" anticipates the irritable, absolute Caesar of Act 3 Scene 1, who brushes aside petitions before the murder. Cicero's fierce dissent points forward to Act 2 Scene 1, where the conspirators debate whether to recruit him and Brutus refuses.

**Scene connection:** Scene 2 is a scene of face-reading from beginning to end. Cassius began by reading Brutus's face ("I have not from your eyes that gentleness"), then offered to be Brutus's mirror; now Brutus reads Caesar's face; in a moment Caesar will read Cassius's. Go back to Chapter 04 and you will see that the mirror motif — "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection" — governs this passage too: each man learns about power by looking at another's face.

**ICSE keywords:** observation; visual imagery; simile; animal imagery; foreshadowing; court versus republic; dramatic tension.

**Exam application:** If an extract question asks what Brutus's description reveals, do not simply list the faces. Interpret them: 'Brutus's picture of a "chidden train" shows a Rome in which everyone's mood depends on Caesar's temper, and Calpurnia's pale cheek quietly prepares us for her terror in Act 2 Scene 2.'

## Caesar's opinion of Cassius

Caesar does not speak to Brutus or Cassius as he passes. He speaks to Antony — but he speaks about Cassius, and he does so within earshot of the audience. This is the first time in the play we hear Caesar in something like a private register: not giving orders, not dismissing a soothsayer, but confiding. What he confides is a theory of men.

### "Let me have men about me that are fat" — the physical description of Cassius

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Caesar:** Let me have men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights. / Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous.

**Simple English:** I want the men around me to be well fed, smooth-haired and able to sleep at night. That Cassius over there looks thin and hungry. He thinks too much. Men like that are dangerous.

**Difficult words:** *fat* — well fed, comfortable, contented (not an insult in this context); *Sleek-headed* — with smooth, glossy, well-groomed hair, the mark of a man at ease with himself; *a-nights* — at night (an old adverbial form); *Yond* — that one over there (short for *yonder*); *lean* — thin, spare; *hungry look* — an appearance of wanting something he does not have; *dangerous* — threatening, a risk to me.

**Literal meaning:** Caesar states a preference for contented men who sleep well, contrasts Cassius's thin and restless appearance with it, and concludes that a thinker like Cassius is a threat.

**Subtext:** Caesar has noticed Cassius watching him. The word "hungry" is not about food; it names appetite — for honour, for position, for what Caesar has. Caesar reads that appetite in a body, and he is right.

**Hidden meaning:** A ruler who wants only fat, sleek, sleeping men around him is asking for a court of flatterers who never think. That is exactly the environment in which a tyrant is both comfortable and blind. Caesar's preference is intelligent as self-protection and disastrous as statecraft: the men who will kill him are the thinkers he wishes to exclude, and the men he wishes to keep (Antony) cannot see the danger.

**Character psychology:** Here is Caesar the connoisseur of human types. He sorts people by appearance, appetite and sleep — a general's habit, formed over years of judging soldiers. It is also the psychology of a man who has begun to think in terms of 'men about me': a personal circle, a retinue, a court. The republican vocabulary of colleagues and equals has vanished from his speech.

**Political meaning:** In a republic, a magistrate does not choose the men "about" him; he shares power with elected equals. Caesar's sentence assumes he can select his company like a king choosing courtiers. The line is therefore evidence for Cassius's case even as it warns against Cassius.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch reports Caesar saying that he did not fear fat, long-haired men, but the pale, thin ones — and Plutarch explains that he meant Brutus and Cassius. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — Shakespeare aims the remark at Cassius alone. One strong reading is that this protects Brutus's image (Caesar never suspects him, which makes the betrayal more painful) and sharpens the contrast between the two conspirators.

**Literary devices:** *Antithesis* — the fat, sleek sleepers against the lean, hungry thinker. *Synecdoche/physiognomy* — the body stands for the mind: Cassius's leanness signifies discontent. *Aphorism* — "Such men are dangerous" has the clipped finality of a maxim; Caesar speaks in rules. *Irony* — Caesar diagnoses the disease precisely and then refuses the cure.

**Effect:** The audience, having spent Part One inside Cassius's resentment, suddenly sees him from the outside, through Caesar's eyes — and Caesar's picture matches what we have just heard. This double view makes Cassius more vivid and Caesar more formidable.

**Dramatic significance:** For the first time, Caesar is shown to be perceptive. If Shakespeare had made Caesar a fool, his fall would be merely deserved; by making him see the danger and wave it away, Shakespeare makes the fall tragic.

**Foreshadowing:** The wish for men "such as sleep a-nights" prepares us for Act 2 Scene 1, where Brutus confesses that he has not slept since Cassius first stirred him against Caesar. The sleepless conspirator is the exact opposite of the man Caesar wants near him.

**Scene connection:** Cassius has just told Brutus "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great?" — a hungry man's question about another man's feeding. Now Caesar answers, unknowingly, in the same vocabulary: he wants fat men, and he sees that Cassius is hungry. The scene's food imagery (fat, lean, hungry, meat, and later sauce, stomach, appetite and "chew upon this") binds the two conversations together.

**ICSE keywords:** characterisation through physical description; antithesis; aphorism; irony; physiognomy; foreshadowing; imagery of appetite.

**Exam application:** A strong answer uses the line to show two things at once: 'Caesar's remark that Cassius "thinks too much" reveals both his shrewd judgement of men and the flaw that destroys him, for a ruler who surrounds himself only with "sleek-headed men" has chosen comfort over truth.'

### Antony's reassurance — "He is a noble Roman, and well given"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Antony:** Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given.

**Simple English:** Do not fear him, Caesar; he is not dangerous. He is a noble Roman with a good disposition.

**Difficult words:** *Fear him not* — do not be afraid of him; *noble* — of high birth and, by implication, high character; *well given* — well disposed, of good inclination and temperament.

**Literal meaning:** Antony dismisses Caesar's suspicion and vouches for Cassius's character.

**Subtext:** Antony either does not see what Caesar sees, or he sees it and chooses to soothe his master in public. Either way, the reassurance is worthless: the audience has just heard Cassius call Caesar "such a thing" and swear he would rather not live than live in awe of him.

**Hidden meaning:** The word "noble" is doing dangerous work. Cassius is noble in rank; whether he is noble in conduct is precisely what the play will put on trial. Antony uses the word as if rank guaranteed virtue, and Caesar, who prides himself on reading men, hears an empty compliment.

**Character psychology:** This is the Antony of Act 1 — loyal, easy, quick to comfort, more interested in the games than in political undercurrents. It is a genuine part of him; Caesar will describe him in a moment as a man who loves plays and music. The audience should store this impression, because the Antony of Act 3 — the reader of crowds, the improviser, the avenger — will seem to have been born in the instant he sees Caesar's corpse. Shakespeare plants the easy Antony here so that the transformation later is a shock.

**Political meaning:** Antony is Caesar's colleague as consul and his most trusted lieutenant, yet his political intelligence in this scene is nil. Caesar's court is loyal but not watchful. The person who could have protected him tells him there is nothing to fear.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Mark Antony (83–30 BC) had served under Caesar in Gaul from 54 BC, commanded his left wing at Pharsalus and was his fellow consul in 44 BC; he had a well-known reputation for revelry, which Shakespeare uses. Cassius (c. 86–42 BC) had fought against Caesar under Pompey and had been pardoned by him — a man with every reason, in Roman terms, to resent the mercy that bound him.

**Literary devices:** *Dramatic irony* — the audience knows what Antony does not. *Antithesis* — the flat denial "he's not dangerous" answers Caesar's "Such men are dangerous" word for word, making the disagreement crisp. *Epithet* — "noble Roman" is a formula, and formulas are what men use when they have not looked closely.

**Effect:** The audience feels a small chill: the one man in a position to warn Caesar has waved the warning away. Our knowledge is now painfully greater than Caesar's.

**Dramatic significance:** This exchange establishes the pattern of the whole tragedy — Caesar receives accurate warnings and is talked out of them. Here the talker is loyal Antony; in Act 2 Scene 2 it will be Decius Brutus; in Act 3 Scene 1 Caesar will talk himself out of them.

**Foreshadowing:** Antony's misjudgement of the conspirators here contrasts with his devastating accuracy about them later: in Act 5 Scene 1 he will remind Cassius and Brutus of how they smiled and fawned on Caesar while planning to kill him. The man who once said Cassius was "not dangerous" becomes the man who understands the conspirators best.

**Scene connection:** Cassius has spent Part One insisting that he is not a flatterer — that he does not "fawn on men and hug them hard / And after scandal them". Antony's reassurance shows the flatterer's opposite danger: honest good nature that fails to see.

**ICSE keywords:** dramatic irony; loyalty; misjudgement; characterisation by contrast; the contested word 'noble'.

**Exam application:** Use this when writing about Antony's development: 'In Act 1 Antony can call Cassius "a noble Roman, and well given"; by Act 3 he has become the play's most penetrating reader of men — Shakespeare plants the easy-going lieutenant so that the avenger's emergence astonishes us.'

### Caesar's political intelligence — "He reads much, / He is a great observer"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Caesar:** He reads much, / He is a great observer, and he looks / Quite through the deeds of men. He loves no plays, / As thou dost, Antony; he hears no music; / Seldom he smiles, and smiles in such a sort / As if he mocked himself and scorned his spirit / That could be moved to smile at anything.

**Simple English:** He reads a great deal; he watches everything, and he sees straight through what men do to their real motives. He does not enjoy plays the way you do, Antony; he does not listen to music. He rarely smiles, and when he does, it is as though he were laughing at himself and despising his own spirit for being capable of smiling at anything.

**Difficult words:** *observer* — one who watches and notices; *looks / Quite through* — sees right through, penetrates to the truth beneath; *deeds* — actions; *plays* — theatrical performances; *sort* — manner, way; *mocked* — ridiculed; *scorned* — despised; *spirit* — inner self, temperament; *moved* — stirred, prompted.

**Literal meaning:** Caesar lists Cassius's habits: reading, observation, penetration into motives, indifference to entertainment and music, rare and self-mocking smiles.

**Subtext:** Caesar is describing a man who cannot be pleased and cannot be bought, because he takes no pleasure in the things (plays, music, feasting) by which a ruler usually buys men. That makes Cassius immune to Caesar's favour — and therefore uncontrollable.

**Hidden meaning:** Caesar is also, without knowing it, describing the audience's own experience of Part One. We watched Cassius observe Brutus, read his face, look "Quite through" his silence. Caesar's portrait is a summary of the seduction we have just seen. The dramatist lets his character review the play so far.

**Character psychology:** This is a brilliant piece of psychological portraiture — and it is Caesar's, not Cassius's. The detail about the smile is extraordinary: Cassius smiles as if he despised himself for smiling. That is the self-mockery of a man who thinks joy is a weakness. It matches everything Cassius has said about not being "a common laughter" and about preferring death to living in awe. Caesar sees the self-contempt at the root of Cassius's envy. The great irony is that a man this perceptive about Cassius sees nothing at all about Brutus.

**Political meaning:** Caesar's list is a portrait of the ideal conspirator: literate, watchful, sceptical, humourless, unmoved by public spectacle. In a political culture that runs on spectacle — triumphs, festivals, crowns offered in the marketplace — the man who "loves no plays" is the man the spectacle cannot capture. Caesar, whose power depends on performance, instinctively fears the one spectator who refuses to applaud.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Cassius was an Epicurean, a philosophical school that valued reasoned observation and was sceptical of superstition (this makes his later reading of the storm in Scene 3 a departure that Chapter 06 examines). Antony's love of entertainment is well attested in the ancient sources. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — the idea that a man who "hears no music" is morally suspect was a familiar Elizabethan notion; in *The Merchant of Venice* Shakespeare gives Lorenzo a speech arguing that a man with no music in himself is not to be trusted. Caesar's remark draws on that shared assumption.

**Literary devices:** *Accumulation* — the portrait is built from a list of short clauses, each adding a trait. *Contrast* — Cassius set against Antony ("As thou dost, Antony") so that the two men are defined by each other. *Paradox* — a smile that scorns smiling. *Characterisation by report* — we learn Cassius's nature through another character's description, a technique this scene uses repeatedly.

**Effect:** The audience is unsettled by how right Caesar is. Our sense of Caesar's intelligence rises sharply at the very moment we know his intelligence will not save him.

**Dramatic significance:** This passage prevents the play from becoming a simple story of a tyrant's blindness. Caesar sees. The tragedy lies in the gap between seeing and acting.

**Foreshadowing:** Cassius's penetration into "the deeds of men" is exactly what he will use in Scene 3 to recruit Casca and in Act 2 Scene 1 to argue (rightly, as it turns out) that Antony should die with Caesar. Brutus overrules him. The observer is correct and ignored — twice over, by Caesar and by Brutus.

**Scene connection:** Cassius told Brutus that "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection". Caesar now acts as Cassius's mirror, showing us a face Cassius cannot see: a man who despises his own capacity for pleasure. The mirror motif has passed from Cassius's hands into Caesar's.

**ICSE keywords:** characterisation by report; perceptiveness; paradox; contrast; the observer figure; irony of the unheeded warning.

**Exam application:** When asked to compare Cassius and Antony, this passage is your anchor: 'Caesar himself supplies the contrast — Cassius "loves no plays" and "hears no music", while Antony does; the man who cannot be entertained cannot be bought, and Caesar recognises the danger in the very qualities that make Cassius the conspiracy's engine.'

### Caesar's fear and his self-contradiction — "for always I am Caesar" and "this ear is deaf"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Caesar:** Would he were fatter! But I fear him not. / Yet if my name were liable to fear, / I do not know the man I should avoid / So soon as that spare Cassius.

> **Caesar:** I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar. / Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him.

**Simple English:** I wish he were fatter! But I am not afraid of him. And yet, if the name 'Caesar' were capable of fear, I do not know anyone I would avoid sooner than that thin Cassius. […] I am telling you what ought to be feared, not what I fear — because I am always Caesar. Come round to my right side, since this ear is deaf, and tell me honestly what you think of him.

**Difficult words:** *Would he were* — I wish he were; *liable to* — subject to, capable of; *avoid* — keep away from; *So soon as* — as quickly as, sooner than; *spare* — thin, lean; *what is to be feared* — what deserves fear (in general); *always* — at all times, unchangingly; *right hand* — right side; *deaf* — unable to hear; *truly* — honestly.

**Literal meaning:** Caesar repeats that he is not afraid, then admits that if his name could feel fear Cassius is the man he would most avoid; he claims to be describing fear in the abstract rather than his own; and he asks Antony to move to his hearing side and give an honest opinion.

**Subtext:** Every denial of fear is followed by a description of fear. "I fear him not" — but here is the man I would avoid. "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear" — but the two are the same man. Caesar is afraid, and the shape of his sentences shows it even as their content denies it.

**Hidden meaning:** Look at the grammar of "if my name were liable to fear". Caesar does not say 'if I were'. He splits himself into the man and the name. The name 'Caesar' cannot fear; the man, whose ear is deaf, plainly can. Cassius attacked the name in Part One — "what should be in that 'Caesar'?" — arguing that it is only a sound. Caesar now takes refuge inside that sound. He has begun to believe that 'Caesar' is a thing greater than himself, and he speaks of it in the third person: "always I am Caesar".

**Character psychology:** This is the psychology of a man performing invulnerability to himself. The third-person self-reference is not vanity for its own sake; it is a coping device. A general who has survived civil war cannot afford to look afraid, so he invents a public self that is exempt from fear and then hides the private self behind it. The request that follows — "tell me truly" — is poignant: the man behind the name does want honest counsel, but he has just told his counsellor that Caesar does not fear, which makes honest counsel almost impossible to give.

**Political meaning:** Caesar's constancy — "always I am Caesar" — is the foundation of his claim to rule: he is the fixed point around which changeable Rome can organise itself. But a republic distrusts fixed points; its whole design (two consuls, annual elections, tribunes with vetoes) exists to prevent any one man from being 'always' anything. The line is therefore both a boast and, to republican ears, a confession.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — no ancient source records that Caesar was deaf; the deaf ear appears to be Shakespeare's invention. By contrast, the "falling sickness" that Casca is about to report is in Plutarch and Suetonius. Shakespeare therefore adds a bodily weakness of his own to those his sources gave him.

**Literary devices:** *Antithesis* — "what is to be feared" against "what I fear". *Illeism* — the habit of referring to oneself in the third person ("always I am Caesar"), which Shakespeare makes Caesar's verbal signature. *Irony* — the assertion of permanence ("always") is followed within a line by the admission of a permanent defect. *Juxtaposition* — the grand abstract claim and the small physical fact placed side by side. *Bathos* — the descent from the majestic to the mundane deflates the boast.

**Effect:** The audience experiences a double response: awe at the confidence and a flicker of pity or unease at the deaf ear. Shakespeare wants both. A Caesar who was only a tyrant would not need to be pitied; a Caesar who was only a victim would not need to be killed.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the moment the play defines its Caesar: a colossal public self mounted on a fallible private body. Everything Cassius said about the fever in Spain and the failed swim is confirmed from Caesar's own mouth.

**Foreshadowing:** "always I am Caesar" is the seed of the great constancy speech of Act 3 Scene 1, in which Caesar compares himself to the northern star, the one fixed point in a moving sky — moments before he is stabbed. The deaf ear anticipates every warning he will fail to hear: the Soothsayer, Artemidorus's letter, Calpurnia's pleading.

**Scene connection:** In Part One Cassius catalogued Caesar's body — the shaking fever, the coward lips, the cry for help in the Tiber. Caesar now adds an item to Cassius's list himself. The scene has been arranged so that the slander and the confession meet.

**ICSE keywords:** illeism (third-person self-reference); hubris; antithesis; irony; the body politic and the body natural; foreshadowing; appearance versus reality.

**Exam application:** This is one of the most useful passages in Act 1 for any later answer on Caesar's character: 'When Caesar declares "for always I am Caesar" and in the same breath asks Antony to move to his good ear because "this ear is deaf", Shakespeare sets the immortal name against the mortal body — the same gap that Antony will exploit when he shows the crowd the wounds in Act 3.'

::: insight Why Caesar's deaf ear matters — Shakespeare's invention and the great man's body
No ancient source gives Caesar a deaf ear. Plutarch and Suetonius give him epilepsy; Shakespeare takes that and then adds deafness, on his own authority, at a very precise moment — the instant after Caesar has claimed to be permanently, unchangeably himself. Ask why a dramatist would invent a disability for his title character. One answer is theatrical: the actor must physically turn, so the audience *sees* the great man rearrange himself around a weakness. A second answer is structural: the play is built on the gap between the *name* of Caesar and the *body* of Caesar. Cassius has slandered the body (the fever, the near-drowning); Casca is about to describe it collapsing in the marketplace; Antony will exhibit it, wounded, in the Forum; and in Acts 4 and 5 the name will go on working after the body is dead, as the conspirators feel Caesar's spirit walking abroad. The deaf ear is the smallest, most intimate item in that catalogue, and the only one Caesar reports himself. A third answer is symbolic, and here you must be careful to say 'many readers feel' rather than 'Shakespeare means': a man who cannot hear on one side is a man who will not hear warnings, and Caesar is about to ignore several. The line rewards you in an exam precisely because it is small — it shows you have noticed how Shakespeare undercuts the boast with the body, rather than merely repeating that Caesar was proud.
:::

::: history Plutarch's thin men, and how Shakespeare re-aims the remark
HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch, in the *Life of Caesar* that Shakespeare read in Sir Thomas North's 1579 English translation, records that Caesar said he did not fear fat, long-haired men, but the pale and thin ones — and that he meant Brutus and Cassius. Plutarch also records the epilepsy (the "falling sickness"), the Lupercalia offer of a diadem, and the deposition of the two tribunes. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — Shakespeare keeps the fat-and-thin idea but points it only at Cassius and removes Brutus from Caesar's suspicion entirely. The effect is to make Caesar's trust in Brutus complete, so that the betrayal in Act 3 lands with full weight, and to make Cassius the play's official 'dangerous man' — a label that his soliloquy at the end of this scene will confirm from the inside.
:::

::: level LEVEL 2 — What does it mean?
The procession passage means three things. First, Rome has become a place where one man's anger scolds a whole crowd of senators — Brutus sees this without being told. Second, Caesar is not blind: he reads Cassius exactly, down to the self-mocking smile, and understands that a man who takes no pleasure in plays or music cannot be bought with favours. Third, Caesar's response to what he sees is to deny fear and take refuge in his name — "always I am Caesar" — while his body, in the same breath, admits a deaf ear. The meaning is not 'Caesar is arrogant'. It is that Caesar has split himself into an unchanging public name and a fallible private man, and has chosen to act as the name. That choice is what makes the warnings of Acts 2 and 3 useless to him.
:::

## Casca's report: the crown offered three times

The procession leaves to a trumpet signal (the stage direction calls it a *sennet*), and one man is left behind, because Brutus has caught hold of his cloak. Casca's first words are a grumble — "You pulled me by the cloak. Would you speak with me?" — and from that moment the scene changes key. Brutus and Cassius have spoken in verse throughout. Casca speaks in **prose**. In Scene 1 prose belonged to the commoners and verse to the tribunes; here a patrician chooses prose, and the choice is a character note. Casca's plainness is a style he puts on, a way of refusing to be impressed. Keep that in mind when Cassius later calls it his "tardy form".

Brutus asks what has happened "That Caesar looks so sad" (*sad* here means serious, grave — not tearful). Casca's first answer is a tease — "Why, you were with him, were you not?" — and only then does he begin the story that the whole first half of the scene has been waiting for.

::: insight Why the crown offering is reported by a cynic rather than shown
Shakespeare could have staged the offer of the crown. He had a stage, an Antony, a Caesar and a crowd of hired men. Instead he sends the procession off, brings back one sour witness, and makes us hear the day's central political event second-hand, in prose, from a man who says twice "to my thinking" and once "I did not mark it". Why? Partly economy — a marketplace of thousands cannot be shown — but the deeper reason is that the play is about interpretation. From Scene 1 onward, Rome is a city of people construing signs: the tribunes construe the holiday, Cassius construes Brutus's face, Cicero will say in Scene 3 that "men may construe things after their fashion". By denying us the event itself, Shakespeare places us where every Roman stands: dependent on a report, obliged to judge the reporter. Casca's opinion that Caesar "would fain have had it" is exactly that — an opinion — and Shakespeare flags it as one. We never learn whether Caesar wanted the crown. That uncertainty is the play's engine: Brutus will kill Caesar for an ambition that is never demonstrated on stage, only reported and inferred. A school guide will tell you that Casca 'reveals Caesar's hypocrisy'. The truer statement is that Casca reveals how Rome reads Caesar, and that Shakespeare has deliberately left the man himself unreadable.
:::

### "he put it by with the back of his hand" — the first telling

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** Why, there was a crown offered him; and, being offered him, he put it by with the back of his hand, thus, and then the people fell a-shouting.

> **Casca:** Ay, marry, was 't, and he put it by thrice, every time gentler than other; and at every putting-by, mine honest neighbours shouted.

**Simple English:** Well, someone offered him a crown, and when it was offered he pushed it away with the back of his hand — like this — and then the people started shouting. […] Yes, indeed it was offered three times, and he pushed it away three times, each time more gently than the last; and every time he pushed it away, my honest neighbours in the crowd cheered.

**Difficult words:** *put it by* — pushed it aside, refused it; *thus* — like this (Casca demonstrates the gesture on stage); *fell a-shouting* — began to shout; *marry* — a mild oath, 'indeed', 'by the Virgin Mary' (an Elizabethan word in a Roman mouth); *was 't* — was it; *thrice* — three times; *gentler than other* — more gently than the previous time; *putting-by* — act of refusal; *mine honest neighbours* — the people standing near me (heavily sarcastic: Casca despises them).

**Literal meaning:** Casca states the bare facts — a crown was offered, refused with a backhanded gesture, and cheered — and then, under questioning, adds that this happened three times, each refusal gentler than the one before.

**Subtext:** The detail "every time gentler than other" is the sting. A man who means his refusal refuses harder each time; a man who is testing the crowd refuses more softly, waiting for the cheers to turn. Casca notices the softening and lets it speak for itself.

**Hidden meaning:** "with the back of his hand" is a gesture of contempt — the way one brushes off a fly or a beggar. Caesar's public refusal is performed as disdain, which is exactly how a man who wants to be seen as above kingship would perform it. Casca's "thus" turns the actor into a mimic; for a second the stage holds two Caesars, the real one who has just left and the parody one Casca is doing.

**Character psychology:** Casca answers each question with the minimum — "Why, for that too" — forcing Brutus and Cassius to drag the story out of him. This is not stupidity; it is a man who enjoys making his superiors wait, and who protects himself by pretending indifference to what he has watched very closely.

**Political meaning:** Three shouts, three refusals, and each shout is for the refusal. The crowd's message is unambiguous: they do not want a king. Whatever Caesar wanted, the people have told him he cannot yet have it. This is the day's real news, and it explains the "angry spot" Brutus saw on Caesar's brow.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch describes Antony, running as a Lupercus at the festival of 15 February 44 BC, offering Caesar a diadem wrapped with laurel; the crowd applauded feebly at the offer and loudly at the refusal, and Caesar finally sent the diadem to the Capitol as an offering to Jupiter. In Plutarch the offer and refusal happen twice. Shakespeare's Casca says three times — a change that gives the episode the shape of a ritual or a fairy-tale test.

**Literary devices:** *Reported action* — the central event is narrated, not staged. *Repetition* — "Why, for that too" twice, then "thrice" and "every time" build the pattern of three. *Gesture as stage business* — Casca's "the back of his hand, thus" is a written instruction to the actor to mime the gesture. *Sarcasm* — "mine honest neighbours".

**Effect:** The audience finally understands the three shouts they heard during Part One. Suspense is released, but with a twist: the refusals were greeted with joy, and Caesar is angry. The gap between what the people wanted and what Caesar wanted opens on stage.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the retrospective key to the whole first half of the scene. Every flourish and shout that interrupted Cassius's seduction now has a meaning, and the meaning is that Rome does not want Caesar crowned — which means the conspirators' fear of a king is, at this moment, unproven.

**Foreshadowing:** In Act 3 Scene 2, Antony will remind the crowd that Caesar three times refused a kingly crown at the Lupercal, and use the refusal as proof that he was not ambitious. The same event that Casca tells cynically will be retold generously by Antony — and the crowd will believe Antony. Shakespeare plants the fact here so that its later use can be measured.

**Scene connection:** The three shouts were heard in Part One: "What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." Brutus feared the opposite of what happened. Cassius made use of Brutus's fear at the time; Casca's report shows the fear was, on that day, misplaced.

**ICSE keywords:** reported action; sarcasm; pattern of three; gesture; crowd psychology; dramatic irony; public opinion.

**Exam application:** 'Casca's observation that Caesar refused "every time gentler than other" is the detail an examiner wants you to notice, because it converts a refusal into a test of the crowd — and it is this same triple refusal that Antony will later turn into evidence of Caesar's humility.'

### "he would fain have had it" — Casca's reading of Caesar's desire, and the coronet

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** I can as well be hanged as tell the manner of it. It was mere foolery; I did not mark it. I saw Mark Antony offer him a crown (yet 'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets), and, as I told you, he put it by once; but for all that, to my thinking, he would fain have had it. Then he offered it to him again; then he put it by again; but to my thinking, he was very loath to lay his fingers off it.

**Simple English:** I could no more describe how it happened than I could describe my own hanging. It was pure nonsense; I did not pay attention. I saw Mark Antony offer him a crown — though it was not really a crown, it was one of those small coronets — and, as I said, he pushed it away once; but for all that, in my opinion, he would gladly have taken it. Then Antony offered it again; he pushed it away again; but, in my opinion, he was very reluctant to take his fingers off it.

**Difficult words:** *as well be hanged as* — I could as easily be hanged as (i.e., it is impossible for me); *the manner of it* — the way it happened; *mere foolery* — utter nonsense, a silly show; *mark* — notice, pay attention to; *coronets* — small or lesser crowns, worn by nobles below the rank of king (Casca belittles the object); *fain* — gladly, willingly; *loath* — reluctant, unwilling; *lay his fingers off it* — take his hand away from it.

**Literal meaning:** Casca claims he cannot describe the event and did not watch it, then describes it in detail — including the object, the two refusals, and his twice-repeated opinion that Caesar wanted the crown.

**Subtext:** The man who "did not mark it" marked everything. Casca's pretence of inattention is a shield; behind it is the sharpest political eye in the marketplace. His contradiction — 'I didn't watch, and here is what I saw' — is a small comedy that tells us how to read his whole performance.

**Hidden meaning:** "to my thinking" appears twice. Shakespeare could have let Casca assert Caesar's desire as fact. He does not. He makes Casca mark his own reading as an interpretation, and he lets the audience hear the marking. Beneath the surface of the comedy is a warning about evidence: the conspiracy will act on a "thinking", not on a proof.

**Character psychology:** Casca cannot resist diminishing everything — the crown becomes a mere coronet, the ceremony "foolery", the crowd "honest neighbours". This is the psychology of the disappointed aristocrat who protects his pride by refusing to be impressed by anyone: not Caesar, not Antony, not the people. It is also, as the play will show, the psychology of a man who is more moved than he lets on — he is the one who strikes first in Act 3.

**Political meaning:** The coronet detail is political. A coronet is a lesser crown; if Antony offered a coronet, the ceremony was a step towards kingship rather than kingship itself — a trial, a way of accustoming Rome to the sight. "very loath to lay his fingers off it" makes Caesar's hand tell the truth his gesture denies. Whether or not Casca is right, his account is the version the conspirators will carry into the night.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — what Antony offered, according to Plutarch, was a diadem: a white band or fillet of Hellenistic kingship, wrapped with laurel. Romans had hated the very word 'rex' since the expulsion of the Tarquin kings in 509 BC, and a diadem was a Greek, eastern symbol; the offer was therefore doubly provocative. Modern historians disagree about whether Caesar staged the episode to test opinion, whether Antony acted alone, or whether Caesar was genuinely embarrassed; the ancient evidence does not settle it. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — Casca's "would fain have had it" is Shakespeare's addition; Plutarch reports the refusal, not the desire.

**Literary devices:** *Paradox/self-contradiction* — "I did not mark it" followed by minute observation. *Hyperbole* — "I can as well be hanged". *Diminution* — crown → coronet; ceremony → foolery. *Repetition* — "to my thinking" twice, which both stresses his opinion and (for the alert listener) reduces it to opinion. *Prose rhythm* — the loose, tumbling sentences imitate a man talking, not orating.

**Effect:** Laughter, then unease. The audience enjoys Casca's grumbling, and then registers that the conspiracy's main piece of evidence against Caesar is one sour man's guess about a hand.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the passage that plants the idea of Caesar's *ambition* in the play. Note how carefully Shakespeare does it: not through Caesar's words or deeds, but through Casca's "thinking". The word 'ambition' will be Brutus's justification in Act 3 Scene 2 and Antony's target in the same scene. Its evidential roots are here, and they are shallow.

**Foreshadowing:** Cassius's soliloquy at the end of this scene will promise letters in which "Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at" — hinted at obliquely. Casca has just shown how ambition is glanced at: by inference from a gesture. The method of the letters is the method of the report.

**Scene connection:** In Scene 1 the tribunes accused the commoners of forgetting Pompey and cheering Caesar. Casca's crowd cheers Caesar's *refusal* of a crown. Put the two together and you have the play's picture of the people: they love Caesar the man and reject Caesar the king — a distinction Antony will exploit and the conspirators will ignore.

**ICSE keywords:** ambition; interpretation versus evidence; unreliable narration; diminution; irony; appearance versus reality.

**Exam application:** 'Shakespeare makes Casca say "to my thinking" twice, so that the charge of ambition enters the play as an opinion rather than a fact; a careful answer notes that Brutus will later kill Caesar for an ambition the audience has never actually seen.'

### The rabblement, the stinking breath and the swoon

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** He put it the third time by, and still as he refused it the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps and uttered such a deal of stinking breath because Caesar refused the crown that it had almost choked Caesar, for he swooned and fell down at it.

> **Casca:** He fell down in the marketplace and foamed at mouth and was speechless.

**Simple English:** He pushed it away the third time, and every time he refused it the mob whooped and clapped their rough, cracked hands and flung their sweaty caps into the air and let out such a quantity of foul breath because Caesar had refused the crown that it nearly suffocated him — for he fainted and collapsed at it. […] He collapsed in the marketplace, foamed at the mouth and could not speak.

**Difficult words:** *still* — continually, every time; *rabblement* — the rabble, the mob (a contemptuous word for the common people); *hooted* — shouted, whooped; *chopped* — chapped, roughened and cracked by work and weather (some editions print 'chapped'); *nightcaps* — soft caps worn in bed; the joke is that the commoners are wearing them in the street; *a deal of* — a quantity of; *stinking breath* — foul-smelling breath; *choked* — suffocated; *swooned* — fainted; *foamed at mouth* — frothed at the mouth (a symptom of a seizure); *speechless* — unable to speak.

**Literal meaning:** At the third refusal the crowd's noise and bad breath were so overwhelming, in Casca's telling, that Caesar fainted and fell, foaming and unable to speak.

**Subtext:** Casca gives a mock-medical explanation — the crowd's breath choked Caesar — that is really an insult to the crowd. But underneath the joke lies a fact he cannot sneer away: Caesar collapsed in public at the moment of his greatest political exposure.

**Hidden meaning:** The crowd's applause is *physical* — hands, caps, breath — and it nearly kills Caesar. Shakespeare is making a picture out of a political truth: the people's approval is Caesar's oxygen, and it can also suffocate him. He depends on their breath; their breath brings him down. The swoon at the third refusal is the body's confession that the performance cost him something.

**Character psychology:** Casca's disgust at the crowd's bodies — their hands, their sweat, their breath — is class contempt of a very physical kind. He literally holds his breath: "I durst not laugh for fear of opening my lips and receiving the bad air." This is a man who would rather suffocate than share air with commoners. Remember this when, in Scene 3, he calls himself simply "A Roman".

**Political meaning:** Caesar's power rests on the very "rabblement" his aristocratic peers despise. Casca's contempt for the crowd tells you why the conspiracy will fail in Act 3: men who hold their noses at the people cannot win the people. Antony can, because he does not.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Caesar's "falling sickness" (epilepsy) is recorded by Plutarch and Suetonius; modern historians debate the exact diagnosis. Nightcaps, like the "doublet" a few lines later, are Elizabethan items: Shakespeare dresses his Roman crowd as a London crowd, which is why we call these details *anachronisms*. They are not mistakes; they are a way of making the Roman mob feel like the audience standing in the yard of the Globe.

**Literary devices:** *Hyperbole* — the breath that chokes a man. *Sensory imagery* — sight (caps thrown up), sound (hooting), touch (chopped hands), smell (stinking breath). *Anachronism* — nightcaps. *Bathos* — the crown, the cheers, and then a man face-down in the marketplace. *Contemptuous diction* — "rabblement", "tag-rag" (a few lines later).

**Effect:** Comedy at the crowd's expense, and then a jolt: the man who was "always I am Caesar" a few minutes ago has been lying speechless in the dirt.

**Dramatic significance:** The swoon completes the catalogue of Caesar's body that Cassius began: the failed swim, the fever, and now the seizure. It also explains the sour faces Brutus read on the returning procession.

**Foreshadowing:** Caesar collapsed at the feet of a crowd; in Act 3 Scene 1 he will fall at the feet of the Senate, and in Act 3 Scene 2 his body will be laid before the crowd again by Antony. The marketplace swoon is a rehearsal for the corpse.

**Scene connection:** Cassius had said, of the fever in Spain, "'Tis true, this god did shake." Casca now supplies the public version of the same truth. Brutus, who dismissed Cassius's anecdotes as "these times", now hears a witness he did not choose.

**ICSE keywords:** hyperbole; sensory imagery; anachronism; bathos; class contempt; the fallible body; mob.

**Exam application:** 'Casca's picture of the "rabblement" whose "stinking breath" nearly chokes Caesar is comic, but it carries the play's central political image: Caesar lives on the people's applause and can be suffocated by it — which is exactly what happens, in a different sense, in the Forum in Act 3.'

### "the falling sickness" — Brutus's diagnosis and Cassius's bitter pun

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** 'Tis very like; he hath the falling sickness.

> **Cassius:** No, Caesar hath it not; but you and I / And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness.

**Simple English:** Brutus: That is very likely; he suffers from epilepsy. Cassius: No — Caesar does not have it; it is you and I and honest Casca who have the falling sickness.

**Difficult words:** *'Tis very like* — that is very probable; *the falling sickness* — the old English name for epilepsy, so called because the sufferer falls down during a fit; *hath* — has; *honest* — here partly sincere, partly flattering (Cassius is recruiting).

**Literal meaning:** Brutus offers a medical explanation for the collapse; Cassius denies it and transfers the disease to himself, Brutus and Casca.

**Subtext:** Cassius's pun turns 'falling' from a medical word into a political one: *we* are the ones who have fallen — into servility, into insignificance beneath Caesar. The disease is not in Caesar's brain but in Rome's freedom.

**Hidden meaning:** The pun reverses the body politic. Traditionally the state is a body and the ruler its head; a sick head means a sick state. Cassius says the opposite: the head is healthy and the limbs are the sick ones, because they have fallen and will not rise. He is diagnosing Rome — and inviting Casca into the diagnosis with the flattering "honest Casca".

**Character psychology:** Brutus's line is characteristic: the calm, factual, generous explanation. He gives Caesar the benefit of the medical doubt. Cassius's line is equally characteristic: he cannot hear a fact about Caesar without turning it into a weapon, and he cannot be in a room with a possible ally without testing him. The contrast between the two men is complete in two lines.

**Political meaning:** This is the first time Cassius draws Casca into a "we" — "honest Casca, we have the falling sickness". Casca's reply — "I know not what you mean by that" — may be genuine puzzlement or a careful refusal to commit in public. Either way, Cassius has planted a seed that he will water in Scene 3.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch and Suetonius record Caesar's epilepsy; whether the historical Caesar had a seizure at the Lupercalia is not recorded, and the swoon appears to be Shakespeare's dramatic addition to the Lupercalia episode.

**Literary devices:** *Pun* (wordplay on 'falling' — medical collapse versus political subjection). *Metaphor* — servility as disease. *Antithesis* — "Caesar hath it not; but you and I". *Irony* — Cassius speaks of falling and will himself fall, by his own sword, at Philippi.

**Effect:** A flash of bitter wit that turns Casca's comedy into politics. The audience understands Cassius's meaning before Casca admits to it, which puts us on the inside of the conspiracy before it exists.

**Dramatic significance:** The line converts a medical anecdote into a recruiting speech in eleven words. It shows how the conspiracy grows: not through argument but through the reinterpretation of everything Caesar does.

**Foreshadowing:** Cassius's pun on falling anticipates the many falls of the play: Caesar's in Act 3 Scene 1, Cassius's own in Act 5 Scene 3, Brutus's in Act 5 Scene 5.

**Scene connection:** Brutus's medical calm here matches his fairness in reading the procession; Cassius's twist matches his method in Part One, where every fact about Caesar became an argument.

**ICSE keywords:** pun; wordplay; body politic; antithesis; recruitment; character contrast.

**Exam application:** If asked to explain the pun, do both halves: 'Brutus means epilepsy; Cassius means that he, Brutus and Casca have "fallen" into slavery under Caesar — the disease, in his view, is political, and by including "honest Casca" he begins to recruit him.'

::: history Plutarch's Lupercalia: what Shakespeare kept, changed and added
HISTORICAL RECORD — The Lupercalia was celebrated on 15 February; its priests, the Luperci, ran through Rome striking bystanders with strips of goat-hide to promote fertility (which is why Caesar asks Antony to touch Calpurnia early in this scene). In 44 BC a third college of Luperci, named the Juliani in Caesar's honour, had been created, and Antony, though consul, ran as one of them. Plutarch says Antony offered Caesar a diadem wrapped with laurel; the crowd applauded weakly at the offer and loudly at each refusal; Caesar declined twice and sent the diadem to the Capitol. Historically the triumph the commoners celebrate in Scene 1 took place in October 45 BC, four months before the Lupercalia; Shakespeare compresses the two into one day. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — Shakespeare kept the offer, the refusals and the crowd's applause; changed twice to thrice; added the swoon at the marketplace, Casca's reading of Caesar's desire, and the coronet; and, most importantly, chose to report it all rather than stage it.
:::

### The crowd as a theatre audience — "as they use to do the players in the theatre"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre, I am no true man.

**Simple English:** If the ragged common people did not applaud him and hiss at him, depending on whether he pleased or displeased them, exactly as they do to the actors in a playhouse, then I am a liar.

**Difficult words:** *tag-rag* — ragged, tattered; the riff-raff; *clap* — applaud; *hiss* — express disapproval by hissing (the Elizabethan audience's equivalent of booing); *according as* — depending on whether; *use to* — are accustomed to; *players* — actors; *I am no true man* — I am not honest (i.e., call me a liar if this is not so).

**Literal meaning:** Casca swears that the crowd responded to Caesar precisely as a theatre audience responds to actors — applause when pleased, hisses when displeased.

**Subtext:** Caesar is a performer and the people are his audience — and an audience, unlike a subject, is free to hiss. Casca means it as contempt for the crowd's fickleness; it is also an admission that the crowd holds power over the performer.

**Hidden meaning:** In 1599 the Globe was itself a new theatre, and the people who paid a penny to stand in its yard were exactly the sort of "tag-rag people" Casca describes. A commoner in the audience hears himself described by a Roman patrician as a clapping, hissing spectator — while clapping and hissing. Shakespeare turns the mirror on his own house: Rome's politics is a play, and the London crowd is its Roman mob.

**Character psychology:** Casca sees through the ceremony to the performance beneath it, which is a form of intelligence; but his contempt for the audience blinds him to the fact that the audience decides the play's fate. The conspirators will make the same mistake in Act 3.

**Political meaning:** Politics in this Rome is theatrical: crowns are offered in marketplaces, refusals are performed with the back of the hand, throats are bared. Power belongs to whoever can hold the audience. That, not the Senate, is where sovereignty actually sits — and it is a truth the republican conspirators despise and Antony understands.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — public London playhouses such as the Theatre, the Curtain, the Rose and (from 1599) the Globe drew large, socially mixed audiences whose standing spectators were famously vocal. A Swiss visitor, Thomas Platter, saw a play about Julius Caesar at a Bankside theatre — almost certainly the Globe — on 21 September 1599.

**Literary devices:** *Extended metaphor / conceit* — politics as theatre; Caesar as a player. *Metatheatre* — the play refers to its own medium. *Hyperbolic oath* — "I am no true man". *Contemptuous diction* — "tag-rag people".

**Effect:** Laughter of recognition in the theatre, and a lingering thought: if Caesar is an actor, what is genuine about him? And if the audience decides, who really rules Rome?

**Dramatic significance:** Casca gives the play its governing metaphor for public life. From here on, every public scene — the Forum speeches above all — can be read as a performance judged by an audience.

**Foreshadowing:** In Act 2 Scene 1 Brutus will tell the conspirators to hide their purposes as Roman actors do; in Act 3 Scene 2 Brutus and Antony will perform in turn before the same crowd, and the crowd will clap one and then the other. Casca has described the rules of the game they will play.

**Scene connection:** In Scene 1 the tribunes tried to end a performance — Caesar's triumph — by clearing away its audience. In Scene 2 the audience has come back and is clapping. The tribunes' failure and this metaphor belong together.

**ICSE keywords:** metatheatre; extended metaphor; the fickle crowd; mob psychology; public opinion; politics as performance.

**Exam application:** 'Casca's comparison of the crowd to spectators who "clap him and hiss him" gives the play its central image of politics as theatre; the Forum scene in Act 3 is that image acted out, with Brutus and Antony as rival players before the same audience.'

::: insight The theatre metaphor for politics — and why it cuts both ways
It is tempting to read Casca's theatre comparison as a simple sneer at the mob. Look harder at who holds power in the image. Actors do not command their audience; they court it, and they can be hissed off the stage. If Caesar is a player, then the "tag-rag people" are his judges, and his fainting fit — the body's collapse under the noise of their approval — shows how much the performance costs him. That is one edge of the metaphor. The other edge is turned towards the conspirators. If politics is theatre, then whoever performs best wins, and the men planning to remove Caesar in the name of truth and liberty will need to perform too. Brutus knows this (he will urge the conspirators to act their parts like Roman actors), but he despises the skill and so performs badly in the Forum, in prose, to reason; Antony performs superbly, in verse, to feeling. The metaphor Shakespeare gives to a cynic in Act 1 therefore predicts the outcome of Act 3. Finally, remember where the line is spoken: on the stage of a playhouse, to a standing crowd. Shakespeare is not only describing Rome; he is describing the room, and inviting his own audience to notice that they, too, are being courted. In an exam, you can use this idea whenever a question asks about the crowd, about Antony's success, or about appearance versus reality — but present it as a strong reading, not as the play's only meaning.
:::

### "plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut" — Caesar's public performance

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** Marry, before he fell down, when he perceived the common herd was glad he refused the crown, he plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut.

> **Casca:** When he came to himself again, he said if he had done or said anything amiss, he desired their Worships to think it was his infirmity.

**Simple English:** Indeed, before he collapsed, when he saw that the common herd was pleased that he had refused the crown, he tore open his jacket and offered them his throat to cut. […] When he recovered, he said that if he had done or said anything wrong, he begged their honours to put it down to his illness.

**Difficult words:** *Marry* — indeed; *perceived* — noticed; *the common herd* — the mass of ordinary people (likened to cattle); *plucked me ope* — tore open (the 'me' is a colloquial filler, roughly 'I tell you'); *doublet* — a close-fitting Elizabethan jacket (an anachronism: Romans wore togas); *offered them his throat to cut* — dramatically invited them to kill him; *came to himself* — regained consciousness; *amiss* — wrongly; *their Worships* — a mock-respectful title for the commoners (Casca's sarcasm, and possibly Caesar's); *infirmity* — illness, weakness.

**Literal meaning:** Casca reports two gestures: before the fit, Caesar bared his throat to the crowd as if inviting them to kill him; after it, he apologised to them, blaming his illness.

**Subtext:** Both gestures are theatre. Baring the throat says 'I would rather die than be your king'; the apology says 'I am your humble servant'. Casca, who has just called the crowd an audience, is showing us Caesar's two best scenes. Casca's own comment — that if he had been a working man and had not taken Caesar at his word he would deserve to go to hell among the rogues — means: I would have cut his throat, and I do not believe he meant it.

**Hidden meaning:** The throat bared in the marketplace will be the body pierced in the Senate. Caesar performs his own death as a gesture of humility, and the gesture is later made real by men who believed the humility was false. There is a terrible circularity in it: the performance of vulnerability invites the reality.

**Character psychology:** Caesar's instinct, when the crowd cheers his refusal, is to over-perform — to convert a setback into a display of Roman self-sacrifice. This is a man who cannot bear to lose a scene. The apology afterwards is more interesting: it is either sincere humility or the shrewdest move of the day, and Shakespeare, through Casca, leaves us unable to decide.

**Political meaning:** The bared throat is a bid for the people's love. In a republic, a magistrate's life belongs to the law; Caesar offers his to the crowd, as if they were his sovereign and he their servant. The gesture bypasses the Senate entirely. It is the politics of the popular leader, and it works — the women near Casca forgive him "with all their hearts".

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch does not report this gesture at the Lupercalia; he does record that Caesar, on a separate occasion, bared his neck to critics and said anyone who wished might strike. Shakespeare fuses such material into one moment. The "doublet" is an Elizabethan garment, like the nightcaps a few lines earlier.

**Literary devices:** *Dramatic gesture reported* — the report of a performance within a report. *Anachronism* — "doublet". *Animal metaphor* — "the common herd". *Sarcasm* — "their Worships". *Dramatic irony* — the audience knows the throat will indeed be cut.

**Effect:** A shiver of foreboding beneath the comedy. The image of Caesar's exposed throat stays in the mind until Act 3 answers it.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the sharpest example in Act 1 of the play's public/private Caesar: the same man who just confessed a deaf ear in private offers his throat to thousands in public.

**Foreshadowing:** The offered throat foreshadows the assassination; the apology for "infirmity" foreshadows Caesar's later claim, in Act 2 Scene 2, that he is more dangerous than danger itself — the humble and the arrogant Caesar are one man in two performances.

**Scene connection:** The three or four "wenches" who cry "Alas, good soul!" and forgive him belong to the crowd Casca has just compared to a theatre audience; Casca's verdict that they would have done the same if Caesar "had stabbed their mothers" completes his portrait of a public that judges by feeling. In Scene 1 the tribunes tried and failed to shame that same public.

**ICSE keywords:** public performance; dramatic irony; anachronism; sarcasm; appearance versus reality; foreshadowing; the fickle crowd.

**Exam application:** 'Caesar's offering "his throat to cut" is public theatre — a display of humility that Casca refuses to believe — and it acquires terrible irony when the conspirators cut that throat in the Senate, convinced that the humility was false.'

### Cicero speaking Greek — "it was Greek to me"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** Ay, he spoke Greek.

> **Casca:** But those that understood him smiled at one another and shook their heads. But for mine own part, it was Greek to me.

**Simple English:** Yes — he spoke in Greek. […] But the people who understood him smiled at one another and shook their heads. As for me, it was Greek to me — I could not follow it.

**Difficult words:** *To what effect?* (Cassius's question) — with what meaning, saying what?; *an I tell you that* — if I tell you that; *ne'er* — never; *look you i' th' face* — look you in the face; *for mine own part* — as for me; *it was Greek to me* — I could not understand it (now a proverb, and this line is its most famous early appearance).

**Literal meaning:** Cicero said something in Greek; those who understood smiled knowingly and shook their heads; Casca claims he did not understand.

**Subtext:** Educated Romans of Cicero's class spoke Greek fluently — and so, almost certainly, did Casca. His "it was Greek to me" is either a deliberate refusal to repeat a dangerous remark ("an I tell you that, I'll ne'er look you i' th' face again") or a pose of ignorance to match his pose of indifference. Many editors take it as a joke against himself; the text allows either.

**Hidden meaning:** Cicero chose Greek so that the crowd could not follow him. The smiles and headshakes tell us the remark was critical of Caesar, and shared only among those who could decode it. Dissent in this Rome has gone into a foreign language — a sign of how far free speech has retreated.

**Character psychology:** Casca protects himself twice in one breath: he refuses to report what Cicero said, and then claims not to have understood it. This is a man who has learned to say nothing quotable. His bluntness is a kind of caution.

**Political meaning:** Cicero, the Republic's greatest voice, can only criticise Caesar in code, in public, to a select few. The "ferret and such fiery eyes" Brutus saw are explained: Cicero has been silenced not by force but by prudence.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Greek was the language of learning and of the eastern Mediterranean, and Roman aristocrats were routinely bilingual; Cicero was a distinguished scholar of Greek philosophy. Shakespeare's Cicero speaks briefly with Casca in Scene 3 and is never recruited; historically he was not part of the conspiracy.

**Literary devices:** *Proverb / idiom in the making* — "it was Greek to me". *Understatement* — Casca reduces a coded political protest to something he 'did not follow'. *Gesture as communication* — smiles and headshakes replace speech.

**Effect:** A flicker of comedy that, on reflection, shows how careful everyone has become. Even the play's cleverest man cannot speak plainly.

**Dramatic significance:** It marks the limit of dissent in Caesar's Rome and prepares the news that follows — the tribunes who *did* speak plainly have been silenced.

**Foreshadowing:** In Scene 3 Cicero will say that men construe things after their fashion — a philosopher's caution that echoes his coded Greek here. In Act 2 Scene 1 the conspirators will consider recruiting him and Brutus will decline: the man who speaks only in code cannot follow another man's beginning.

**Scene connection:** Brutus described Cicero's eyes; Casca describes his words; together they complete a portrait of a senator who is furious and muzzled — the Republic's condition in miniature.

**ICSE keywords:** irony; understatement; coded dissent; proverb; characterisation of Casca and Cicero.

**Exam application:** 'Casca's "it was Greek to me" is comic, but it also records that Cicero could criticise Caesar only in a language the crowd could not follow — a measure of how far open dissent has already been driven underground.'

### The fate of the tribunes — "are put to silence"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** I could tell you more news too: Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence. Fare you well. There was more foolery yet, if I could remember it.

**Simple English:** I could give you more news as well: Marullus and Flavius have been silenced for pulling the decorations off Caesar's statues. Goodbye. There was more nonsense too, if only I could remember it.

**Difficult words:** *scarves* — the decorative bands or hangings placed on the statues (the "ceremonies" and "trophies" of Scene 1); *images* — statues; *put to silence* — silenced (removed from office, forbidden to speak; the phrase is a euphemism whose exact meaning the play leaves open); *foolery* — nonsense, trivial business.

**Literal meaning:** The two tribunes we met in Scene 1 have been punished for stripping Caesar's statues.

**Subtext:** Casca drops the news as an afterthought — "I could tell you more news too" — sandwiched between a joke about Greek and a shrug about "more foolery". The casualness is the point: in this Rome, the removal of two elected tribunes is minor gossip.

**Hidden meaning:** "put to silence" is chilling because it is vague. Deposed? Imprisoned? Killed? Shakespeare does not say, and the vagueness makes Caesar's power feel unbounded. The man who theatrically refused a crown an hour ago has, in the same day, silenced the officers whose job was to protect the people from exactly such power.

**Character psychology:** Casca's flat delivery reveals a man who has learned not to react in public. His refusal to editorialise is itself an editorial.

**Political meaning:** Tribunes were, by law, sacrosanct — untouchable. To silence them for defending the Republic's symbols is to announce that the Republic's protections no longer apply. This single sentence is stronger evidence of Caesar's tyranny than anything in the crown episode, and it is delivered as a footnote.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — in January 44 BC the tribunes Gaius Epidius Marullus and Lucius Caesetius Flavus removed a diadem placed on Caesar's statue on the Rostra and arrested people who hailed Caesar as 'rex'; Caesar had them deposed from the tribunate (and, according to Suetonius, removed from the Senate). Plutarch says the people hailed them as 'Brutuses' — a detail Shakespeare omits but which chimes with Cassius's appeal to the ancient Brutus in Part One. Historically this happened before the Lupercalia; Shakespeare places it on the same day.

**Literary devices:** *Euphemism* — "put to silence". *Anticlimax / throwaway delivery* — the gravest news is given the lightest weight. *Structural closure* — the thread begun in Scene 1 ("Disrobe the images") is tied off.

**Effect:** For the audience, who watched the tribunes give their orders in Scene 1, the news lands harder than it does for Casca's listeners. We saw the crime; we now hear the punishment.

**Dramatic significance:** Scene 1 and Scene 2 are locked together by this line. The tribunes' resistance has failed and been punished; the resistance now passes from elected officers to private conspirators.

**Foreshadowing:** The silencing of critics foreshadows the fate of dissent in the play — Cinna the poet torn apart in Act 3 Scene 3 for having the wrong name, the proscriptions of Act 4 Scene 1.

**Scene connection:** Flavius in Scene 1: "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies." Casca in Scene 2: for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, "put to silence". Cause and consequence, one scene apart.

**ICSE keywords:** euphemism; tyranny; tribunes; structural link between scenes; irony of tone; suppression of dissent.

**Exam application:** 'Casca's throwaway news that Marullus and Flavius "are put to silence" closes the thread opened in Scene 1 and gives the conspiracy its only hard evidence of tyranny — delivered, tellingly, as an afterthought between two jokes.'

### Casca's character — "quick mettle", "tardy form" and "a sauce to his good wit"

Before leaving, Casca declines supper tonight ("I am promised forth" — I have a prior engagement) and accepts dinner tomorrow with a triple condition: "Ay, if I be alive, and your mind hold, and your dinner worth the eating." The joke is dry and dark. 'If I be alive' is Casca's ordinary sourness, but the audience hears it as something more, and 'your dinner worth the eating' will prove true in a way he cannot yet know: the meal Cassius is preparing is a conspiracy. Then he is gone, and the two friends judge him.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** What a blunt fellow is this grown to be! / He was quick mettle when he went to school.

> **Cassius:** So is he now in execution / Of any bold or noble enterprise, / However he puts on this tardy form. / This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit, / Which gives men stomach to digest his words / With better appetite.

**Simple English:** Brutus: What a blunt, rough man he has become! He was lively and spirited when we were at school together. Cassius: He still is, when it comes to carrying out any bold or noble undertaking, however much he puts on this slow, dull manner. His rudeness is like a sauce on his sharp intelligence — it gives people an appetite to take in what he says.

**Difficult words:** *blunt* — rough, plain-spoken; *mettle* — spirit, temperament (spelt 'metal' in some editions; the two words were not yet distinguished, and the pun on metal that can be worked will matter in Cassius's soliloquy); *quick* — lively, alert; *execution* — carrying out, performance; *enterprise* — undertaking, venture; *puts on* — assumes, wears like a costume; *tardy* — slow, sluggish; *form* — outward manner, appearance; *rudeness* — roughness; *sauce* — a relish that sharpens the taste of food; *wit* — intelligence; *stomach* — appetite, inclination; *digest* — take in and absorb.

**Literal meaning:** Brutus is surprised at how rough Casca has become since their schooldays; Cassius replies that Casca is still spirited in action and that his sluggish manner is a disguise, his rudeness making his intelligence more palatable.

**Subtext:** Cassius is already thinking of Casca as a recruit — a man of "execution" for a "bold or noble enterprise". He has assessed Casca's usefulness before Brutus has finished being puzzled by his manners.

**Hidden meaning:** "puts on this tardy form" — the theatrical verb again. Casca *wears* dullness as a costume. In a scene about performance, even the blunt man is performing; the sourness is a mask, and Cassius, the observer who looks "Quite through the deeds of men" (Caesar's phrase), sees through it.

**Character psychology:** Brutus judges by the past and by manners — he remembers the schoolboy. Cassius judges by present usefulness. Casca himself is a man who hides ability under rudeness, either because he is disappointed or because bluntness is safer than eloquence in Caesar's Rome.

**Political meaning:** Cassius's phrase "bold or noble enterprise" is the conspiracy's vocabulary before the conspiracy exists. He will use "enterprise" again in Scene 3, of the plot itself.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Publius Servilius Casca Longus struck the first blow at Caesar on the Ides of March; he was tribune in 43 BC and died after Philippi. Cassius's confidence in his "execution" is historically well founded.

**Literary devices:** *Food metaphor* — rudeness as sauce, wit as the dish, listeners' "stomach" and "appetite". *Antithesis* — quick mettle against tardy form. *Costume metaphor* — "puts on". *Characterisation by report* — Casca is defined after he has left.

**Effect:** A moment of relaxed, almost affectionate talk that lets the audience breathe — and quietly finishes the portrait of a future assassin.

**Dramatic significance:** The exchange confirms Casca as recruitable and Cassius as the recruiter. It also completes the scene's chain of food images: fat men, lean and hungry men, "what meat" Caesar feeds on, "chew upon this", and now sauce, stomach, digestion and appetite. Power in this scene is something men eat or are eaten by.

**Foreshadowing:** "in execution / Of any bold or noble enterprise" points straight to Casca's first stroke in Act 3 Scene 1.

**Scene connection:** Brutus's "chew upon this" was an invitation to digest slowly; Cassius now speaks of giving men "stomach to digest his words". The image passes from Brutus to Cassius exactly as the initiative in the scene has.

**ICSE keywords:** characterisation; food imagery; antithesis; appearance versus reality; recruitment; foreshadowing.

**Exam application:** 'Cassius's judgement that Casca's rudeness is "a sauce to his good wit" shows the recruiter at work: while Brutus remembers a schoolboy, Cassius weighs a man of "execution" — and Casca will indeed strike the first blow.'

::: level LEVEL 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way?
Ask why the crown episode is *reported*, why it is reported by *Casca*, and why in *prose*. Reported, because the play is about how Romans read Caesar rather than about what Caesar is — the audience must be made to depend on a witness, as Brutus does. By Casca, because a cynic's account leaves Caesar's motives permanently uncertain: "to my thinking" is not evidence, and Shakespeare wants the conspiracy to rest on inference. In prose, because prose belongs to the commoners of Scene 1 and to plain speech; Casca's prose lets Shakespeare change the scene's temperature after Cassius's intense verse, and it marks Casca's bluntness as a chosen style — a "tardy form" he "puts on". Notice also the order of the news: the crown first (the spectacular, ambiguous event), then Cicero's Greek (dissent in code), then the tribunes (dissent punished), each item smaller in Casca's voice and larger in meaning. Shakespeare arranges the report so that the gravest evidence of tyranny arrives as an afterthought, which is how tyranny arrives in real life.
:::

## Brutus's parting: "chew upon this" and the appointment for tomorrow

Brutus leaves Cassius twice in this scene, and the two partings should be read together. The first comes just before the procession returns, when he closes the long private conversation with a request to be left alone with his thoughts and a sentence for Cassius to digest. The second comes after Casca has gone, when he makes an appointment for the next day and walks off, leaving Cassius alone on the stage. Part One (Chapter 04) traced the argument that led up to the first parting; here we look at the words of the parting itself, because they tell you exactly how far Brutus has moved — and how far he has not.

### "Till then, my noble friend, chew upon this" — the first parting

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** What you have said / I will consider; what you have to say / I will with patience hear, and find a time / Both meet to hear and answer such high things. / Till then, my noble friend, chew upon this: / Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us.

**Simple English:** I will think about what you have said; I will listen patiently to whatever else you have to say, and I will find a suitable time to hear and reply to such weighty matters. Until then, my noble friend, think carefully about this: Brutus would rather be a peasant in a village than call himself a citizen of Rome under the harsh conditions that this age looks likely to impose on us.

**Difficult words:** *consider* — think over; *with patience* — calmly, without interrupting; *meet* — fitting, suitable (an adjective here, not the verb); *high things* — grave, important matters (with a hint of danger); *chew upon* — think over slowly, ruminate (the image is of a cow chewing the cud); *had rather* — would rather; *villager* — a country-dweller of no rank, a nobody; *repute himself* — consider himself, call himself; *a son of Rome* — a Roman citizen, with all the pride that title carries; *hard conditions* — harsh terms (as in the terms of a surrender); *Is like to* — is likely to; *lay upon us* — impose on us.

**Literal meaning:** Brutus postpones his answer, promises to listen further at a proper time, and gives Cassius one thought to take away: he would rather give up his Roman identity than keep it on the terms that the present age seems about to dictate.

**Subtext:** Brutus is saying 'not yet' — but he is also saying 'yes, in principle'. A man who would rather be a villager than a Roman under Caesar has already decided that Caesar's Rome is intolerable. What he has not decided is what to do about it. The careful future tenses ("I will consider", "I will with patience hear") hold the door open while refusing to walk through it.

**Hidden meaning:** Notice who is giving whom something to chew on. For a hundred lines Cassius has been feeding Brutus arguments; now Brutus hands one back. It is a quiet reassertion of dignity — Brutus will not be merely worked upon; he will set the terms. But the thought he offers is more radical than anything Cassius has said: Cassius has argued that Brutus is Caesar's equal; Brutus answers that he would surrender Rome itself rather than live beneath Caesar. The pupil has outrun the teacher, and neither of them remarks on it.

**Character psychology:** Three things define Brutus here. First, deliberation: he will not answer "such high things" on a street corner. Second, honesty: he does not pretend to be unmoved, and he does not hide his conclusion. Third, the third person — "Brutus had rather be a villager" — the same habit Caesar has. Both men speak of themselves as public figures with public names. It is one of the play's quiet signals that Brutus, the man who will kill Caesar for being Caesar, shares more with him than he knows.

**Political meaning:** "a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions" treats the Republic as a family and Caesar's rule as a surrender imposed on it. The phrase "hard conditions" is the language of a defeated city accepting terms from a conqueror. In Brutus's mind, then, Rome has already been conquered — by one of her own.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — the historical Brutus had fought on Pompey's side against Caesar in 49 BC, had been pardoned after Pharsalus, and had since been promoted by Caesar to the governorship of Cisalpine Gaul and the urban praetorship for 44 BC. He lived, in other words, very comfortably under the 'conditions' he here says he cannot bear. Shakespeare simplifies this: his Brutus speaks as a pure republican conscience, not as a beneficiary of Caesar's mercy.

**Literary devices:** *Metaphor* — thought as food to be chewed ("chew upon this"), which joins the scene's long chain of appetite images. *Antithesis* — "villager" against "son of Rome". *Illeism* — "Brutus had rather". *Parallelism* — "What you have said / I will consider; what you have to say / I will with patience hear" balances past speech against future speech. *Euphemism* — "such high things" for the unspoken subject, conspiracy.

**Effect:** The audience hears a man refusing to be rushed and yet, in his last four lines, declaring a position from which there is no retreat. The effect is suspense of a particular kind: not 'will he?' but 'how long will it take him to admit that he already has?'

**Dramatic significance:** This is the first time Brutus states, in his own voice, that Caesar's Rome is unacceptable to him. Everything Cassius does afterwards — the dinner with Casca, the letters, the midnight visit — is built on this sentence. Cassius's reply, "I am glad that my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus", is false modesty with a sharp accuracy underneath: it was a spark, not yet a fire.

**Foreshadowing:** The villager who would renounce Rome will, in Act 2 Scene 1, reason his way to the conclusion that Caesar must die for what he might become. The postponed answer is given in his orchard, alone, at night.

**Scene connection:** Cassius had asked, "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great?" Brutus now offers Cassius something to "chew upon"; Cassius will shortly describe Casca's rudeness as "a sauce to his good wit" that gives men "stomach to digest his words". Thought, in this scene, is always something eaten.

**ICSE keywords:** deliberation; internal conflict; illeism; antithesis; metaphor of digestion; republican principle; postponement.

**Exam application:** When asked how far Cassius succeeds in this scene, quote this and measure it: 'Brutus does not agree to anything, yet his parting thought — that he "had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome" under Caesar — shows that Cassius has won the argument about Rome's condition even though he has not yet won Brutus's hand.'

### "Tomorrow… Come home to me" — the second parting and "think of the world"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** And so it is. For this time I will leave you. / Tomorrow, if you please to speak with me, / I will come home to you; or, if you will, / Come home to me, and I will wait for you.

> **Cassius:** I will do so. Till then, think of the world.

**Simple English:** Brutus: Quite so. For now I will leave you. Tomorrow, if you would like to talk to me, I will come to your house; or, if you prefer, come to mine, and I will wait for you there. Cassius: I will. Until then, think about the state of the world.

**Difficult words:** *And so it is* — that is true (agreeing with Cassius's judgement of Casca); *For this time* — for now, for the present; *if you please to* — if you wish to; *come home to you* — come to your house; *wait for you* — be at home expecting you; *the world* — the state of public affairs, Rome and its condition (not the planet).

**Literal meaning:** Brutus agrees with Cassius about Casca, says goodbye, and offers to meet tomorrow at either man's house. Cassius accepts and tells him to think about the state of the world in the meantime.

**Subtext:** This is an appointment, and Brutus makes it. He could have let the matter drop; instead he proposes a private meeting at home, out of the street and out of public view. The man who said 'not yet' has arranged the 'when'.

**Hidden meaning:** "think of the world" is a very large phrase for a very small farewell. Cassius is asking Brutus to carry the scene's whole argument home with him and to frame his private thoughts as public duty: not 'think of Caesar' or 'think of yourself', but think of the world. It is exactly the frame in which Brutus will reason in Act 2 Scene 1, where he decides against Caesar on grounds of what is good for Rome in general rather than anything Caesar has done to him. Cassius has supplied the frame; Brutus will supply the logic.

**Character psychology:** Brutus's courtesy is complete — he offers to come to Cassius, then offers his own house, and promises to "wait". This is a generous, honourable man who is also, without noticing it, making himself available for recruitment. Cassius's farewell is short because his real speech is about to begin: the moment Brutus leaves, he turns to the audience.

**Political meaning:** Politics has moved indoors. In Scene 1 it was in the street; at the start of Scene 2 it was in the open, in a festival; now it is a conversation to be held at home tomorrow. The retreat from public to private is how a conspiracy is born.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch's account has Cassius working on Brutus over a period of time and finally visiting him to ask whether he intends to be in the Senate on the Ides of March; Shakespeare compresses this into one meeting and an appointment, and then, in Scene 3, into a midnight visit to Brutus's house. Shakespeare's Cassius does not wait for tomorrow.

**Literary devices:** *Dramatic irony* — Brutus speaks of an ordinary social call; the audience, about to hear the soliloquy, knows what tomorrow is for. *Understatement* — "think of the world" compresses the whole case against Caesar into four quiet words.

**Effect:** Relief and unease together. The tone is friendly and domestic; the content is the first fixed step of a plot.

**Dramatic significance:** Brutus's exit clears the stage for the first soliloquy of the play. Shakespeare has withheld private speech from everyone until now — even Caesar's confidences were to Antony. The first man allowed to speak his mind alone is the manipulator.

**Foreshadowing:** The invitation "Come home to me" is accepted in a way Brutus does not intend: in Act 2 Scene 1 the conspirators come to his house at night, hooded, and he receives them.

**Scene connection:** Cassius's "pluck Casca by the sleeve" and Brutus's "You pulled me by the cloak" were about catching men in the street; "Come home to me" moves the action to the house where Act 2 will begin.

**ICSE keywords:** dramatic irony; understatement; private versus public; recruitment; structural transition; soliloquy prepared.

**Exam application:** 'Brutus's offer — "Come home to me, and I will wait for you" — is spoken as ordinary courtesy, but it fixes the first appointment of the conspiracy; Shakespeare lets Brutus himself propose the meeting so that his later involvement cannot be blamed on Cassius alone.'

## Cassius alone: the soliloquy

A **soliloquy** is a speech spoken by a character who is alone on stage, in which the convention of the theatre allows us to assume we are hearing the character's true thoughts. It is the most powerful instrument a dramatist has for letting an audience know more than the other characters do. Shakespeare has kept it in reserve for the whole of Act 1 so far. Now, with the stage empty, Cassius speaks fifteen lines that change the meaning of everything you have heard him say. Read the whole speech first, slowly; then we will take it in three parts.

::: quote The soliloquy in full
> **Cassius:** Well, Brutus, thou art noble. Yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed. Therefore it is meet / That noble minds keep ever with their likes; / For who so firm that cannot be seduced? / Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus. / If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me. I will this night / In several hands in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens, / Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name, wherein obscurely / Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at. / And after this, let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure.

Spoken by Cassius, alone, at the very end of Act 1 Scene 2. (Some editions print "metal" for "mettle" and "glanced" for "glancèd"; the sense is the same.)
:::

### "Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed" — the verdict on Brutus

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Well, Brutus, thou art noble. Yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed. Therefore it is meet / That noble minds keep ever with their likes; / For who so firm that cannot be seduced?

**Simple English:** Well, Brutus, you are a noble man. And yet I can see that your honourable nature can be worked — bent away from its natural inclination. That is why it is right that noble minds should always keep company only with their own kind; for who is so steadfast that he cannot be led astray?

**Difficult words:** *noble* — of high character (Cassius means it; he is not being sarcastic); *mettle* — temperament, spirit; the spelling 'metal' in some editions brings out the hidden image of a metal that can be heated and hammered; *wrought* — worked, shaped (the old past participle of *work*, as in 'wrought iron'); *From that it is disposed* — away from what it is naturally inclined to; *meet* — fitting, proper; *keep ever with their likes* — always associate with people like themselves; *firm* — steadfast, constant; *seduced* — led astray, corrupted (a stronger and more sexual word in Shakespeare's day than 'persuaded').

**Literal meaning:** Cassius concedes Brutus's nobility, then states as a fact that it can be bent; draws the general rule that noble men should mix only with noble men; and asks, as a rhetorical question, whether anyone is incorruptible.

**Subtext:** The man who spent the whole scene praising Brutus's "hidden worthiness" now coolly notes that the worthiness can be bent. "Yet I see" is the hinge: everything before it is the public Cassius, everything after it is the private one. And the general rule — noble minds should keep with their likes — is Cassius admitting that *he* is not Brutus's like. He knows that his company is corrupting.

**Hidden meaning:** The metalworking image is exact. You cannot forge iron that is already the shape you want; you forge it *away from* its shape by heating and striking. Cassius is describing the whole scene as a forge: the anecdotes were the heat, the Colossus speech the hammer, and Brutus's honour the metal that is beginning to bend. The disturbing word is "seduced": Cassius does not say 'persuaded' or 'convinced'. He knows that what he is doing to Brutus is not argument but seduction — and he does it anyway.

**Character psychology:** Here is Cassius's self-knowledge, and it is formidable. He understands Brutus's nature ("thou art noble"), his own method ("wrought"), the moral status of that method ("seduced"), and the rule he is breaking ("noble minds keep ever with their likes"). He has no illusions about himself. This separates him from Brutus, who will spend Act 2 Scene 1 constructing illusions about why Caesar must die. Cassius is the play's clear-eyed sinner; Brutus is its self-deceived saint. Note too the flash of something like regret in "who so firm" — the question could be a sigh as easily as a sneer, and actors have played it both ways.

**Political meaning:** Cassius has just identified the weakness of all republican politics in one line: honourable men can be worked upon by less honourable men. The Republic depends on virtue, and virtue can be forged. Caesar's whole case for one-man rule — that the Senate is a quarrelling crowd of men manipulating one another — is quietly confirmed by the leading republican in the play.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch presents Cassius as the instigator of the conspiracy and Brutus as the man whose reputation was needed to make it respectable; Plutarch also records that Cassius had a private grievance, having been passed over for the more prestigious urban praetorship, which went to Brutus. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — Shakespeare omits the praetorship grievance and gives Cassius instead this general theory of seduction, which makes his motives seem both more intellectual and more sinister.

**Literary devices:** *Metaphor* — the mind as metal to be "wrought"; *pun* — mettle/metal; *aphorism* — "noble minds keep ever with their likes" has the shape of a proverb; *rhetorical question* — "who so firm that cannot be seduced?"; *volta* or turn — "Yet I see" pivots the speech, and the play, from public praise to private calculation.

**Effect:** A cold shock. The audience, which may have half-believed Cassius's republican fervour, hears him describe his friend as raw material. From this moment we watch Cassius with suspicion and Brutus with pity.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the first soliloquy of the play and it belongs to the manipulator. Shakespeare could have given the first private speech to Brutus, so that we entered the conspiracy through the honest man's doubts; instead he lets us enter it through the schemer's confidence, so that when Brutus's doubts come in Act 2 we already know they have been engineered.

**Foreshadowing:** "noble minds keep ever with their likes" is a rule Brutus will break by joining Cassius, and the quarrel of Act 4 Scene 3 — where Brutus accuses Cassius of corruption and Cassius accuses Brutus of not loving him — is what happens when unlike minds have kept company too long.

**Scene connection:** In Part One Cassius told Brutus, "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of." The soliloquy shows what the glass was for: not to reveal Brutus to Brutus, but to reshape him. The mirror was a forge.

**ICSE keywords:** soliloquy; manipulation; metaphor of metalworking; pun; aphorism; rhetorical question; dramatic irony; self-knowledge.

**Exam application:** 'The soliloquy's key word is "wrought": Cassius sees Brutus's honour as metal to be hammered out of shape, and his rhetorical question "who so firm that cannot be seduced?" admits that his method is seduction, not persuasion — a confession that colours every compliment he paid earlier in the scene.'

### "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus" — the personal motive

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus. / If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me.

**Simple English:** Caesar bears a grudge against me, but he loves Brutus. If I were in Brutus's position and he were in mine, he would not be able to work on me the way I am working on him.

**Difficult words:** *doth bear me hard* — bears me ill will, has a grudge against me, treats me harshly; *humour* — to indulge, manage or win over by playing on someone's moods; here, to manipulate (some editors gloss the line differently — see below).

**Literal meaning:** Cassius contrasts Caesar's hostility to him with Caesar's love for Brutus, and then imagines the positions reversed.

**Subtext:** The first half of the first line is the only moment in the scene where Cassius admits a *personal* reason for hating Caesar. All his public arguments were about Rome, liberty and the ancestral Brutus; here, alone, he says that Caesar dislikes him. The second half — "but he loves Brutus" — is the reason Brutus is so valuable: a conspiracy that includes the man Caesar loves cannot be dismissed as the revenge of men Caesar has slighted.

**Hidden meaning:** The second sentence is one of the most discussed lines in the scene, and you should know that it is genuinely ambiguous. One reading: 'If I were Brutus — loved by Caesar — and Brutus were Cassius, I would not let him manipulate me as I am manipulating him.' On this reading Cassius admits that Brutus is more easily worked on than he himself would be, and perhaps that Brutus is the better man for being so trusting. A second reading: 'If I were Brutus and he were Cassius, he (Caesar) should not win me over with favours as he does Brutus' — that is, Cassius would not let Caesar's love buy him. The text allows both; the first is more widely accepted, and it is the one that makes the line a confession.

**Character psychology:** Cassius is honest about envy here in a way he was not when speaking to Brutus. He wants what Brutus has — Caesar's love — and he hates Caesar partly for not giving it. This is the psychology Caesar described a few minutes earlier: a man "never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". Caesar's reading is confirmed from inside Cassius's own head. The scene has arranged a perfect loop: Caesar diagnoses Cassius; Cassius, alone, proves the diagnosis.

**Political meaning:** Cassius needs Brutus not for his arguments or his sword but for his relationship with Caesar. The conspiracy's respectability depends on the one conspirator Caesar trusts. That is a cynical calculation, and it is also, in Roman terms, shrewd: Plutarch says the conspirators believed that Brutus's name alone would make the deed appear just.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Caesar had pardoned both Brutus and Cassius after Pharsalus and advanced both to praetorships for 44 BC, but ancient sources agree that he favoured Brutus and was suspicious of Cassius; the rumour that Caesar was Brutus's natural father (which modern scholars reject on grounds of chronology) shows how close the two were thought to be. Shakespeare's "he loves Brutus" is well grounded.

**Literary devices:** *Antithesis* — "bear me hard" against "loves Brutus"; *hypothetical inversion* — the swapping of roles ("If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius"), a kind of chiasmus of persons; *dramatic irony* — the audience now holds a motive the other characters never hear.

**Effect:** The audience's sympathy for Cassius drops sharply and then, for some, recovers a little: a man who can say 'he would not do this to me' knows exactly how he is behaving. Shakespeare refuses to make him simply a villain.

**Dramatic significance:** This line converts the scene from political persuasion into personal drama. Whatever the merits of the republican case, the man making it has a grudge, and the play will never let us forget it.

**Foreshadowing:** Antony, in Act 5 Scene 1, will accuse the conspirators of fawning on Caesar and then stabbing him; in Act 4 Scene 3 Brutus will accuse Cassius of an itching palm. The suspicion that Cassius's motives are personal, planted here, follows him to his death.

**Scene connection:** In Part One Cassius said Caesar had become "a god" while Cassius "must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him". Now we hear the private version: Caesar does not merely tower over him; Caesar dislikes him. The public grievance was about rank; the private one is about love.

**ICSE keywords:** envy; personal motive; antithesis; dramatic irony; ambiguity; characterisation through soliloquy.

**Exam application:** 'Only when alone does Cassius give a personal reason for his hatred — "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus" — and the admission turns his republican rhetoric into something the audience must weigh against envy; an examiner rewards a candidate who can hold both motives together rather than choosing one.'

### The forged letters — "in several hands", "glancèd at", "we will shake him"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** I will this night / In several hands in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens, / Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name, wherein obscurely / Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at. / And after this, let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure.

**Simple English:** Tonight I will throw in at Brutus's windows letters written in several different handwritings, as if they came from several different citizens, all of them dwelling on the high opinion that Rome has of his name, and in which Caesar's ambition will be hinted at indirectly. And after that, let Caesar make his seat as secure as he can — for we will shake him, or else suffer worse days than these.

**Difficult words:** *this night* — tonight; *several hands* — different handwritings (*hand* = handwriting; *several* = separate, various); *in at his windows throw* — throw in through his windows; *writings* — letters, papers; *tending to* — aiming at, dwelling on; *the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name* — the high regard in which Rome holds the name Brutus; *obscurely* — indirectly, in veiled language; *glancèd at* — hinted at, touched on in passing (the accent marks that the -ed is pronounced as a separate syllable for the metre); *seat him sure* — settle himself securely, make his position firm (the image is of sitting firmly on a throne or in a saddle); *shake him* — dislodge him; *or worse days endure* — or else put up with worse times than these.

**Literal meaning:** Cassius announces a plan: tonight he will forge letters in different handwritings, pretending they come from various citizens, flattering Brutus's name and hinting at Caesar's ambition, and throw them through Brutus's windows. Then, whatever Caesar does to secure himself, the conspirators will topple him or face the consequences.

**Subtext:** The whole speech has been building to this. The man who said "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of" now plans to manufacture a false reflection: a chorus of Roman voices that is in fact one man with several pens. The mirror is not merely a forge; it is a forgery.

**Hidden meaning:** Look at how the letters are designed. They do not argue; they flatter — "the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name" — and they accuse only "obscurely". Cassius has learned from the scene itself that Brutus cannot be pushed; he must be invited to push himself. The letters will give Brutus the two things he needs to act: the belief that Rome is asking him, and the freedom to supply the accusation against Caesar from his own mind. In Act 2 Scene 1 he does exactly that, reading the letters and filling in their gaps with his own reasoning. The word "obscurely" is the whole method of the conspiracy in one adverb: ambition is never shown, only glanced at — by Casca's "to my thinking", by Cassius's letters, and finally by Brutus's own imagination.

**Character psychology:** The man who called Caesar a swimmer who needed rescuing and a sick girl crying for drink now shows what he himself is: a forger. Yet the last couplet — "let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure" — is not cynical at all. It is the voice of a man who genuinely believes that Caesar must fall and that the alternative is worse. Cassius is both: the manipulator and the believer. The play never lets you separate them.

**Political meaning:** The conspiracy's first act is a lie to its own most honourable member. That tells you something about the Republic Cassius is trying to save: it can only be defended by methods that betray its values. Many critics see this as the moral centre of the play — the deed may be arguable, but the means are poisoned from the start. Present that as a reading, not a fact; the text invites it without stating it.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch records that anonymous messages appeared on the tribunal where Brutus sat as praetor and on the statue of his ancestor, urging him to wake up and act, and that these were understood to express the people's wish; Plutarch does not say Cassius wrote them, though he says Cassius and Brutus's friends worked on him. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — Shakespeare's decisive change is to make the letters Cassius's personal forgery and to tell us so in advance, so that when Brutus reads one aloud in Act 2 Scene 1 we know he is being deceived. A genuine popular appeal becomes a private fraud.

**Literary devices:** *Dramatic irony* — the audience knows the letters are forged; Brutus never will. *Rhyming couplet* — "sure / endure" closes the scene with a snap, a common Shakespearean signal that a scene is ending and a decision has been taken. *Metaphor* — "seat him sure" (power as a seat or saddle) and "shake him" (overthrow as physical dislodging). *Antithesis* — "shake him" against "worse days endure". *Alliteration* — "several hands… several citizens" stresses the multiplication of one voice into many.

**Effect:** The scene ends on a threat spoken directly to the audience, and the audience leaves it complicit — we know a secret that will destroy Brutus and we cannot warn him. The couplet's finality tells us the plot is now in motion.

**Dramatic significance:** This speech is the hinge between the private persuasion of Scene 2 and the open conspiracy of Scene 3. In Scene 3 we see the letters being distributed — Cassius hands them to Cinna to lay in the praetor's chair, throw in at Brutus's window and fix on the statue of old Brutus — and in Act 2 Scene 1 we see one being read. Shakespeare shows plan, delivery and effect in three consecutive scenes.

**Foreshadowing:** "we will shake him" is fulfilled in Act 3 Scene 1 — and "or worse days endure" is fulfilled too, in the other direction: the proscriptions, the civil war and Philippi are the worse days that the conspirators bring on themselves. Cassius's either/or turns out to be both.

**Scene connection:** Cassius began the scene by telling Brutus that the eye "sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things". The letters are the "other things" — manufactured reflections. The soliloquy closes the circle the mirror speech opened.

**ICSE keywords:** forgery; manipulation; dramatic irony; rhyming couplet; metaphor; ends and means; plot mechanism.

**Exam application:** This passage is essential for any answer on how Brutus is drawn in: 'Cassius plans letters "in several hands" in which "Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at" — the ambition is only hinted, the voices are forged, and Brutus will supply the rest himself; the conspiracy begins with a lie to its noblest member.'

::: insight Why Cassius's soliloquy changes how we hear everything he said earlier
A soliloquy placed at the end of a scene works backwards. Until Brutus leaves, you have been hearing Cassius's speeches as a republican might hear them: passionate, exaggerated, perhaps unfair to Caesar, but sincere. The soliloquy obliges you to replay the whole scene in a different key. "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself" now sounds like the opening move of a confidence trick. "honour is the subject of my story" sounds like a man choosing the subject his listener cannot resist. The Tiber story and the fever in Spain become not memories but exhibits. Even "I am glad that my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus" becomes a craftsman's note on the progress of the work. Yet — and this is the point a weaker guide misses — the soliloquy does not make the earlier speeches *false*. Cassius really does think Caesar a feeble man grown monstrous; he really would rather die than be a slave; "we will shake him, or worse days endure" is spoken with conviction. What the soliloquy adds is not insincerity but method: we learn that the sincere man is also a calculating one, that his flattery is deliberate and his letters are forged. Shakespeare wants you to carry both truths at once, because the play's judgement on the conspiracy depends on it. If Cassius were merely a hypocrite, Brutus would be merely a dupe and the tragedy would collapse into a crime story. Because Cassius believes what he says *and* manipulates, Brutus is seduced by a genuine argument dishonestly delivered — which is a far more painful thing to watch. In an answer, show that you have heard both: the republican and the forger are the same man, and the soliloquy is where they meet.
:::

::: level LEVEL 4 — How does it connect to the whole play?
Almost every thread of the tragedy is tied here. The public Caesar who refuses a crown three times becomes Antony's evidence in the Forum that Caesar was not ambitious; the private Caesar with a deaf ear and a fit in the marketplace becomes the wounded body Antony shows the crowd. Brutus's "chew upon this" and his appointment for tomorrow lead to the orchard of Act 2 Scene 1, where he reasons alone and reads a forged letter. Cassius's letters, planned here, are handed to Cinna in Scene 3 and read by Brutus in Act 2. Casca, assessed here as a man of "execution", strikes the first blow in Act 3 Scene 1. Antony, dismissed here as a lover of plays who says Cassius is "not dangerous", turns the Forum into a play in Act 3 Scene 2 and names the conspirators' danger exactly in Act 5. The crowd that clapped and hissed "as they use to do the players in the theatre" claps Brutus and then Antony and then tears Cinna the poet apart. The tribunes "put to silence" are the first of many silenced voices. And Cassius's "we will shake him, or worse days endure" is answered, on both sides of the 'or', by Acts 3 to 5.
:::

::: keyword Keyword box: terms the second half of the scene teaches you to use
**Soliloquy** — a speech by a character alone on stage, taken to reveal true thoughts; Cassius's closing speech is the play's first. **Reported action** — an event narrated rather than staged; the crown offering. **Unreliable narrator** — a reporter whose bias colours the account; Casca's "to my thinking". **Metatheatre** — a play drawing attention to its own theatrical nature; the crowd compared to a theatre audience. **Illeism** — speaking of oneself in the third person; "always I am Caesar", "Brutus had rather". **Physiognomy** — judging character from physical appearance; the "lean and hungry look". **Anachronism** — an object out of its time; nightcaps, doublet. **Euphemism** — a mild phrase for a harsh fact; "put to silence". **Pun** — wordplay on two meanings; "the falling sickness". **Rhyming couplet** — two rhymed lines, often closing a scene; "sure / endure". **Dramatic irony** — the audience knows what characters do not; the forged letters. **Forgery** — the letters "in several hands". **Public self / private self** — the gap between the Caesar of the marketplace and the Caesar of the deaf ear. Scene 2 is the longest scene in Act 1 and, by some distance, the most important scene in the play for a student of Acts 3 to 5, because nearly every later event is either planted here or explained by something here. Use the table below as a map. The left-hand columns are SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT; the right-hand column is where the seed grows. Later-act wording is paraphrased in single quotes so that you do not confuse it with Act 1 quotations.

## PUBLIC CAESAR / PRIVATE CAESAR

Scene 2 is the only scene in the play that shows you both Caesars on the same day, and most of the private evidence comes from Part Two. The table separates what Rome sees from what the audience is allowed to see. Remember the labels: the left two columns are SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT; the right-hand column is LITERARY INTERPRETATION.

| Aspect | PUBLIC CAESAR (what Rome sees) | PRIVATE CAESAR (what the audience learns) | What the gap tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fear** | "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear"; "always I am Caesar" | He names the one man he "should avoid / So soon as" — Cassius — and asks Antony to "tell me truly" | The denial of fear and the description of fear are the same speech; the public self is a performance for the private self too |
| **The body** | He strides at the head of a "chidden train", refuses a crown with "the back of his hand", bares his throat | "this ear is deaf"; he "swooned and fell down"; "foamed at mouth and was speechless" | The name is immortal; the man is not. The play's whole treatment of Caesar lives in this gap |
| **Kingship** | Refuses the coronet "thrice, every time gentler than other" while the crowd cheers | Casca's reading: "he would fain have had it"; "very loath to lay his fingers off it"; the "angry spot" Brutus sees afterwards | We are never shown his desire — only a cynic's inference and a flush of anger. Shakespeare keeps the private wish unprovable |
| **Judgement of men** | Wants "men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights" | Reads Cassius exactly: "He reads much, / He is a great observer… Seldom he smiles" | Caesar is perceptive about his enemy and chooses comfort over truth; the tragedy is in the choice, not in blindness |
| **Humility** | Apologises to the crowd and asks them to blame "his infirmity"; the wenches forgive him "with all their hearts" | Has the tribunes "put to silence" the same day for touching his statues | Public humility and private absolutism coexist; the apology is theatre, the silencing is power |
| **Relationship to warnings** | Dismisses the Soothsayer as "a dreamer" before all Rome | Turns his good ear to Antony because the other is deaf | The man who cannot hear on one side is the man who will not hear; many readers take the deaf ear as an emblem of this |
| **Language** | Commands and maxims: "Set on, and leave no ceremony out"; "Such men are dangerous" | Hesitations and reversals: "Would he were fatter! But I fear him not. / Yet…" | In public Caesar speaks in rules; in private his sentences double back on themselves |

::: insight The two Caesars and the problem of the 'tyrant'
Students are often taught to decide whether Caesar 'is' a tyrant, and to collect evidence on each side. Scene 2 suggests that this is the wrong question, or at least a question Shakespeare deliberately refuses to answer. Look at the evidence the scene actually supplies. For tyranny: a court that looks "chidden", tribunes "put to silence", a Caesar who speaks of having "men about me" as a king speaks of courtiers. Against tyranny: a crown refused three times before a cheering crowd, an apology to the people, a sick and deaf man of middle age. For ambition: a cynic's "to my thinking" and a flush of anger. Against: nothing Caesar says or does on stage. The balance is not accidental. Shakespeare has built a Caesar whose public gestures can be read either way and whose private wishes are never shown, so that the conspirators must act on interpretation — and so must we. This is why Brutus, in Act 2, will have to reason from what Caesar 'may' become rather than from what he has done, and why Antony, in Act 3, will be able to turn the same facts the other way. When you write about Caesar's character, resist the verdict and describe the design: a man built to be construed. That is a far stronger answer than 'Caesar was arrogant' or 'Caesar was not really ambitious', and it is the answer the text supports.
:::

## Using the second half of Scene 2 in an ICSE answer

::: icse Honest exam relevance — where this half of the scene earns marks
EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE — Act 1 is the Class IX portion of the ICSE syllabus; the Class X board paper (Paper 2, Literature in English) sets its *Julius Caesar* extracts from Acts III, IV and V. So you will not be given Casca's report or Cassius's soliloquy as a board extract. What happens instead is this: the Act 3–5 extracts repeatedly assume you know this half of Scene 2. When Antony tells the Forum that Caesar refused a crown three times, a sub-question may ask what event he is referring to and how it was first described — and the candidate who can say 'reported by Casca, who thought Caesar "would fain have had it"' is answering at a level the others cannot reach. When Brutus reads a letter in Act 2 or speaks of the people's wishes in Act 3, the knowledge that the letters were forged "in several hands" by Cassius changes the whole character answer. When Caesar claims constancy in Act 3 Scene 1, "always I am Caesar" and the deaf ear are the Act 1 roots a strong answer reaches for. In the 2025 paper, one sub-question asked what 'the work the ides of March begun' referred to — a straight test of Act 1 knowledge inside an Act 5 extract. Never claim any of this is guaranteed; it is simply how the paper has behaved. For Class IX internal examinations, this half of the scene is a natural source of reference-to-the-context extracts, and the sub-questions tend to be: who is speaking and to whom; what has just happened; explain a phrase in your own words; what does the speech reveal about the speaker; what happens next. The line-by-line sections above are arranged to answer exactly those questions. Chapter 19, 'Extract-Based Training (Part Two)', gives you practice extracts from this half of the scene with marked answers.

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
Three rules, then a worked example. **Rule one: quote small and interpret big.** A three-word quotation — "to my thinking", "this ear is deaf", "in several hands" — followed by two sentences of interpretation earns more than a six-line quotation with none. **Rule two: separate the report from the fact.** Whenever you write about the crown, make it clear that Caesar's desire for it is Casca's opinion. Examiners reward candidates who notice that Shakespeare reported the event rather than staged it. **Rule three: travel.** Connect the Act 1 seed to the later payoff in one sentence — that is what 'knowledge of the whole play' means in a mark scheme. Worked example, for a question asking what Cassius's soliloquy reveals about him: 'The soliloquy reveals that Cassius's republicanism and his envy are inseparable. He admits a private motive — "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus" — and describes his method with a metalworker's word, "wrought": Brutus's honour is metal to be hammered. His plan to throw letters "in several hands" through Brutus's windows, in which "Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at", shows that the conspiracy will begin with a forgery, and the closing couplet, "we will shake him, or worse days endure", shows that he nevertheless believes in the cause. Shakespeare makes him both sincere and manipulative, which is why Brutus's later ruin in Acts 4 and 5 feels tragic rather than merely deserved.' That paragraph is about one hundred and twenty words, quotes five short phrases, names a device, states a reading as a reading, and travels forward. It is the shape to aim for.

::: weak Weak answer — what not to write about Casca's report
'Casca tells Brutus and Cassius that Antony offered Caesar the crown three times and Caesar refused it three times. The people cheered. Then Caesar fainted because he had epilepsy. Casca also says Cicero spoke Greek and the tribunes were punished. This shows that Caesar was a hypocrite who really wanted the crown.' — This retells the plot, treats Casca's opinion as fact ('really wanted'), quotes nothing, names no device, and makes no connection to the rest of the play. It would earn little beyond the marks for basic recall.
:::

::: strong Excellent answer — the same material, properly used
'Shakespeare does not stage the crown offering; he lets Casca report it in sour prose, so that the day's most important political event reaches us filtered through a cynic. Casca twice marks his account as opinion — "to my thinking" — and his claim that Caesar "would fain have had it" is an inference from a hand that was "very loath to lay his fingers off it". The crowd, meanwhile, cheered every refusal "as they use to do the players in the theatre", an image that makes Roman politics a performance judged by its audience. Two further details carry weight: Caesar "swooned and fell down" at the third refusal, completing Cassius's earlier catalogue of the weak body behind the great name, and the tribunes "are put to silence", dropped by Casca as an afterthought though it is the scene's hardest evidence of tyranny. The effect is that the conspiracy will act on interpretation rather than proof — which is exactly what Antony exploits in Act 3 Scene 2 when he turns the same triple refusal into evidence of Caesar's humility.' — Short quotations, the report/fact distinction, a device named and explained, and a connection forward.
:::

::: textnote Spellings and small variants you may meet in your edition
Folger prints "Calphurnia"; most ICSE editions and the MIT/Globe text print "Calpurnia", which this book uses. "mettle" (Folger) appears as 'metal' in older editions in both Brutus's "quick mettle" and Cassius's "honourable mettle" — the words were not yet distinguished, and the pun on workable metal is present either way. "chopped hands" is printed 'chapped hands' in some editions; the meaning (roughened by work) is the same. "glancèd" carries an accent in Folger to show that the ending is pronounced as a separate syllable for the metre; your edition may print "glanced". None of these variants changes the meaning, but in an exam quote whichever spelling your prescribed text uses.

::: revision ONE-MINUTE REVISION — Act 1 Scene 2
**5 must-remember events**
1. Caesar's procession returns angry; Brutus reads the faces — the "angry spot", the "chidden train", Calpurnia pale, Cicero's "ferret and such fiery eyes".
2. Caesar tells Antony he distrusts "lean and hungry" Cassius; Antony calls him "a noble Roman, and well given"; Caesar claims "always I am Caesar" and admits "this ear is deaf".
3. Casca reports that Antony offered Caesar a coronet three times, that Caesar refused it "every time gentler than other", that the crowd cheered the refusals, and that Caesar "swooned and fell down".
4. Casca adds that Cicero "spoke Greek" and that Marullus and Flavius "are put to silence".
5. Brutus and Cassius agree to meet tomorrow; alone, Cassius plans forged letters "in several hands" and vows "we will shake him, or worse days endure".

**5 keywords**
soliloquy · reported action · dramatic irony · illeism · forgery

**3 quotations**
"Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous." (Caesar)
"for always I am Caesar. / Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf" (Caesar)
"Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed" (Cassius)

**3 character insights**
Caesar reads Cassius accurately and acts on comfort rather than truth — a perceptive man, not a blind one.
Casca hides a sharp political eye under a "tardy form" of bluntness; his "to my thinking" is opinion, not proof.
Cassius is sincere and manipulative at once: he believes Caesar must fall and he forges letters to make Brutus believe it too.

**3 themes**
Appearance versus reality (the public Caesar and the private one) · Manipulation and the ethics of means (the letters) · Public opinion and politics as theatre (the clapping, hissing crowd)

**2 literary devices**
Pun — "the falling sickness" (epilepsy / Rome's political fall) · Rhyming couplet — "let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure"

**2 historical points**
Plutarch records Caesar's remark about fat and thin men, the diadem offered at the Lupercalia (twice, not thrice) and the deposition of the two tribunes; the deaf ear is Shakespeare's invention.
The anonymous messages that historically urged Brutus to act become, in Shakespeare, Cassius's personal forgeries.

**3 connections**
Scene 1 → Scene 2: Flavius's "Disrobe the images" leads to the tribunes "put to silence".
Scene 2 → Act 2 Scene 1: "Come home to me" and the forged letters lead to Brutus's orchard and the conspirators at his door.
Scene 2 → Act 3 Scene 2: Casca's cynical account of the triple refusal is retold by Antony as proof that Caesar was not ambitious.

**1 central idea**
Rome judges Caesar by signs and reports it cannot verify — and the conspiracy is built on an interpretation, not a proof.
