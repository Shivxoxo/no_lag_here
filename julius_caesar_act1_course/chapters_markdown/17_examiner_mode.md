# ICSE Examiner Mode: How Marks Are Earned

*This chapter stops teaching you the play for a moment and teaches you the examiner. You will learn the five mental operations an ICSE literature paper tests, what separates a weak answer from an excellent one on eight measurable qualities, how a 3-mark and a 4-mark sub-answer are actually built, how to cure the habit of retelling the story, and how to turn Act 1 knowledge into marks on Act 3–5 questions. Seven important Act 1 questions are answered twice, weakly and excellently, and then dissected. Twelve MCQs and a catalogue of common errors close the chapter. Read it after Chapters 3 to 16; it assumes you know the text.*

## The Honest Starting Point: Where Act 1 Sits in Your Examinations

Before any technique, one fact must be perfectly clear, because a student who misunderstands it will prepare for the wrong examination.

**EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE:** Act 1 of *Julius Caesar* is prescribed for Class IX. Your school examines it, usually through reference-to-the-context or extract questions. The ICSE Class X board paper examines Acts III, IV and V. Act 1 is therefore never set as an extract in the board paper. Yet the board paper leans on Act 1 constantly: the 2025 paper asked candidates what 'the work the ides of March begun' refers to, a question that cannot be answered by a student who does not remember the Soothsayer's warning in Act 1 Scene 2. Every character motive, every piece of foreshadowing and every relationship that Acts III–V exploit was planted in Act 1. So this chapter trains you for two examinations at once: the Class IX paper that sets Act 1 directly, and the Class X paper that assumes it.

::: icse The 2025 paper structure in one glance
The Literature in English paper (Paper 2) carries 80 marks and lasts 2 hours, plus reading time; 20 further marks come from internal assessment. In the 2025 board paper the structure was: Section A, compulsory, Question 1 with 16 one-mark MCQs across drama, prose and poetry; Sections B (Drama), C (Prose) and D (Poetry), each with extract-based questions of 16 marks. Candidates had to attempt one question from each of B, C and D, and one more from any section. Each 2025 drama extract question had five sub-parts marked [3], [3], [3], [3], [4]. The four 3-mark parts typically moved from context and recall through explanation to character; the 4-mark part asked for judgement or personal response supported from the text. Nothing in this chapter predicts what will be set. It describes what has been set and how marks were earned on it.
:::

Keep the shape [3][3][3][3][4] in your head as you read. Almost every piece of advice below is calibrated to it.

## WHAT THE EXAMINER IS TESTING

An examiner marking literature is not asking 'Has this student read the play?' That is assumed. The examiner is asking which mental operation the student can perform on the text, and the sub-questions are deliberately graded so that each one tests a different operation. CISCE describes these skills, in substance, as recall of the text, understanding of meaning, application and interpretation, analysis of character, theme and device, and evaluation or personal response supported by the text. Think of them as five gears. A weak student stays in first gear, retelling, no matter what the question asks. A strong student hears the verb in the question and shifts into the gear it demands.

Below, each level is defined, matched to the question words that signal it, and demonstrated with an Act 1 question and a model answer of realistic exam length.

### Level 1: Recall

Recall is the retrieval of textual fact: who speaks, to whom, where, what has just happened, what is said next. It is tested by the first sub-question of nearly every extract ('Where are the speakers? What has just happened?') and by MCQs. Marks are lost here not through ignorance but through imprecision: the right event in the wrong order, the right line given to the wrong speaker.

**Example question (Recall, 3 marks):** 'According to Casca, who offered Caesar the crown, how many times was it offered, and how did Caesar respond each time?'

**Model answer:** Casca reports that Mark Antony offered Caesar the crown in the marketplace during the Lupercal games. It was offered three times, and Casca adds that it was not a true crown but "one of these coronets". Caesar refused it each time, but Casca notices that he "put it by thrice, every time gentler than other", so the refusals grew weaker. At the third refusal the crowd's shouting and foul breath so overwhelmed Caesar that he "swooned and fell down at it".

Notice what earns the three marks: the offerer (Antony), the number (three), and the manner of refusal (progressively gentler, ending in a swoon), each pinned by a short exact quotation. Nothing is interpreted. Interpretation here would waste time without earning marks.

### Level 2: Understanding

Understanding is the ability to say what the words mean, in your own words, with the metaphors unpacked. It is tested by 'Explain the lines…', 'What does X mean by…?', 'Give the meaning of…'. The commonest failure is to repeat the quotation in slightly rearranged form and call it an explanation.

**Example question (Understanding, 3 marks):** 'Explain in your own words what Cassius means when he tells Brutus that "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings."'

**Model answer:** Cassius is rejecting the idea that Caesar's supremacy is fated. Romans believed the stars at a person's birth shaped his destiny; Cassius says that if he and Brutus are now inferiors ('underlings') under Caesar, the blame lies not with destiny but with their own failure to act. The line is an argument for free will: men become subjects only by allowing themselves to be subjected. It also carries an unspoken accusation, because if the fault is in 'ourselves', then Brutus, by remaining passive, is partly responsible for Caesar's rise.

The three marks are earned by (1) unpacking 'stars' as fate, (2) unpacking 'underlings' and the idea of self-caused inferiority, and (3) identifying the implied point, that inaction is a choice. The answer never wanders into Cassius's motives or into Act 3.

### Level 3: Application and Interpretation

Application asks you to use your understanding to answer a 'why' question that the text does not answer in so many words. 'Why does the speaker say this?', 'Why is X anxious?', 'What is the speaker trying to achieve?' You are reasoning from evidence to purpose. The commonest failure is to answer 'why' with 'what': asked why Cassius tells the Tiber story, the weak student retells the Tiber story.

**Example question (Application, 3 marks):** 'Why does Cassius tell Brutus the story of swimming the Tiber with Caesar?'

**Model answer:** Cassius tells the story to destroy the gap between Caesar and ordinary men in Brutus's mind. He has just claimed "I was born free as Caesar; so were you", and the anecdote is his proof: in the flooded Tiber Caesar's strength failed and he cried "Help me, Cassius, or I sink!", so that Cassius had to carry him ashore as Aeneas carried his father from Troy. The purpose is to make Caesar's godlike status look absurd: "this man / Is now become a god, and Cassius is / A wretched creature". By casting himself as the rescuer and Caesar as the rescued, Cassius argues that Rome's hierarchy has been turned upside down and that Brutus should feel the same resentment.

The marks come from naming the purpose (to shrink Caesar), showing the mechanism (the rescue inverts the hierarchy) and tying it to the surrounding argument (born equal, therefore no cause for awe).

### Level 4: Analysis

Analysis is the examination of how something works: how a speech reveals character, how a device creates effect, how a scene builds a theme. Signal words: 'What does this reveal about…?', 'How does Shakespeare…?', 'Comment on…', 'What impression do you form of…?' Analysis is where terminology (irony, imagery, foreshadowing, dramatic irony) becomes useful, provided each term is attached to a quotation and an effect.

**Example question (Analysis, 3 marks):** 'What does Caesar's description of Cassius reveal about Caesar himself?'

**Model answer:** Caesar's reading of Cassius is acute. He notices that Cassius "reads much", is "a great observer" and "looks / Quite through the deeds of men", and that he "loves no plays" and "hears no music", so Caesar correctly identifies a brooding, restless intelligence that cannot be "at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". Yet the speech reveals Caesar's fatal self-image more than it reveals Cassius. He insists "I fear him not" and claims to speak only of "what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar", as though the public name had replaced the private man. The irony is sharpened by his next words: "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf". The man who sees through others is physically unable to hear a warning, a bodily emblem of the political deafness that will kill him.

Three marks: Caesar's perceptiveness (with evidence), his vanity and third-person self-image (with evidence), and the dramatic irony of the deaf ear. Each point is a quotation plus an inference, never a quotation alone.

### Level 5: Evaluation and Personal Response

Evaluation asks for judgement: which argument is most persuasive, whether a character deserves sympathy, how far you agree with a statement, what you would have done. It is the usual territory of the 4-mark final sub-part. The examiner is not looking for a particular opinion. The examiner is looking for an opinion that is stated clearly, supported from the text, and aware of the other side.

**Example question (Evaluation, 4 marks):** 'By the end of Act 1 Scene 2, do you think Cassius has already won Brutus over? Give reasons based on the text.'

**Model answer:** I think Cassius has opened a door without yet walking through it. Brutus's own words show how far he has moved: he admits he fears the people "Choose Caesar for their king", he says that for "the general good" he will "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other", and he ends by declaring that he "had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions". These are the words of a man already arguing himself towards action. But Brutus also refuses to be hurried: "What you have said / I will consider", and he still says of Caesar "I love him well". Cassius himself measures the result modestly: his "weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire". In his soliloquy he then plans the forged letters, which he would not need if Brutus were already won. So the scene shows a Brutus persuaded in principle but not yet committed, which is exactly why Cassius turns from argument to deception.

Four marks: a clear judgement, evidence for movement (two or three quotations), evidence for resistance (two quotations), and a resolution that uses the soliloquy as a test of the judgement. The conclusion is not 'both sides have a point'; it is a precise position ('persuaded in principle, not committed') that the evidence has earned.

::: insight What an examiner actually reads in the first two lines of an answer
Examiners mark quickly and in volume, and the first two lines of a sub-answer set an expectation that the rest of the answer either confirms or struggles against. In those two lines the examiner is reading for three signals. First, the verb of the question answered back: if the question asked why, the first line should contain a reason, not a narration ('Cassius tells the story in order to…', not 'Cassius and Caesar once went swimming…'). Second, a textual anchor: a name, a precise moment, or a short quotation that proves the student knows where in the play this is ('after the Soothsayer's warning', 'in his soliloquy once Brutus has left'). Third, the absence of stalling: no 'In this extract the speaker is speaking about…', no repetition of the question, no 'Shakespeare was a great writer who…'. An answer that opens with a reason, an anchor and no stalling has, in the examiner's mind, already earned its first mark, and the examiner reads the rest looking for the remaining two. An answer that opens with a story summary has planted the suspicion that the student does not know the difference between retelling and answering, and the examiner reads the rest looking for evidence that the suspicion is wrong. Both answers may end up containing the same points. They will not receive the same marks, because the second one made the examiner hunt for them.
:::

## WHAT MAKES AN ANSWER WEAK / AVERAGE / STRONG / EXCELLENT

Examiners do not mark by instinct alone. Whether or not they use a printed grid, they are judging an answer on a small number of qualities, and each quality can be present at four strengths. The table below names eight of those qualities and describes what each looks like at each strength. Use it diagnostically: after writing a practice answer, find the row for each quality and ask honestly which column you are in.

| Quality | Weak | Average | Strong | Excellent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Textual precision** | Vague or wrong: 'Antony gives Caesar a crown'; speakers confused; events misordered | Correct in outline, but approximate ('Cassius says something about a Colossus') | Exact: right speaker, right moment, short accurate quotation | Exact and selective: the quotation chosen is the one word or phrase that proves the point ("coronets", "put it by thrice") |
| **Context** | None, or the whole plot retold from Scene 1 | Where and when stated, but loosely ('in Rome, during a festival') | Precise placement: 'just after Caesar's train has left the stage, Casca pulled by the cloak' | Placement plus relevance: the context is used ('because this is reported, not shown, we see it through Casca's cynicism') |
| **Analysis** | Absent: the answer describes what happens | A single general observation ('this shows Cassius is clever') | Each point moves from evidence to inference ('the rescue inverts the hierarchy, so…') | Inference with awareness of alternatives ('one reading is…, though Cassius's own bitterness colours the account') |
| **Evidence** | No quotation, or a long block copied from the extract | One quotation, often too long and not explained | Short quotations woven into sentences, each followed by a comment | Quotations chosen for a key word and the key word itself discussed ('fain' tells us Casca is guessing Caesar's desire) |
| **Terminology** | None, or wrong ('simile' for a metaphor) | Terms named but not used ('there is irony here') | Term, quotation and effect linked ('dramatic irony: we know what Caesar does not') | Terms used only where they sharpen the point; never decoration |
| **Cause and effect** | Events listed in sequence with 'then… then…' | One cause noted ('Brutus is worried so he is distant') | Chains traced: 'because X, Y; and Y makes Z possible' | Chains traced and weighed: the main cause distinguished from contributing ones |
| **Character motivation** | Labels: 'Cassius is jealous', 'Brutus is good' | Label plus one reason | Motive derived from the character's own words and actions | Motive shown as mixed or contested, with the text left open where it is open ('envy and principle are both present in the soliloquy') |
| **Significance** | Not addressed, or 'this is very important' | 'This foreshadows what happens later' without saying what | Specific link to a later moment or a theme ('the deaf ear anticipates his refusal of the Soothsayer and Calpurnia') | Significance that explains why Shakespeare placed it here, in this form, through this speaker |

Two patterns in this table deserve attention. First, the move from Weak to Average is usually about accuracy; the move from Average to Strong is about linking evidence to inference; the move from Strong to Excellent is about selectivity and judgement. Most students who have 'done the notes' sit in the Average column on every row. Second, the Excellent column never requires more writing than the Strong column. It requires better choices. The excellent answer is often shorter than the strong one.

## THE ANATOMY OF A 3-MARK SUB-ANSWER

A 3-mark sub-answer is the basic unit of the ICSE literature paper; four of them make up three-quarters of an extract question. Build it to a fixed design and you will never again stare at a question wondering how much to write.

### Structure

Think of three marks as three distinct, markable points, each of which must be visible to a tired examiner at reading speed. The cleanest design is:

1. **Direct answer in the first sentence.** The question's verb answered immediately: the reason, the meaning, the speaker and moment.
2. **Development with evidence.** The second and third sentences each add a point, and each point is pinned to a short quotation or an exact textual detail.
3. **A closing clause of significance or effect**, only where the question invites it ('What does this reveal…?' invites it; 'Who is being addressed?' does not).

### Length

Five to seven sentences, roughly 70 to 110 words. If you are writing a second paragraph on a 3-mark question, you are either repeating yourself or answering a question that was not asked. If you have written two sentences, you have probably made one point and are hoping it counts for three.

### How many points

Three. Not one point explained three ways, which examiners spot instantly, and not five points, which crowds out the explanation each one needs. If the question has two clear parts ('What does Brutus fear, and how does Cassius respond?'), split the marks accordingly: usually two points for the larger part and one for the smaller.

### When to quote

Quote when the exact word matters, and always quote short: a phrase, half a line, at most a line and a half. For a recall question, quotation is confirmation ('Casca calls it "one of these coronets"'). For an understanding question, quote the phrase you are unpacking and then put it into your own words; do not paraphrase around the quotation and leave it unexplained. For a 'why' or 'what does this reveal' question, quote the words that carry the evidence for your inference. If you cannot remember the exact words, do not invent them: a precise paraphrase ('Caesar admits that his ear is deaf') earns the point; an invented quotation can lose it, because the examiner knows the text.

### A worked 3-mark answer, annotated

Question: 'Why does Brutus say he is "not gamesome"? What does his reply reveal about his state of mind?'

Answer: Brutus declines Cassius's invitation to watch the race because he lacks the appetite for sport, admitting "I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony" [point 1: the direct reason, with the exact words]. The reply reveals a man turned inward: he has, by his own confession, been "with himself at war", troubled by "passions of some difference", so that he has neglected his friends [point 2: state of mind derived from his own words]. The contrast with Antony, the man of "quick spirit" who runs in the games, quietly sets up the opposition between the reflective Brutus and the physical, pleasure-loving Antony that the whole play will exploit [point 3: significance, kept to one sentence].

Three points, three pieces of evidence, six sentences. That is the shape.

## THE ANATOMY OF A 4-MARK EVALUATIVE SUB-ANSWER

The final sub-part of a drama extract in the 2025 paper carried 4 marks and asked for judgement: which argument had the greatest impact, whether a character is justified, how far a statement is true, what the extract reveals about a relationship. It is marked differently from a 3-mark part, and students who write it as a longer recall answer lose marks they could easily have had.

### What the extra mark is for

The fourth mark is for judgement. Three marks can be earned by making well-supported points; the fourth is earned by committing to a position and defending it against the obvious objection. An answer that says 'both Cassius and Brutus have good reasons' and stops has refused to evaluate. An answer that says 'Cassius's strongest argument is the appeal to Brutus's ancestor, because it attacks Brutus's honour rather than Caesar's weakness; the Tiber story is vivid but it reveals Cassius's envy more than Caesar's unfitness' has evaluated.

### Structure

1. **Position, stated in the first sentence.** 'The argument with the greatest impact is…'; 'Caesar is not justified in dismissing the Soothsayer because…'; 'I agree only partly, because…'.
2. **Two supporting points, each with evidence.** These are the same evidence-plus-inference moves as in a 3-mark answer.
3. **The counter-consideration.** One sentence acknowledging the strongest fact against your view, and one sentence showing why your view survives it.
4. **A closing sentence that sharpens the position**, not merely repeats it.

### Length

Eight to ten sentences, roughly 120 to 160 words. A 4-mark answer is not twice a 3-mark answer in length; it is one-third longer and differently shaped.

### How many points

Two main points and one counter-consideration. Three or four thin points are weaker than two developed ones, because evaluation is about depth of reasoning, not breadth of coverage.

### When to quote

Quote to support each main point and, ideally, once for the counter-consideration, since an objection you can support from the text is far more convincing than one you merely mention. Keep every quotation short. The 4-mark answer is the one place where the examiner wants to hear your voice; do not drown it in the text's.

### The two words that save the fourth mark

'Because' and 'although'. 'Because' forces you to give a reason for your judgement. 'Although' forces you to acknowledge the other side. A 4-mark answer that contains both words, each doing real work, has almost always evaluated. An answer that contains neither has almost always described.

## HOW TO STOP WRITING STORY SUMMARIES

The single most common cause of lost marks in literature answers is retelling. It is not laziness. Students retell because retelling feels safe, because it fills the space, and because for years 'tell me what happened' was the question they were asked. The examiner at this level is asking a different question, and the cure is to learn to hear the difference.

### Diagnosing the habit

You are summarising when your sentences begin with a character's name followed by a past- or present-tense action verb ('Cassius then tells Brutus…', 'Caesar comes back and says…') and are joined by 'then', 'after that', 'next'. You are answering when your sentences begin with a reason, a quality, a judgement or a textual word ('Cassius's purpose is…', 'The word "fain" shows…', 'This is ironic because…').

A quick test: delete the question from the top of your answer. If a reader could not reconstruct what was asked from your first two sentences, you have summarised.

### The rewrite exercise

Below is a weak answer to a Scene 1 question, followed by the same material rewritten. Read both slowly, then do the exercise that follows.

**Question (3 marks):** 'Why are Flavius and Marullus angry with the commoners in Act 1 Scene 1?'

**Before (summary):**

'In the beginning of the play, some commoners are walking on the street. Flavius and Marullus are two tribunes. They see the commoners and ask them what their trade is. The carpenter says he is a carpenter. Then the cobbler makes jokes and says he is a mender of bad soles. Flavius and Marullus get angry and ask them why they are not working. The cobbler says they have come to see Caesar. Then Marullus gives a long speech about Pompey and tells them to go home. Flavius tells Marullus to pull down the decorations from Caesar's statues. This shows the tribunes are angry with the commoners.'

This is 113 words, accurate, and worth perhaps one mark. Every sentence narrates. The word 'why' is never answered until the last sentence, and that sentence does not answer it either; it merely restates the question as a conclusion.

**After (answer):**

'The tribunes are angry for two connected reasons. The first is political: the commoners are celebrating Caesar's triumph over Pompey's sons, a victory over fellow Romans, and Marullus is disgusted that the very people who once climbed "to chimney tops" to see "great Pompey pass the streets of Rome" now "strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood". Their rejoicing is, to him, ingratitude and a betrayal of Pompey's memory. The second reason is fear: Flavius sees Caesar's popularity as "growing feathers" that must be plucked before he can "soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness". The commoners' holiday is dangerous because it feeds the power of a man the tribunes believe threatens the Republic. Their anger at the idle workmen is really anger at Rome's forgetfulness and alarm at Caesar's rise.'

This is 143 words and worth three marks. Compare the openings: 'In the beginning of the play, some commoners are walking' against 'The tribunes are angry for two connected reasons'. Compare the use of the text: the first version mentions Pompey; the second quotes the exact contrast Marullus builds between past devotion and present fickleness. Compare the endings: the first restates the question; the second names what the anger is really about.

### Your turn

Take this question and write a summary answer deliberately, then rewrite it: 'Why does Cassius want to recruit Casca in Act 1 Scene 3?' Your rewrite must open with a reason, must quote Casca's own pledge ("Be factious for redress of all these griefs") or Cassius's description of the men waiting at "Pompey's Porch", and must not contain the word 'then'. When you have finished, check your first two sentences against the insight box above. If an examiner could not tell from them that the question was 'why', start again.

### The three sentence-openers that cure retelling

Train yourself to begin sub-answers with one of these shapes until it becomes reflex:

- **The reason-first opener:** 'X does this because…' / 'The purpose of this speech is…'
- **The quality-first opener:** 'The speech reveals a man who…' / 'What stands out is…'
- **The word-first opener:** 'The key word is "fain", which…' / 'Casca's phrase "mere foolery" tells us…'

Each of these makes narration grammatically awkward. You cannot comfortably follow 'The purpose of this speech is' with a plot summary. The sentence shape does the discipline for you.

## WEAK VS EXCELLENT ANSWERS

Seven questions follow, each of a kind that Act 1 extract papers regularly ask and that Act 3–5 questions silently depend on. Each is answered twice. The weak answer is not a bad student's answer; it is the answer a reasonably prepared student writes under time pressure when technique fails. The excellent answer is what the same knowledge looks like when it is organised to earn marks. The dissection after each pair uses six fixed labels so that you can see exactly which move earns which mark. Treat all seven as 3-mark answers unless marked otherwise.

### Question 1: Why do the tribunes drive the commoners home?

::: weak Weak answer
Flavius and Marullus are tribunes who do not like Caesar. At the start of the play they find the commoners in the street on a working day and scold them. The cobbler makes jokes and says he is a surgeon to old shoes. The tribunes are angry because the people have come to see Caesar's triumph. Marullus reminds them about Pompey and says they are ungrateful. Then they send them home and go to remove decorations from the statues because they are jealous of Caesar's power.
:::

::: strong Excellent answer
The tribunes drive the commoners home because the crowd's celebration is both a betrayal and a danger. It is a betrayal because the triumph honours a victory "over Pompey's blood", and Marullus is appalled that people who once waited "The livelong day, with patient expectation, / To see great Pompey pass" now "cull out a holiday" for the man who destroyed Pompey's cause; his insult "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!" treats their fickleness as a failure of memory and feeling. It is a danger because, as Flavius makes explicit after they leave, popular adoration is the material of tyranny: Caesar's "growing feathers" must be "plucked" or he will "soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness". Sending the crowd home and stripping the images are therefore the same act, an attempt to deny Caesar the public worship that the tribunes, as guardians of the people's liberty, believe will end the Republic.
:::

**DISSECTION**

**Textual evidence:** The weak answer has one accurate detail ("surgeon to old shoes") that is irrelevant to the question, and no quotation that bears on motive. The excellent answer selects three: the Pompey contrast, the insult, and the feathers metaphor, each of which is a reason rather than an event.

**Keyword:** 'Ingratitude' and 'liberty' are the two concepts the examiner expects. The weak answer gestures at the first ('ungrateful') and replaces the second with 'jealous', which is a misreading of the tribunes' office. The excellent answer names betrayal and danger and explains both.

**Analysis:** The weak answer narrates in sequence; its only analytical sentence attributes jealousy, for which the text offers no support. The excellent answer reads the feathers image as a political theory of how tyranny grows, which is what the image is.

**Explanation:** The excellent answer explains why removing decorations and dispersing a crowd are the same action, a connection the weak answer never makes because it treats them as consecutive events.

**Significance:** The excellent answer's closing phrase, 'guardians of the people's liberty', tells the examiner the student knows what a tribune is for. That is the difference between a student who knows the plot and one who understands the scene.

**Connection:** Casca's later report that Marullus and Flavius "are put to silence" completes this scene's argument: the men who tried to pluck Caesar's feathers are themselves plucked. The excellent answer leaves room for that connection in a 4-mark version; the weak answer has no framework into which it could fit.

### Question 2: How does Cassius persuade Brutus?

::: weak Weak answer
Cassius is jealous of Caesar and wants Brutus to join the conspiracy. He tells Brutus that he has noticed he has been distant. Then he says he will be a mirror to Brutus. He tells the story of how Caesar nearly drowned in the Tiber and how he had a fever in Spain and shook like a girl. He says Caesar is like a Colossus and that Brutus's name is as good as Caesar's. He also mentions Brutus's ancestor who drove out the kings. Brutus says he will think about it. So Cassius uses many arguments to persuade Brutus.
:::

::: strong Excellent answer
Cassius persuades Brutus by working on his self-image rather than on his politics. He begins by offering himself as a mirror, "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of", so that whatever follows will seem to Brutus like self-discovery rather than persuasion. Having heard Brutus declare that he loves "The name of honour more than I fear death", Cassius announces "honour is the subject of my story" and tailors every argument to it. The Tiber and the fever anecdotes degrade Caesar into a man who cried "Help me, Cassius, or I sink!" and groaned "As a sick girl", so that bending to him becomes dishonourable. The Colossus image then makes Brutus's passivity shameful: "we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves". Finally he turns to ancestry, reminding Brutus of "a Brutus once" who would have endured "Th' eternal devil" in Rome "As easily as a king". The sequence moves from flattery to disgust to shame to inherited duty, and each step is an appeal to honour, which is why Brutus, who is unmoved by envy, is moved.
:::

**DISSECTION**

**Textual evidence:** Both answers mention the same four episodes. The weak answer mentions them; the excellent answer quotes the operative phrase from each and tells the examiner what the phrase does.

**Keyword:** 'Honour' is the keyword, and the excellent answer shows it is Cassius's keyword too ("honour is the subject of my story"). The weak answer's keyword is 'jealous', which describes Cassius's motive and leaves the question (how does he persuade?) unanswered.

**Analysis:** The weak answer's final sentence ('uses many arguments') is a list disguised as a conclusion. The excellent answer identifies a method (flattery, then disgust, then shame, then duty) and a reason the method works (every step targets honour).

**Explanation:** The excellent answer explains the mirror image as a technique for disguising persuasion as self-knowledge. The weak answer reports that Cassius 'will be a mirror' without saying why that matters.

**Significance:** The excellent answer's last clause, that Brutus is unmoved by envy but moved by honour, prepares the student for the soliloquy, where Cassius admits that Brutus's "honourable mettle may be wrought". Persuasion and manipulation are shown as the same process seen from two sides.

**Connection:** In Act 3 Brutus will justify the killing to the people on exactly these terms, as a matter of honour and the general good. A student who has written the excellent answer already understands why Brutus's funeral speech sounds the way it does.

### Question 3: What does Caesar's speech about Cassius reveal?

::: weak Weak answer
Caesar tells Antony that he wants fat men around him who sleep at night. He says Cassius has a lean and hungry look and thinks too much. Antony says Cassius is not dangerous. Caesar says he is not afraid of Cassius but if he were afraid of anyone it would be Cassius, because he reads a lot and does not like plays or music and rarely smiles. Then he tells Antony to come to his right side because his left ear is deaf. This shows Caesar is a good judge of character but also proud.
:::

::: strong Excellent answer
The speech reveals a Caesar who sees others clearly and himself not at all. His portrait of Cassius is exact: a man who "reads much", who "looks / Quite through the deeds of men", who "loves no plays" and "hears no music", and who "Seldom he smiles, and smiles in such a sort / As if he mocked himself". Caesar understands the psychology of envy perfectly when he says such men "be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". But every insight is cancelled by his need to be seen as fearless: "I fear him not"; "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar". The third-person self-naming shows that Caesar has begun to believe in 'Caesar' as a public institution immune to private danger. Shakespeare then undercuts the whole speech with one physical detail, "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf", so that the great reader of men ends by admitting he cannot hear on one side. The deafness is not in the historical record; it is Shakespeare's emblem for a ruler whose perceptiveness is defeated by his vanity.
:::

**DISSECTION**

**Textual evidence:** The weak answer paraphrases the whole speech evenly, giving equal weight to every detail. The excellent answer quotes selectively and places the heaviest weight on the two lines that matter most for the question, "for always I am Caesar" and the deaf ear.

**Keyword:** 'Irony' and 'self-image' (or 'hubris', used carefully) are the terms the examiner expects. The weak answer offers 'proud', which is correct but unexplained.

**Analysis:** The weak answer's last sentence contains the right two ideas (good judge, proud) but does not relate them. The excellent answer's opening sentence relates them at once: sees others, not himself. That one relation is the analysis.

**Explanation:** The excellent answer explains why the third-person 'Caesar' is significant (a man becoming an institution), and what the deaf ear does dramatically (undercuts the speech). The weak answer records the deaf ear as a stage instruction.

**Significance:** The excellent answer notes that the deafness is Shakespeare's invention, labelled as such, which signals to an examiner that the student can separate the play from history. That is a mark of genuine understanding, not decoration.

**Connection:** The same pattern, acute perception defeated by self-regard, governs Caesar's refusal of Calpurnia's warnings in Act 2 and his speech to the Senate in Act 3. The excellent answer has defined the pattern; the weak answer has only listed the symptoms.

### Question 4: What is the significance of the Soothsayer?

::: weak Weak answer
The Soothsayer is a fortune teller who appears in Act 1 Scene 2. When Caesar is going to the games, the Soothsayer calls out to him and warns him to beware the ides of March. Caesar asks who it is and Brutus repeats the warning. Caesar calls the Soothsayer before him and the Soothsayer repeats the warning. Caesar says he is a dreamer and ignores him. This is significant because Caesar is killed on the ides of March, so the Soothsayer was right. It shows the theme of fate.
:::

::: strong Excellent answer
The Soothsayer is significant for what he says, how he is answered, and where he is placed. His warning, "Beware the ides of March", is the play's first naming of the date on which Caesar will die, so from this moment the audience watches every scene against a deadline that the characters do not know they are living under; this is the source of the play's dramatic irony. His voice is unmistakable even in a crowd, "a tongue shriller than all the music", which suggests a truth that cuts through ceremony. Caesar's response is the second significance: he gives the man a full hearing, "Set him before me. Let me see his face", and then dismisses him, "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." The dismissal shows that Caesar's failure is not deafness to warnings but refusal of them, a choice rather than an accident. Finally, the placement matters: the warning interrupts the Lupercal procession, so that Caesar's hope for an heir, voiced a moment earlier in his instruction to Antony to touch Calpurnia, is immediately shadowed by a prophecy of his death. The text leaves open whether the Soothsayer speaks for fate or merely for shrewd observation of Rome's mood; Shakespeare gives him no explanation, and that silence is part of his power.
:::

**DISSECTION**

**Textual evidence:** The weak answer quotes nothing; the excellent answer quotes the warning, the "shriller" line and both halves of Caesar's response, each quotation attached to a point.

**Keyword:** 'Dramatic irony' and 'foreshadowing' are expected, and the excellent answer uses the first exactly: the audience knows what the characters do not. The weak answer offers 'the theme of fate' without showing how the Soothsayer creates it.

**Analysis:** The weak answer's analysis is 'the Soothsayer was right'. The excellent answer analyses the dismissal as a choice, which converts Caesar from victim of fate to agent of his own fall, the central question of the play.

**Explanation:** The excellent answer explains why the placement next to the fertility ritual matters (hope for heirs against prophecy of death). This is Level 3 thinking, 'why did Shakespeare put it here?', and it is what separates Strong from Excellent.

**Significance:** The excellent answer closes by refusing to settle what the text leaves unsettled (fate or observation), and says so. Examiners reward this precision; it is the opposite of vagueness.

**Connection:** The 2025 board paper's question about 'the work the ides of March begun' is answerable only by a student who can place this warning and its fulfilment. The excellent answer has already done the placing.

### Question 5: How does Casca describe the crown offering, and what does it reveal about Caesar and the crowd?

::: weak Weak answer
Casca tells Brutus and Cassius what happened at the games. Antony offered Caesar a crown three times and Caesar refused it three times. The people shouted every time he refused. Then Caesar fainted because of the bad smell of the crowd and fell down foaming at the mouth. When he woke up he said sorry to the people. Casca also says Cicero spoke Greek and that Marullus and Flavius have been punished. This shows that Caesar wanted the crown and the people are foolish.
:::

::: strong Excellent answer
Casca describes the offering in deliberately contemptuous prose, calling it "mere foolery" and insisting the crown was really "one of these coronets". The description is slanted at every point, and that slant is the evidence. Of Caesar he says that although he "put it by thrice", he did so "every time gentler than other", that "to my thinking, he would fain have had it" and was "very loath to lay his fingers off it". The repeated 'to my thinking' tells us this is Casca's interpretation of Caesar's desire, not a fact, but the interpretation reveals a Caesar performing reluctance before a crowd whose approval he needs, and whose disapproval literally fells him, since he "swooned and fell down" when the "rabblement hooted". Of the crowd, Casca is merciless: they "clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps", and they praised and hissed Caesar "as they use to do the players in the theatre". That simile is the key: Rome's politics has become a performance, Caesar an actor who even "offered them his throat to cut" for effect, and the people an audience whose love is as cheap as their applause. The report thus reveals a Caesar whose power depends on the mob and a mob that can be played.
:::

**DISSECTION**

**Textual evidence:** The weak answer is factually sound but quotes nothing and treats the report as neutral. The excellent answer quotes the words that carry Casca's bias ("fain", "loath", "to my thinking") and the words that carry his contempt ("rabblement", "sweaty nightcaps").

**Keyword:** 'Reported, not staged' and 'fickle' are the two keywords. The excellent answer builds its whole argument on the first (the slant is the evidence) and earns the second through the theatre simile rather than asserting it.

**Analysis:** The weak answer's conclusion ('Caesar wanted the crown') states as fact what Casca offers as guess. The excellent answer separates Casca's inference from the event, which is exactly the discipline the examiner is testing.

**Explanation:** The excellent answer explains the theatre simile, the most quotable line in the report, as a diagnosis of Roman politics. The weak answer omits it.

**Significance:** Caesar's dependence on the crowd, and the crowd's dependence on performance, are the two facts that make Antony's funeral oration in Act 3 possible. The excellent answer has stated both.

**Connection:** The weak answer wastes a sentence on Cicero's Greek and the tribunes, which are not part of the question. Relevance is itself a marked quality: an examiner reads an irrelevant sentence as a sign the student is unsure what was asked.

### Question 6: Why is the storm in Scene 3 significant?

::: weak Weak answer
In Act 1 Scene 3 there is a big storm with thunder and lightning. Casca is very frightened and tells Cicero about the strange things he has seen, like a slave whose hand was on fire, a lion near the Capitol, women who saw men on fire and an owl in the daytime. Cicero is calm and goes home. Then Cassius comes and says he is not afraid of the storm. He uses the storm to convince Casca to join the conspiracy. The storm is significant because it shows something bad is going to happen.
:::

::: strong Excellent answer
The storm is significant because Shakespeare uses it as a test of how each man reads the world, and the readings predict the men. Casca reads it as divine anger: "Either there is a civil strife in heaven, / Or else the world, too saucy with the gods, / Incenses them to send destruction", and his catalogue of prodigies, the slave's burning hand "Not sensible of fire", the lion that "went surly by", the owl "Even at noonday", is the frightened piety of a man who believes the gods still speak. Cicero reads it sceptically: "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves", a warning that omens mean whatever the interpreter wants, which is precisely what Cassius then proves. Cassius calls the storm "A very pleasing night to honest men" and construes it as "instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state", then makes the tempest an image of Caesar himself, "a man / Most like this dreadful night". The storm is thus significant on three levels: as atmosphere it makes the conspiracy feel like a convulsion of nature; as foreshadowing it anticipates the violence Cassius names when he calls the night's "complexion" "Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible"; and as a study of interpretation it dramatises the theme that signs are made, not found. Whether the heavens truly warn Rome is left open; Cicero's sentence is the play's own caution.
:::

**DISSECTION**

**Textual evidence:** The weak answer lists the prodigies accurately but cannot use them. The excellent answer quotes one phrase from each reader of the storm, so the structure of the answer is the structure of the scene.

**Keyword:** 'Pathetic fallacy' (nature mirroring human disorder) and 'omen' are expected; 'interpretation' is the keyword the best students add. The excellent answer earns all three without listing them as labels.

**Analysis:** The weak answer's analysis is 'something bad is going to happen', which is foreshadowing at its vaguest. The excellent answer analyses the storm as a device for contrasting three minds.

**Explanation:** The excellent answer explains Cicero's line and then shows Cassius illustrating it, which converts a quotation into an argument. The weak answer notes only that Cicero 'is calm'.

**Significance:** The three-level structure (atmosphere, foreshadowing, interpretation) is a reusable template for any question about setting or weather in Shakespeare. The weak answer offers nothing reusable.

**Connection:** Calpurnia's dream and the augurers' findings in Act 2 continue this argument about reading signs, as does the Soothsayer's reappearance on the morning of the Ides. The excellent answer has given the student the thread; the weak answer has given a list of frightening things.

### Question 7: What does Cassius's soliloquy reveal?

::: weak Weak answer
After Brutus leaves, Cassius speaks alone. He says Brutus is noble but can be influenced. He says Caesar does not like him but likes Brutus. He plans to write letters in different handwriting and throw them through Brutus's window pretending they are from citizens, saying how much Rome respects Brutus and hinting at Caesar's ambition. This shows that Cassius is cunning and jealous and will do anything to bring down Caesar.
:::

::: strong Excellent answer
The soliloquy reveals the gap between what Cassius has said to Brutus and what he thinks. He grants Brutus's virtue, "Well, Brutus, thou art noble", but immediately turns it into material: "Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed", where 'wrought' is a metalworker's word, so that Brutus's honour becomes something to be heated and bent. His general maxim, "For who so firm that cannot be seduced?", shows a man who believes no integrity is proof against skilled pressure. Personal grievance surfaces in "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus", and the contrast that follows, "If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me", admits that Cassius would not be swayed as Brutus is being swayed, a confession that he sees Brutus as weaker. The plan for forged letters, "In several hands", "As if they came from several citizens", in which "Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at", reveals a conspiracy that will secure its noblest member by fraud. Yet the soliloquy also contains a principle: the fear that Caesar will "seat him sure" unless Rome acts. The text leaves envy and republican conviction intertwined, and the honest reading is that both are real.
:::

**DISSECTION**

**Textual evidence:** The weak answer paraphrases the plan correctly. The excellent answer quotes the words that reveal attitude rather than action: 'wrought', 'seduced', 'humour', 'as if'.

**Keyword:** 'Manipulation' and 'soliloquy as truth' are expected, and the excellent answer uses the convention of the soliloquy (a character alone speaks his mind) as its organising idea: the gap between said and thought.

**Analysis:** The weak answer's analysis is the label 'cunning and jealous'. The excellent answer analyses the metalworking metaphor and the hypothetical reversal, each of which yields a specific insight about how Cassius sees Brutus.

**Explanation:** The excellent answer explains why forged letters are needed (argument has produced only "thus much show of fire") and what they cost (the conspiracy's honour). The weak answer records the letters as a plan.

**Significance:** The excellent answer refuses to reduce Cassius to envy and says so explicitly, while giving envy its full weight. Examiners reward the writer who can hold two motives in view; they do not reward the writer who picks the simpler one.

**Connection:** When Brutus accuses Cassius of corruption in their quarrel in Act 4, the audience remembers that the conspiracy was founded on a forgery. A student who has written the excellent answer can bring this soliloquy to an Act 4 question and earn marks with it.

## HOW ACT 1 KNOWLEDGE EARNS MARKS IN ACT 3–5 QUESTIONS

This section is the honest answer to a question students ask constantly: 'If the board paper only sets Acts III–V, why am I studying Act 1 so carefully?' The answer is that an extract from Act 3, 4 or 5 is a surface; the marks lie underneath it, in motives, relationships and promises that Act 1 established. The five worked examples below each take a kind of question the board paper could set from Acts III–V and show precisely where an Act 1 fact or quotation converts an average answer into a strong one. In each case the Act 3–5 material is paraphrased, not quoted, so that you are not tempted to learn later-act lines from this book rather than from your text.

### Worked example 1: A Brutus funeral-speech extract

**The kind of sub-question:** 'What reason does Brutus give the people for killing Caesar? How far is this reason consistent with what you know of him?'

**The Act 1 key:** In Act 3 Scene 2 Brutus tells the crowd, in prose, that he loved Caesar but loved Rome more, and that Caesar died because he was ambitious. He asks the citizens whether they would rather live as slaves under a living Caesar or as free men with Caesar dead, and he stakes the whole speech on his own honour. An average answer explains this reasoning from the extract. A strong answer shows that it is not new: Brutus announced the principle in Act 1 Scene 2 when he told Cassius that for "the general good" he would "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other" and that he loved "The name of honour more than I fear death". He also declared he "had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions". The funeral speech is these two sentences spoken to a crowd. The strong answer therefore earns its consistency mark by proof rather than assertion, and can add the sharper point that the word 'ambitious' comes to Brutus from Cassius, whose forged letters were designed so that "Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at".

### Worked example 2: An Antony funeral-oration extract

**The kind of sub-question:** 'How does Antony use the word honourable in this extract? What does his success reveal about the crowd?'

**The Act 1 key:** Antony's oration turns the conspirators' claim to honour into a weapon by repeating that Brutus is an honourable man until the phrase means its opposite. An average answer identifies the irony. A strong answer brings two Act 1 facts to it. First, Caesar's own estimate of Antony in Act 1: Cassius "loves no plays, / As thou dost, Antony", so Caesar saw Antony as a lover of pleasure, and Antony's own earlier words were those of a loyal subordinate, "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed". The orator of Act 3 is therefore a man everyone, including Cassius, has underestimated, and the student can say so. Second, the crowd Antony turns is the crowd Shakespeare characterised in Act 1: the commoners who switched from Pompey to Caesar so completely that Marullus called them "blocks" and "stones", and the mob Casca saw clap and hiss Caesar "as they use to do the players in the theatre". Antony's success reveals nothing new about the crowd; it confirms the diagnosis Act 1 made. That sentence, properly supported, is worth a mark in itself.

### Worked example 3: The 'ides of March begun' question

**The kind of sub-question:** The 2025 paper asked what 'the work the ides of March begun' refers to. The words are Cassius's, spoken to Brutus at Philippi in Act 5 before the battle, as they contemplate that this day may finish what the assassination started.

**The Act 1 key:** The answer is the assassination of Caesar on 15 March, and a mark is earned simply by knowing that. But the fuller answer shows where the date entered the play: the Soothsayer's "Beware the ides of March" in Act 1 Scene 2, repeated when Caesar demanded "Speak once again", and dismissed with "He is a dreamer". The student can then explain the weight of Cassius's phrase: 'the work' began on a day the conspirators were warned about through the man they meant to kill, and it is still unfinished at Philippi, so that the Soothsayer's date has become the measure of the whole tragedy. A question that looks like simple recall becomes, for the prepared student, an opportunity to show understanding of the play's architecture.

### Worked example 4: The quarrel scene in Act 4

**The kind of sub-question:** 'What does this quarrel reveal about the relationship between Brutus and Cassius?'

**The Act 1 key:** In the tent at Sardis Brutus accuses Cassius of taking bribes and of having an itching palm, and Cassius, wounded, protests that Brutus has wronged him; the quarrel exposes Brutus's rigid honour against Cassius's practical corruption. An average answer describes this clash. A strong answer explains why it was inevitable, using the soliloquy of Act 1 Scene 2. Cassius said there that Brutus's "honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed", and that "If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me". The alliance was built by a realist bending an idealist. In Act 4 the idealist, now awake, is judging the realist by the standard the realist used to recruit him. The strong answer can add that Cassius's "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus" showed from the start that Cassius needed Brutus more than Brutus needed Cassius, which is why, in the quarrel, it is Cassius who finally yields.

### Worked example 5: Caesar's last speech in the Senate (Act 3 Scene 1)

**The kind of sub-question:** 'What impression of Caesar do you form from his refusal of the conspirators' petition? Is it consistent with the Caesar we have seen earlier?'

**The Act 1 key:** Moments before he is stabbed, Caesar refuses to recall Publius Cimber, comparing himself to the fixed northern star and declaring that he alone of men is unshakeable. An average answer calls this arrogance. A strong answer shows the arrogance has a history. In Act 1 Scene 2 Caesar spoke of himself in the third person, "for always I am Caesar", and claimed to be above fear while admitting a deaf ear in the same breath. He dismissed the Soothsayer as "a dreamer". He told Antony what "is to be feared" rather than what he feared. The Senate speech is the end point of a self-image the student has watched forming since the Lupercal. The strong answer also notes who strikes first: Casca, the man Brutus remembered as "quick mettle when he went to school" and whom Cassius described as swift "in execution / Of any bold or noble enterprise". A student who knows Act 1 can explain why it is Casca's hand that begins the killing; a student who does not can only report it.

::: icse Using Act 1 in a board answer: the rule of proportion
Act 1 material in an Act 3–5 answer is a key, not a second answer. One precise reference, with a short quotation and a sentence showing its relevance, earns the mark. A paragraph on Act 1 in an Act 3 question signals that the student would rather answer a different extract, and examiners read it that way. Bring the Act 1 fact, use it, and return to the extract in front of you.
:::

## MCQ TRAINING

Section A of the 2025 paper carried 16 one-mark MCQs across the three genres, and MCQs on the drama can reach back to earlier acts for context. The twelve questions below are written in the ICSE manner: a short stem, four options, one correct answer, and distractors built from the errors students actually make. Answer all twelve before reading the key. A score below ten means the Common Errors section that follows is urgent reading.

1. Who speaks the first line of the play?
   (a) Marullus (b) Flavius (c) the Cobbler (d) Caesar

2. The Cobbler describes himself as a "surgeon to old shoes". He means that he
   (a) treats injured feet (b) repairs worn footwear (c) sells leather to surgeons (d) mends the tribunes' tempers

3. Marullus reminds the commoners of the days when they waited "To see great Pompey pass the streets of Rome" in order to
   (a) praise Caesar's generosity (b) expose the crowd's fickleness (c) describe Pompey's triumph over Caesar (d) explain the feast of Lupercal

4. Caesar asks Antony to touch Calpurnia during the race because
   (a) she is ill (b) the elders say it cures barrenness (c) Antony has asked to meet her (d) it is a sign of Caesar's authority

5. Caesar's response to the Soothsayer's warning is
   (a) to arrest him (b) to ask Brutus to question him (c) to call him a dreamer and move on (d) to postpone the games

6. When Cassius tells Brutus "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself", he means he will
   (a) show Brutus his own hidden worth (b) protect Brutus from Caesar (c) report Brutus's words to Caesar (d) reflect Caesar's greatness

7. The crown Casca describes was offered by
   (a) Brutus (b) Casca himself (c) Antony (d) the Senate

8. According to Casca, Caesar refused the crown
   (a) once (b) twice (c) three times (d) four times

9. Casca says that Caesar "foamed at mouth and was speechless". Brutus explains this as
   (a) poison (b) the falling sickness (c) exhaustion from the race (d) a trick to win sympathy

10. "It was Greek to me" is said by Casca about
    (a) Cicero's words (b) Antony's speech (c) the Soothsayer's warning (d) Caesar's apology

11. In Act 1 Scene 3, Cassius asks Cinna to place one of the papers
    (a) on Caesar's statue (b) in Antony's house (c) in the praetor's chair (d) at the Capitol

12. Which of the following is NOT one of the prodigies Casca reports to Cicero?
    (a) a slave's hand burning without harm (b) a lion near the Capitol (c) an owl hooting at noon in the marketplace (d) blood raining on the Capitol

### Answer key with one-line justifications

1. **(b) Flavius.** His "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!" opens the play; Marullus speaks second. Students confuse the two because they share one role.
2. **(b) Repairs worn footwear.** The joke is that he 'recovers' (re-covers) shoes "when they are in great danger", a surgical pun on his trade.
3. **(b) Expose the crowd's fickleness.** The memory of their devotion to Pompey is set against their present rejoicing "over Pompey's blood".
4. **(b) The elders say it cures barrenness.** Caesar says "The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse"; the detail reveals his wish for an heir.
5. **(c) Call him a dreamer and move on.** "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." He does look the man in the face first, which option (a) and (b) ignore.
6. **(a) Show Brutus his own hidden worth.** Cassius will "discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of".
7. **(c) Antony.** Casca answers "Why, Antony" when Cassius asks who offered it; students who answer (a) have confused the offerer with the questioner.
8. **(c) Three times.** "he put it by thrice, every time gentler than other". Plutarch records two offers; Shakespeare's Casca says three.
9. **(b) The falling sickness.** Brutus: "'Tis very like; he hath the falling sickness." Cassius's reply turns the phrase into a metaphor for Rome.
10. **(a) Cicero's words.** Casca says Cicero "spoke Greek" and that those who understood "smiled at one another and shook their heads".
11. **(c) In the praetor's chair.** "lay it in the Praetor's chair, / Where Brutus may but find it"; the others go in at Brutus's window and on old Brutus's statue, not Caesar's.
12. **(d) Blood raining on the Capitol.** Casca reports the burning hand, the lion, the women who saw men in fire, and the "bird of night" at noon; raining blood is not among them.

::: icse Reading an MCQ the examiner's way
Distractors are built from predictable confusions: the two tribunes swapped, the questioner for the offerer, Caesar's statue for Brutus's, Plutarch's two offers for Shakespeare's three. Before choosing, ask which confusion each wrong option is testing. If you can name the confusion, you have usually found the answer.
:::

## COMMON ERRORS

Each error below has cost real students real marks. Each is stated, corrected from the text, and followed by the habit that prevents it.

**1. Attributing Casca's report of the crown to Antony or Brutus.** Antony offered the crown; Casca reported the offering; Brutus and Cassius listened and asked questions. Students write 'Antony tells Brutus that Caesar refused the crown' or 'Brutus saw Caesar refuse the crown'. Neither happened. Brutus and Cassius were off-stage in conversation when the offering occurred, which is why Brutus must ask "Tell us the manner of it, gentle Casca". Prevention: whenever you write about the crown, write 'Casca reports' before anything else.

**2. Calling the Cobbler a carpenter.** There are two commoners who speak. The Carpenter answers Flavius's question in one line; the Cobbler makes all the puns, including "a mender of bad soles" and "all that I live by is with the awl". Students who merge them write 'the carpenter jokes about mending souls', which an examiner marks as a textual error. Prevention: the pun on soles belongs to the man who makes soles.

**3. Saying the crown was offered on stage.** The offering happens in the marketplace while Brutus and Cassius talk elsewhere; the audience hears only shouts and flourishes, and learns what happened from Casca afterwards. This is not a technicality. Shakespeare chose to show us the event through Casca's cynical eyes so that we cannot know whether Caesar truly wanted the crown. A student who writes 'we see Caesar refuse the crown' has missed the dramatic method. Prevention: write 'reported, not staged' in the margin of your notes on this scene.

**4. Treating Caesar's deaf ear as history.** "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf" is Shakespeare's addition; no ancient source records it. Students write 'Caesar was historically deaf in one ear', which is an invented fact. Caesar's falling sickness, by contrast, is in Plutarch. Prevention: label the deafness as Shakespeare's dramatic emblem and the epilepsy as recorded, and keep the two separate.

**5. Saying Cicero joins the conspiracy.** In Act 1 Cicero speaks with Casca during the storm and leaves before Cassius arrives; he is never recruited, and in Act 2 the conspirators decide against including him. Historically he was not a conspirator. Students write 'Cicero and Casca plan the conspiracy' because both are in Scene 3. Prevention: remember that Cicero's exit line, "This disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in", removes him from the stage before any plotting begins.

**6. Writing 'jealous' for Cassius without analysis.** 'Cassius is jealous of Caesar' is true and earns nothing on its own. The mark comes from showing how the jealousy appears (the Tiber rescue, the fever, "this god did shake"), what it is mixed with (republican conviction, the fear that Caesar will "seat him sure"), and where the text lets the two motives show at once (the soliloquy). Caesar himself gives the analysis the student should give: such men "be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". Prevention: never write 'jealous' without a 'because' and a quotation following it.

**7. Saying Brutus joins the conspiracy in Act 1.** He does not. He agrees to listen, "What you have said / I will consider", and Cassius's soliloquy and the forged letters exist precisely because Brutus is not yet won. His decision comes in Act 2. Prevention: track Brutus's commitment scene by scene; in Act 1 it is 'moved, not committed'.

**8. Placing the storm on the night of the Lupercal.** Cicero's question "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" fixes Scene 3 on the eve of the Ides, so a month passes silently between Scenes 2 and 3. Students who write 'that same night' miscount the play's time scheme. Prevention: write the date beside each scene when revising.

**9. Confusing the two Cinnas.** The Cinna of Act 1 Scene 3 is a conspirator who carries Cassius's letters. The Cinna torn apart by the mob in Act 3 is a poet who shares the name. Prevention: 'Cinna the conspirator' and 'Cinna the poet' in your notes, always with the label.

**10. Inventing quotations.** Under pressure students produce lines that sound Shakespearean but are not in the play, or splice two lines together. Examiners know the text. A paraphrase introduced with 'Cassius says that…' is safe; an invented quotation in quotation marks is a visible error. Prevention: learn fewer quotations and learn them exactly; Chapter 15 gives you the set.

::: revision The examiner-mode checklist
Before you write any sub-answer: find the verb in the question and name the level it demands (recall, understanding, application, analysis, evaluation). First sentence: answer the verb, with a textual anchor, no stalling. Three-mark answer: three points, three short pieces of evidence, five to seven sentences. Four-mark answer: a position, two supported points, one 'although', one 'because', eight to ten sentences. Never begin with a character's name and an action verb. Never write 'jealous', 'proud', 'important' or 'foreshadows' without the evidence that earns it. In an Act 3–5 answer, use Act 1 as a key, in one precise sentence, and return to the extract. And remember what the examiner reads first: the first two lines.
