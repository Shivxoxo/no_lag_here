# Themes of Act 1 (Part Two): Fate, Free Will, Superstition, Omens, Appearance vs Reality, Friendship, Jealousy, Political Anxiety, Leadership, Individual vs State, Mob Psychology

*What this chapter does: Part One dealt with the themes about ruling and obeying. This chapter deals with the themes about knowing — what a Roman in Act 1 can know about the future, about the heavens, about his friends and about himself, and how badly he can be wrong. Eleven themes are taught in full, each with the same ten labels, and each as a genuine piece of literary analysis rather than a list. In between, the chapter stages the argument that holds the whole play together — fate against free will — by setting four Act 1 voices against one another, and it builds a table for appearance versus reality. It closes by showing you, sentence by sentence, how a theme paragraph is actually written.*

## How to read this chapter

The themes in Part One were mostly about what people *do* to one another: seize power, flatter, betray, persuade. The themes here are mostly about what people *believe* — and the uncomfortable discovery Act 1 keeps making is that belief and action do not line up. The man who says the heavens are warning Rome does nothing; the man who says there is no such thing as fate goes out and makes one. The man who scorns superstition asks his wife to stand in a fertility runner's path. The friend who speaks most warmly of love is composing forged letters within the hour.

So the habit I want you to practise throughout this chapter is a single question: *what is the gap between what this character says he believes and what he does?* That gap is where Shakespeare puts his meaning, and it is where an examiner's best marks are hidden.

Each theme section again uses ten bold labels in a fixed order, following the course's five levels. **Definition**, **Act 1 evidence**, **Important scenes** and **Characters** are Levels 1 and 2. **Quotations** are verified against the text; learn them exactly, with the speaker and scene. **Hidden significance**, **Historical context** and **Literary significance** are Level 3. **Connection to later Acts** is Level 4, and it is written as labelled glances ahead rather than quotation, because later Acts are not this book's text. **ICSE keywords** are Level 5. Where I say LITERARY INTERPRETATION, I am telling you that careful readers disagree; where I say HISTORICAL RECORD, I am telling you a fact from the sources; where I say SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT, I am pointing at the words themselves.

A reminder you have read before and will read again, because it matters: Act 1 is the Class IX part of the syllabus. The Class X board paper sets Acts III, IV and V. None of these themes will be asked about *as Act 1 themes* in that paper. But every one of them is a thread the examiner expects you to be able to pull: an answer on Caesar's ghost, on Cassius's death, on the eagles and ravens before Philippi, on the storm of guilt in Brutus's tent, is an answer about fate and omens that began in Act 1. You are learning the roots so that you can explain the tree.

## 1. Fate

**Definition:** Fate is the idea that what will happen to a person has been fixed in advance by a power outside them — the gods, the stars, destiny — and cannot be changed by anything they choose to do. Keep it distinct from two neighbours. *Fortune* is luck, which turns without reason and can be good or bad. *Providence* is a plan with a purpose, usually a divine one. Fate in *Julius Caesar* is neither comfort nor plan: it is simply the future arriving on schedule, whether or not anyone listens. The exam-useful shape of the theme in Act 1 is this: the future is announced in the very first scene in which Caesar appears, and the whole of the rest of the Act is a set of people choosing not to hear it.

**Act 1 evidence:** The Soothsayer speaks eleven words in Act 1, and five of them twice: "Beware the ides of March." Notice how Shakespeare stages this. Caesar is in the middle of a public ceremony; a single voice cuts through the music; Caesar himself stops the procession and commands the man forward — "Set him before me. Let me see his face." He looks at the face of fate, hears the warning a second time, and pronounces judgement: "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." The warning is not disproved, not argued with, merely classified as unimportant. The second strand is Cassius's famous denial — "Men at some time are masters of their fates" — which we shall examine on its own below. The third strand is the storm of Scene 3. Casca reads it as the gods' message; Cicero says men read into things what suits them; Cassius turns it into a portrait of Caesar. Between these three strands sits a small, easily missed moment. Cassius invites Casca to dinner tomorrow and Casca replies, "Ay, if I be alive, and your mind hold, and your dinner worth the eating." It is a sour joke; it is also the only line in Act 1 in which a Roman admits that tomorrow is not his to promise.

**Important scenes:** The Soothsayer exchange early in Act 1 Scene 2; Cassius's speech about stars and underlings in the same scene; the whole of Act 1 Scene 3, where the storm becomes a disputed text about the future.

**Characters:** The Soothsayer is fate's voice — nameless, without rank, unable to do anything but repeat. Caesar is the man fate addresses and who declines to listen. Cassius is the man who says fate does not exist. Casca is the man who believes it does and is terrified. Cicero is the man who says that whatever is coming, you cannot read it off the sky. Brutus, notably, says nothing about fate in Act 1 at all; his struggle is with himself, not the heavens, and that silence is worth noticing.

**Quotations:**
- "Beware the ides of March." — the Soothsayer to Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2, spoken twice.
- "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." — Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings." — Cassius to Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Ay, if I be alive, and your mind hold, and your dinner worth the eating." — Casca to Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2.

**Hidden significance:** The deepest thing to see is that fate in this play never *does* anything. There is no god on stage, no oracle's riddle fulfilled by trickery. The Soothsayer names a date, and then men — not stars — fill that date with knives. LITERARY INTERPRETATION, but a strong one: Shakespeare constructs fate so that it is indistinguishable from the sum of human choices. If Caesar had listened, if Brutus had refused, if the letters had not been thrown in at the window, the Ides of March would have been a Wednesday. This is why the audience's position is so strange. We know the date is real; Caesar does not. The device is dramatic irony, but it has a moral weight here: we watch a man dismiss the one piece of true information he will be given, and we also watch the man who says there is no fate set about constructing it. A second hidden point: Caesar's word "dreamer" is not a random insult. It accuses the Soothsayer of belonging to the world of sleep and imagination rather than of politics and daylight. The whole play will test whether that division holds — the storm, Calpurnia's dream and Caesar's ghost all come from the side he dismisses.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch and Suetonius both report that a seer, named by Suetonius as Spurinna, warned Caesar that danger would come to him no later than the Ides of March. The Ides was one of the three fixed marker days in every Roman month — the Kalends, the Nones and the Ides — falling on the 15th of March, May, July and October and the 13th of the other months, and it was sacred to Jupiter. So "the ides of March" is not a mystical phrase; to a Roman ear it is simply a calendar date, like saying 'the fifteenth'. That is precisely what makes the warning sinister: fate here speaks in the plainest possible terms, and is still ignored. The Romans took the reading of the future extremely seriously as a public matter — see the history box on augury below — which means Caesar's dismissal would have looked less like rational scepticism to his own people than it does to us.

**Literary significance:** The theme is built on repetition and anticlimax. The warning is repeated so that no one in the audience can miss it, and then brushed aside with a monosyllable, so that the gap between what we know and what Caesar knows opens like a trapdoor. The Soothsayer's lack of a name is itself a device: he is a function, not a person, the way a chorus is in Greek tragedy. His voice is "shriller than all the music" — Caesar's own words — which gives fate a sound: thin, high, cutting through ceremony. Against this Shakespeare sets Cassius's astrology image, "our stars", drawn from the belief that the position of the planets at birth governed a life; Cassius uses the image only in order to reject it. Structurally, the fate theme is a frame: it opens Caesar's first scene and will close his last.

**ICSE keywords:** fate, destiny, foreshadowing, dramatic irony, prophecy, soothsayer, portent, inevitability, hubris, tragic warning, anticlimax, chorus-like figure.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 2, where fate speaks again through Calpurnia's dream and the priests' sacrifice, and Caesar again overrules it. Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 3 and Act 2 Scene 4, where Artemidorus's letter and the Soothsayer's second appearance give Caesar further chances he does not take. Glance ahead to the opening of Act 3 Scene 1, where Caesar taunts the Soothsayer that the Ides have come and is told, in effect, that they have not yet gone — the exact completion of the warning you have just read. Glance ahead to Act 4 Scene 3 and Act 5, where Caesar's ghost and the omens before Philippi turn fate's attention from the victim to the killers, and where Cassius, the man of "our stars", dies on his own birthday.

## 2. Free Will

**Definition:** Free will is the belief that human beings genuinely choose their actions and are therefore responsible for them — that the future is open, and that what a man becomes depends on what he decides. Philosophically it is the opposite pole from fate. In this play it has a political edge: if men are not "underlings" by destiny, then the man who bows to Caesar has *chosen* to bow, and can choose to stop. Free will in Act 1 is not a comfortable idea about freedom. It is a demand: get up and act.

**Act 1 evidence:** The theme is almost entirely Cassius's property in Act 1, and he states it twice, in two different keys. To Brutus, in daylight, the key is reasonable and flattering: men are at some time masters of their fates; the fault is not in our stars but in ourselves. To Casca, in the storm, the key is defiant and physical. Cassius claims he has walked the streets with his clothing open — "thus unbracèd" (unbraced means with the doublet unfastened) — and offered his chest to the lightning. When Casca reports that the Senate means to crown Caesar, Cassius answers that he knows where he will wear his dagger: "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius." He then develops the idea into a hymn to the free will of the suicide: no tower, wall, dungeon or chain "Can be retentive to the strength of spirit" (retentive means able to hold in); life "Never lacks power to dismiss itself." Casca catches the idea at once — "So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity" (a bondman is a slave). Observe, though, that this is the free will of self-destruction before it becomes the free will of rebellion. The first liberty Cassius names is the liberty to die.

**Important scenes:** Cassius's stars speech in Act 1 Scene 2; Cassius's soliloquy at the end of that scene, where his own theory of free will is quietly applied to Brutus; the dagger speech and the handshake with Casca in Act 1 Scene 3.

**Characters:** Cassius is the theorist and the practitioner. Casca is the convert, and his conversion is suspiciously fast. Brutus is the test case: a man whose will Cassius publicly praises as free and privately plans to steer. Caesar, oddly, is also a free-will figure in Act 1: his "Pass" is a man choosing, in full knowledge, to disregard a warning.

**Quotations:**
- "Men at some time are masters of their fates." — Cassius to Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius." — Cassius to Casca, Act 1 Scene 3.
- "Therein, you gods, you make the weak most strong; / Therein, you gods, you tyrants do defeat." — Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3.
- "For who so firm that cannot be seduced?" — Cassius, alone, Act 1 Scene 2.

**Hidden significance:** Read the soliloquy and the stars speech side by side and the theme turns inside out. To Brutus's face, Cassius says the fault is in ourselves, that is, in our wills. Alone, he says that Brutus's "honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed" — that his noble metal can be beaten into a new shape (wrought means worked, as a smith works iron) — and that no one is so firm he cannot be seduced. In other words, the great preacher of free will does not believe that Brutus's will is free at all; he believes it can be engineered. That is the hidden argument of Act 1: free will is proclaimed as a principle and treated as a mechanism. A second layer concerns the gods. Cassius thanks them — "Therein, you gods" — for making the weak strong through suicide. The man who says the fault is not in our stars still addresses the heavens when it suits his rhetoric. A third layer is dramatic irony: when Cassius says his dagger will deliver him from bondage, the audience that knows the story hears a prophecy of Philippi, where the same hand and a dagger will end his life. His theory of free will is true in the one way he did not intend.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: the real Cassius was an Epicurean — a follower of the Greek philosopher Epicurus, whose school taught that the gods, if they existed, took no interest in human affairs, and that omens were superstition. The Brutus of history leaned towards Stoicism, which taught that a wise man accepts what he cannot change and keeps his virtue under any fortune. Shakespeare does not label either man in Act 1, but his Cassius's contempt for fate fits what the sources say about him, and his Brutus's silence on the subject fits a Stoic's indifference to omens. HISTORICAL RECORD also: Roman thought honoured suicide as a free man's last exercise of will against tyranny — Cato the Younger, Brutus's uncle by marriage, had killed himself in 46 BC rather than accept Caesar's pardon, and that death was still fresh when the play's events take place. Cassius's "bondage" speech would have sounded to a Roman like a reference to a very recent example.

**Literary significance:** The theme is carried by imagery of metal and of binding. Brutus's nature is "mettle" (spirit; but the word puns on metal, and the MIT text actually spells it 'metal'), which can be "wrought"; freedom is the absence of "walls of beaten brass" and "strong links of iron". Cassius's dagger speech is built on anaphora — "Nor stony tower, nor walls of beaten brass, / Nor airless dungeon, nor strong links of iron" — a piling-up of impossibilities that makes the single "But" which follows feel like a door opening. Notice, too, the grammar of "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius": the subject and the object are the same man, which is the whole idea of free will compressed into a line. And Shakespeare gives the free-will speeches to the storm, not to the sunshine: the man insisting that men control events is shouting over thunder he did not make.

**ICSE keywords:** free will, agency, autonomy, self-determination, Stoicism, Epicureanism, bondage, tyrannicide, anaphora, dramatic irony, soliloquy, manipulation.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 1, where Brutus's orchard soliloquy is the play's great exercise of a will choosing — and choosing on hypotheses about what Caesar *might* become. Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 1, where the conspirators act as 'masters of their fates' and immediately discover that the act has consequences none of them chose. Glance ahead to Act 5 Scene 1, where Cassius admits that he once held Epicurus's opinion and now partly believes the birds, and where Brutus debates whether a Roman should take his own life — the Stoic and the Epicurean positions you have just met, finally spoken aloud. Glance ahead to Act 5 Scene 3 and Act 5 Scene 5, where both men use the freedom Cassius described in the storm.

## FATE AND FREE WILL: THE PLAY'S CENTRAL ARGUMENT

Most school guides tell you that *Julius Caesar* is 'about' fate versus free will and leave it there, as if naming the two sides were the same as understanding the fight. It is not. The argument is worth a section of its own because Act 1 does something unusual: it gives the argument four voices, lets none of them win, and then shows you what each voice does with its hands.

**Voice one: the Soothsayer.** His position is the simplest. The future exists and can be known; his job is to say it. He does not explain, persuade or plead. He says "Beware the ides of March" twice and is gone. If you take him at his word, the universe has already decided, and the only freedom left to men is whether to listen.

**Voice two: Cicero.** In Scene 3, Casca pours out his catalogue of horrors and Cicero replies with two of the most intelligent lines in the Act: "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves" (construe means interpret; clean from means entirely apart from). Cicero does not deny that the night is strange — he calls it "a strange-disposèd time". He denies that anyone can safely say what it *means*. His is the voice of the sceptic: the sky may be full of signs, but every reading of a sign is made by a man with a motive. Then he asks the only practical question in the scene — "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" — and goes home, because the sky "Is not to walk in". Cicero is the one Roman in Act 1 who separates knowledge from interpretation, and Shakespeare removes him from the stage before the conspiracy forms. That exit is a choice by the dramatist, and you may read it as a judgement: there is no room in this plot for the man who refuses to over-read.

**Voice three: Cassius.** He takes the opposite position to the Soothsayer — "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves" — and yet, watch him in the storm. He tells Casca that heaven "hath infused them with these spirits / To make them instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state" — the prodigies are messages after all, and the message is about Caesar. So which is it? Does the sky mean nothing, or does it mean Caesar? The honest answer is that Cassius believes whichever is useful at the moment. To Brutus, who needs to feel responsible, the stars are empty. To Casca, who is already frightened of the heavens, the heavens are recruiting for the conspiracy. Cassius is exactly the man Cicero was describing: he construes things after his fashion.

**Voice four: Casca.** He is the believer. "It is the part of men to fear and tremble / When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us." For Casca the storm is unambiguous — "Either there is a civil strife in heaven, / Or else the world, too saucy with the gods, / Incenses them to send destruction" (saucy means insolent; incenses means enrages). And here is the thing to notice: the man who most believes the gods are speaking is the man who most quickly agrees to kill. Terror of fate does not make Casca passive; it makes him available. Within a hundred lines of saying men should tremble, he has shaken Cassius's hand.

Now put the four voices in a row. The Soothsayer says the future is fixed and does nothing but speak. Cicero says you cannot read the future and goes to bed. Cassius says there is no fixed future and manufactures one. Casca says the gods are furious and becomes an instrument of other men. Shakespeare does not tell us who is right. What he shows is that the *belief* a man holds about fate predicts almost nothing about what he will do, while the *use* he makes of that belief predicts everything. That is the central argument of the play in miniature, and it is why a good answer never says 'the play shows that fate controls everything' or 'the play shows that men make their own destiny'. The play shows men arguing about fate while they make it.

::: insight Why the man who denies fate is the one who plots
There is a logic to Cassius's position that is easy to miss because it is so familiar to us. If you believe the gods have already written the ending, conspiracy is pointless: whatever you do, the Ides will come or they will not. If you believe the ending is open, then every hour you spend not acting is an hour in which you are choosing Caesar. Denial of fate is therefore not a philosophical ornament on Cassius; it is the fuel of his plot. He has to believe the fault is in ourselves, because otherwise there is nothing for him to do with his dagger except wear it. But the deeper point is this. Cassius's denial of fate does not extend to other people. He treats Brutus as a thing to be "wrought" and Casca as a man who will "set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest" once his pride is touched. Free will, for Cassius, is something he has and others do not. That is not a philosophy; it is the psychology of a manipulator who has found a philosophy that flatters him. And Shakespeare quietly turns the knife: the one man in Act 1 who most insists that he controls his fate is the one the audience knows will die by the dagger he is boasting about. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — but it is the reading that explains why the Act's fate speeches and its plotting are given to the same mouth.
:::

## 3. Superstition

**Definition:** Superstition is belief in supernatural causes and cures that has no rational basis — a belief that an action or object can bring luck, ward off harm or reveal the future. The word is unfair to the Romans, for whom many of these practices were official religion, and you should use it carefully: what one culture calls superstition another calls piety. For exam purposes, treat superstition as the *personal* side of the theme (what an individual does to protect himself from the unseen) and omens as the *public* side (what the unseen is thought to be saying). Act 1 shows both, and shows them in the same man.

**Act 1 evidence:** Caesar's first instructions in the play are superstitious. He tells Calpurnia to stand in Antony's way as he runs the Lupercal course, and tells Antony "Forget not in your speed, Antonius, / To touch Calpurnia, for our elders say / The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse." He wants a childless wife struck by a runner because the old men say it works, and he ends with "Set on and leave no ceremony out." Within fifteen lines he dismisses a soothsayer as a dreamer. That juxtaposition is the whole theme. In Scene 1, Flavius orders the tribunes to strip Caesar's statues and Marullus hesitates — "You know it is the feast of Lupercal" — a flicker of religious scruple that Flavius overrides. In Scene 3, superstition becomes a flood: Casca's hand on his sword, his insistence that these are not natural things, his conviction that men should tremble. Cassius mocks him — "You are dull, Casca" — and then, within the same speech, offers his own supernatural reading. And in the smallest detail of all, Caesar asks Antony to speak into his good ear: "this ear is deaf". A man who trusts ceremonies to cure barrenness cannot hear a warning given on his left.

**Important scenes:** The opening of Act 1 Scene 2 (the Lupercal instructions and the Soothsayer); the exchange about the images at the end of Act 1 Scene 1; the whole of Act 1 Scene 3.

**Characters:** Caesar is the superstitious sceptic — the contradiction is the character. Casca is the frank believer. Cassius is the man who scorns belief and exploits it. Marullus has one line of scruple. Calpurnia is silent in Act 1, a woman whose body is the object of a ritual; her own fears belong to Act 2.

**Quotations:**
- "The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse." — Caesar to Antony, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Set on and leave no ceremony out." — Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." — Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2, a few lines later.
- "It is the part of men to fear and tremble / When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us." — Casca, Act 1 Scene 3.

**Hidden significance:** Caesar's superstition is selective, and the selection tells you about him. He accepts the ritual that promises him an heir — that is, that promises to extend his power into the next generation — and rejects the prophecy that threatens to end it. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: superstition in Caesar is not credulity; it is appetite. He believes what enlarges him. This is why "leave no ceremony out" is a politically loaded line: the ceremonies Caesar insists on are the ones that place him at the centre of Rome's religious life, and the tribunes' 'ceremonies' on his statues are the ones that make him look like a king. The second hidden point is about Casca. His superstition is real, but the sword he has not sheathed since meeting the lion is also real, and so is his hand when he offers it to Cassius. Fear of the gods and readiness to kill live comfortably together in him. The third point: the Lupercal rite is a fertility cure, and Caesar is childless. A line that sounds like local colour is actually telling you that the most powerful man in the world has no son — which is one reason the question of what happens after him is so urgent to everyone in the Act.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: the Lupercalia fell on 15 February and was an ancient purification and fertility festival. Its priests, the Luperci, ran through the city striking bystanders, especially women, with strips of goat-hide, and the touch was believed to promote fertility and ease childbirth. In 44 BC a new college of Luperci, the Juliani, had been created in Caesar's honour, and Antony, who was consul that year, ran as one of its members. So Caesar's instruction is not a private eccentricity; it is a public rite he had just personalised. HISTORICAL RECORD also: Calpurnia had married Caesar in 59 BC and the marriage was childless. For how Roman religion read the heavens as a matter of state, see the box below.

**Literary significance:** The theme is built on juxtaposition: the fertility rite and the death warning sit within a single short passage, so that the audience sees Caesar's two faces in one glance. The vocabulary is religious — "holy chase", "sterile curse", "ceremony" — and Shakespeare lets it rhyme with the political: "ceremonies" on statues, "Caesar's trophies". The deaf ear is a stage image as much as a line; a director can make Caesar physically turn away from the side the warning came from. Casca's speeches are prose in Scene 2 and verse in Scene 3, and the shift matters: the sardonic reporter of the crown becomes the shaken believer of the storm, and verse is the register of a man no longer in control of his tone.

**ICSE keywords:** superstition, ritual, ceremony, piety, scepticism, credulity, juxtaposition, hubris, selective belief, fertility rite, Lupercalia, dramatic irony.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 2, where Caesar sends for the augurers to sacrifice and then rejects what they find, and where he swings from granting Calpurnia's plea to overturning it on Decius's flattering reinterpretation of her dream — the selective superstition of Act 1 played out in full. Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 1, where Cassius proposes they swear an oath and Brutus refuses: the conspiracy itself argues about ceremony. Glance ahead to Act 5 Scene 1, where Cassius, the mocker of Casca, confesses that he has begun to credit the omens of birds.

::: history Roman augury and omens (background)
HISTORICAL RECORD, offered as background so that you can judge how Caesar's dismissal of the Soothsayer would have sounded to Romans. The Roman state took the will of the gods seriously as a matter of public procedure. A college of augurs — Cicero himself was a member — interpreted the auspices: the flight, calls and feeding of birds, and other signs such as lightning. No assembly met and no magistrate took up major business without the auspices being taken, and an unfavourable sign could lawfully halt public action. A second class of specialists, the haruspices, of Etruscan origin, examined the entrails of sacrificed animals; it was in this tradition that the seer Spurinna, who warned Caesar about the Ides, stood. Beyond these were *prodigia* — prodigies, unnatural events such as rains of stones, speaking animals or fire in the sky — which were reported to the Senate, officially recorded and then 'expiated' by sacrifice as warnings that the peace between Rome and its gods had been disturbed. Caesar was himself *pontifex maximus*, Rome's chief priest, from 63 BC until his death. Plutarch's account of the days before the assassination records lights in the sky, birds settling in the Forum, men who seemed to walk about on fire, and a slave whose hand blazed without being burned — the last of which Shakespeare gives directly to Casca. Two cautions. First, educated Romans disagreed about all this: Cicero wrote a treatise, *On Divination*, in which the case against omens is argued as strongly as the case for them. Second, the lion that Casca meets near the Capitol has no clear counterpart in Plutarch's account and is generally regarded as Shakespeare's own addition; do not present it as history.
:::

## 4. Omens

**Definition:** An omen is an event taken as a sign of what is to come — a comet, a strange birth, an animal where it should not be. Shakespeare's own words for it in Act 1 are "prodigies" (unnatural wonders), "portentous things" (things that foretell) and "tokens" (signs). The crucial feature of an omen, as distinct from a prophecy, is that it does not speak. A soothsayer tells you to beware; a burning hand simply burns, and somebody has to decide what it means. This is why the theme of omens is really the theme of *interpretation*, and why it belongs next to appearance versus reality rather than merely next to fate.

**Act 1 evidence:** Scene 3 opens with thunder and lightning and Casca's catalogue, which you should learn as a list of five. A slave's left hand "did flame and burn / Like twenty torches joined" and was unscorched. Near the Capitol he "met a lion, / Who glazed upon me and went surly by" (glazed means glared; surly means sullenly). A hundred terrified women "swore they saw / Men all in fire walk up and down the streets." Yesterday "the bird of night did sit / Even at noonday upon the marketplace, / Hooting and shrieking." And the night itself is "a tempest dropping fire". Casca's reasoning follows: "When these prodigies / Do so conjointly meet, let not men say / 'These are their reasons, they are natural'". Cassius then produces a second list, more abstract — fires, "gliding ghosts", birds and beasts acting against their kind, old men, fools and children who "calculate" (prophesy) — and reads them all as a warning "Unto some monstrous state". Later he says the sky's complexion "In favour's like the work we have in hand, / Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible." Cinna, entering, adds a third witness: "What a fearful night is this! / There's two or three of us have seen strange sights." Three men, three readings, one sky.

**Important scenes:** Act 1 Scene 3 from beginning to end. Nothing before it is an omen in the strict sense; the Soothsayer is a prophecy, and the swoon in the marketplace is a symptom that Cassius chooses to treat like a sign.

**Characters:** Casca is the frightened witness whose evidence is vivid and whose reasoning is simple. Cicero is the sceptic who declines to interpret. Cassius is the interpreter who turns omens into a recruiting speech. Cinna is the ordinary conspirator, scared and useful.

**Quotations:**
- "Against the Capitol I met a lion, / Who glazed upon me and went surly by / Without annoying me." — Casca, Act 1 Scene 3.
- "the bird of night did sit / Even at noonday upon the marketplace, / Hooting and shrieking." — Casca, Act 1 Scene 3.
- "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves." — Cicero, Act 1 Scene 3.
- "In favour's like the work we have in hand, / Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible." — Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3.

**Hidden significance:** Casca's omens are all inversions: fire that does not burn, a wild beast that does not attack, a night bird at noon, men made of flame. Each is a thing behaving against its nature — and that is exactly what Cassius will accuse Rome of a few minutes later: a city whose fathers' minds are dead, governed by "our mothers' spirits". LITERARY INTERPRETATION: the omens are a picture of a state turned upside down, and the question Act 1 refuses to answer is *who* has turned it — Caesar by rising, or the conspirators by what they are about to do. Cassius says the sky is "like the work we have in hand". He means it as encouragement. Hear it as a confession: the bloody, fiery, terrible thing the heavens are warning about may be the conspiracy itself. A second point is about "the lion in the Capitol". Casca met a lion; Cassius turns it into a simile for Caesar, who "roars / As doth the lion in the Capitol". Within fifty lines a thing seen has become a thing meant. That is how omens work in politics: a report becomes an argument.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch, Shakespeare's source through North's translation, lists prodigies before the assassination, including the slave whose hand flamed harmlessly and birds in the Forum; Shakespeare takes these, intensifies them, and adds a lion. The Roman habit of reading unnatural events as warnings to the state is described in the history box above. For how Shakespeare's first audience might have received this scene, read the box below — and notice that Cicero's scepticism had a respectable home in both worlds.

**Literary significance:** The omens are the play's great set piece of imagery, and almost all of it is fire: torches, a tempest "dropping fire", men "all in fire", "fires", a sky "fiery". The storm is pathetic fallacy on a civic scale — the weather expressing not one man's mood but a nation's crisis — and it is also a theatrical necessity, since on a daylight stage the thunder, the breathlessness and the staring eyes are what tell an audience it is night and something is wrong. Casca's verse here is full of sibilants and hard consonants — "scolding winds", "Shakes like a thing unfirm" — and the women are "Transformèd with their fear". Structurally, the scene is a debate about reading, and Shakespeare arranges the readers in order of increasing self-interest: Casca (frightened), Cicero (detached), Cassius (motivated).

**ICSE keywords:** omen, portent, prodigy, pathetic fallacy, foreshadowing, inversion of nature, interpretation, scepticism, fire imagery, atmosphere, foreboding, supernatural.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 1, where the same storm is still raging when Brutus's conspirators arrive and the men dispute, absurdly, where the sun rises. Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 2, where Calpurnia reports a new list of prodigies — a lioness whelping in the streets, graves yielding their dead, warriors fighting in the clouds — and Caesar answers that these signs are for the world in general, not for him. Glance ahead to Act 5 Scene 1, where Cassius reads his own omen in eagles replaced by ravens, crows and kites above his army, and becomes, at the end, the Casca he mocked.

::: history Elizabethan attitudes to portents (background)
HISTORICAL RECORD as background, with the caution that we cannot know what any one spectator in 1599 thought. Shakespeare's audience lived in a culture in which the heavens were widely held to comment on human affairs. Comets and eclipses were recorded as warnings; almanacs predicting the weather, illness and public events were among the most widely sold printed books; and Elizabeth I's court had consulted the astrologer and mathematician John Dee, who is said to have chosen a propitious date for her coronation. The official Church of England discouraged astrology and divination, yet the government's own Homilies — sermons read in parish churches — taught that order in the state mirrored order in creation, so that rebellion was a kind of disorder in nature. Audiences may therefore have felt that a storm over Rome on the eve of a murder was not decoration but doctrine. At the same time, sceptical voices existed: Reginald Scot's *Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584) attacked belief in witches and wonders, and Cicero's own treatise against divination was read in schools. In 1599, with an ageing, unmarried queen and no named heir, a play in which the sky protests the fall of a ruler, and in which the interpretation of signs is itself shown to be political, had an obvious contemporary charge. State it as 'may have', never as 'did'.
:::

## 5. Appearance vs Reality

**Definition:** The theme of appearance versus reality concerns the gap between how things seem and how they are — and, just as importantly, who is able to see across that gap. In most plays the gap is a matter of disguise or lies. In *Julius Caesar* it is subtler: almost nobody in Act 1 lies outright. Instead people perform, interpret, report, and are misread. The theme is therefore inseparable from the play's interest in sight: eyes, mirrors, glasses, reflection, looking "Quite through" a man.

**Act 1 evidence:** Start with Caesar's performance in the marketplace, which we never see. Casca reports that the crown was refused three times, "every time gentler than other", and that to his thinking Caesar "would fain have had it"; he also tells us "'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets". Whether the refusal was real modesty, a test of the crowd or a show, SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT leaves undecided — we have only a hostile witness's reading of a scene that happened offstage. Then Cassius's mirror. Brutus admits that "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things", and Cassius offers to be that reflection: "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of." A mirror that chooses what to show is not a mirror. By the end of the scene, Cassius is planning letters "As if they came from several citizens" — appearance manufactured to produce a reality. Set against all this one man who does see through surfaces: Caesar on Cassius, "he looks / Quite through the deeds of men". Antony, by contrast, sees only the surface — "He is a noble Roman, and well given." And Casca, whom Brutus takes for a blunt fool, is described by Cassius as a man who "puts on this tardy form" (puts on a slow, dull manner): the sourness is a costume.

**Important scenes:** The mirror dialogue in Act 1 Scene 2; Casca's report in the same scene; Caesar's reading of Cassius; Cassius's soliloquy; and, in Act 1 Scene 3, Casca's prediction that Brutus's reputation will make the conspiracy look virtuous.

**Characters:** Cassius is the maker of appearances. Caesar is the one true reader in the Act — and the one who is going to be fooled. Brutus is the man who cannot see himself and is offered a crooked glass. Casca is a man wearing a mask of dullness. Antony is the surface-reader. The crowd sees only what is shown to it.

**Quotations:**
- "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things" — Brutus to Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of." — Cassius to Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "he looks / Quite through the deeds of men" — Caesar on Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness." — Casca on Brutus, Act 1 Scene 3.

**Hidden significance:** The most important hidden point is that the only character who reads others accurately is also the character most wrong about himself. Caesar diagnoses Cassius perfectly — the lean look, the reading, the mirthless smile, the inability to be at ease while a greater man exists — and then adds "for always I am Caesar", as if the name were armour. Reality is available to him and he declines to apply it. A second point: Casca's alchemy image tells you that the conspirators themselves know the difference between their appearance and their reality. They need Brutus not because he will fight better but because his "countenance" (face, and also approval) will turn what "would appear offence in us" into "virtue". That is a confession that the enterprise, seen plainly, looks like a crime. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: Act 1 is a study in how a republic of watchers — "a great observer", "I do observe you now of late" — nevertheless manages to see almost nothing true.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: the real Brutus did receive anonymous messages urging him to act — Plutarch records graffiti and notes asking whether he was asleep and telling him he was no true Brutus. Shakespeare's change is to give them an author. In Plutarch they come from the people; in the play they are forged by Cassius. That single alteration converts a historical fact about public opinion into a theme about manufactured appearance, and it is the strongest piece of evidence that Shakespeare wanted this theme in his Act.

**Literary significance:** The theme's vocabulary is optical: "eyes", "see", "reflection", "mirrors", "glass", "shadow", "looks", "observer". Cassius's mirror speech is an extended metaphor, and it is also a paradox, since a mirror that "modestly" discovers what you do not know is doing the opposite of reflecting. Casca's "alchemy" is a metaphor from the pseudo-science of turning base metal to gold — Brutus's face as the philosopher's stone that turns murder to virtue. The structural device is report: the crown scene reaches us second-hand so that we, like Brutus, must take appearance on trust. And the Act's final line is Cassius's promise to "be sure of him" — the language of certainty closing an Act in which nothing has been certain.

**ICSE keywords:** appearance, reality, perception, illusion, manipulation, extended metaphor, mirror image, paradox, reported action, dramatic irony, deception, self-knowledge.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 1, where Brutus tells the conspirators to hide their purposes behind smiles and 'formal constancy', making concealment an official policy of the honourable men. Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 2, where Decius reinterprets Calpurnia's dream of blood as a vision of Rome's revival — the mirror trick worked on Caesar himself. Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 2, where Antony, the surface-reader of Act 1, turns out to be the play's master of appearances, calling the murderers honourable men until the word means its opposite.

Second, notice that the right-hand column is nearly empty of Brutus. The most honourable man in the Act sees through nothing, and that is a fact about his tragedy, not a flaw in your table.

## 6. Friendship

**Definition:** Friendship, in the Roman sense Shakespeare inherited through Plutarch, was not merely affection; *amicitia* was a public bond of mutual obligation between men of standing, and to call a man friend was to make a claim on him. In Act 1 the word 'friend' and the word 'love' are used almost interchangeably, and almost always by someone who wants something. The theme to track is therefore not whether these men like one another but what the language of friendship is *doing* in each scene.

**Act 1 evidence:** The long dialogue between Brutus and Cassius opens as a quarrel between friends. Cassius complains that he no longer receives from Brutus's eyes "that gentleness / And show of love as I was wont to have" and that Brutus is harsh "Over your friend that loves you." Brutus's answer is one of the Act's most moving passages: his trouble is inward, "poor Brutus, with himself at war, / Forgets the shows of love to other men." He then says the sentence the whole play depends on: "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well." Brutus loves Caesar. He also loves Cassius — he parts with "Till then, my noble friend, chew upon this" — and Cassius, alone, admits the asymmetry at the heart of it: "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus." Caesar's own friendship is on display too: he confides in Antony, asks him to walk on his right side, and receives in return loyalty so fast it is a reflex — "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous." In Scene 3, friendship becomes a handshake in the dark: "Hold. My hand." The Act begins with Cassius mourning a lost "show of love" and ends with him certain of Brutus as if the man were a possession.

**Important scenes:** The opening of the Brutus–Cassius dialogue in Act 1 Scene 2; Caesar and Antony in the same scene; Cassius's soliloquy; the Casca–Cassius pact in Act 1 Scene 3.

**Characters:** Brutus loves both sides and will have to betray one. Cassius uses the word love as a lever and believes himself unloved by Caesar. Caesar trusts Antony and is right to; he does not count Brutus among his dangers, and that silence is terrible. Antony is loyalty without analysis — in Act 1.

**Quotations:**
- "I have not from your eyes that gentleness / And show of love as I was wont to have." — Cassius to Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well." — Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus." — Cassius, alone, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous." — Antony to Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2.

**Hidden significance:** Notice the word "shows". Brutus speaks of "the shows of love"; Cassius of "show of love". Both men assume that love has an outward performance that can be given or withheld, and Cassius's whole method is to treat the performance as the thing itself: if Brutus will *show* love, Cassius can work with it. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: friendship in Act 1 is the medium through which manipulation travels — nobody could be seduced by a stranger. The second point is the one students miss: Caesar's great mistake in Act 1 is not that he trusts the wrong friend but that he studies the wrong enemy. He reads Cassius flawlessly and never looks at Brutus, because Brutus is a friend and friends are not read. "He loves Brutus" is the most dangerous sentence in the Act.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Brutus had fought for Pompey against Caesar and been pardoned after Pharsalus; Caesar then advanced him, making him governor of Cisalpine Gaul and, in 44 BC, urban praetor — the senior praetorship — over Cassius, which ancient sources record as a grievance of Cassius. Brutus's mother Servilia had long been Caesar's mistress. So the friendship between Caesar and Brutus was real, recent and generous, and Cassius's jealousy of it had a specific cause. Shakespeare keeps the emotional shape and drops the details.

**Literary significance:** The theme is carried by direct address — "good Brutus", "gentle Brutus", "dear Brutus", "my noble friend" — a texture of endearments that makes the soliloquy's cold "Well, Brutus, thou art noble" land like a change of temperature. The eye image belongs here as much as to appearance versus reality: a friend is supposed to be the mirror in which you see yourself truly, and Shakespeare makes Cassius claim precisely that role while abusing it. Structurally, friendship frames the Act's two recruitments: the slow, loving one of Brutus and the fast, handshaken one of Casca.

**ICSE keywords:** friendship, love, loyalty, trust, betrayal, intimacy, manipulation, confidant, obligation, asymmetry, pathos, dramatic irony.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 1, where Caesar's friends arrive at Brutus's house to plan his death, and where Brutus insists that they carve Caesar as a dish fit for the gods — love attempting to control the manner of a murder. Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 1 for the moment this theme is built towards: Caesar's last words to Brutus. Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 2, where Antony calls Brutus Caesar's angel, and to Act 4 Scene 3, where Brutus and Cassius quarrel and reconcile in a tent — the Act 1 friendship tested to breaking and mended.

## 7. Jealousy

**Definition:** Jealousy is pain at another's possession of what you believe should be yours — rank, love, reputation. Distinguish it from envy, which wants what another has, and from ambition, which wants to rise; jealousy is specifically the wound of comparison. A warning about the word itself: in Shakespeare's English 'jealous' often means suspicious, and that is what Brutus means by "I am nothing jealous" and Cassius by "be not jealous on me". The theme is present in the Act's emotions long before it is present in its vocabulary.

**Act 1 evidence:** Cassius's two stories about Caesar are not evidence of unfitness to rule; they are the memories of a man who cannot bear a comparison. The Tiber story ends not with Caesar's weakness but with Cassius's status: "this man / Is now become a god, and Cassius is / A wretched creature and must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him." The fever story circles the same wound — "'Tis true, this god did shake" — and ends with Caesar crying for drink "As a sick girl". The name speech is jealousy made musical: "Why should that name be sounded more than yours?" And Caesar, from the other side, diagnoses the condition in a single sentence: "Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". Cassius's own soliloquy supplies the motive in the plainest words: "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus." That is two jealousies in one line — of Caesar's power and of Brutus's place in Caesar's affection.

**Important scenes:** The Tiber and fever narratives and the name speech in Act 1 Scene 2; Caesar's assessment of Cassius in the same scene; Cassius's soliloquy.

**Characters:** Cassius is the Act's embodiment of jealousy and, importantly, never names it in himself; he calls it honour. Caesar is both its object and its best analyst. Brutus is innocent of it — his "nothing jealous" is literally true — and that innocence is what makes him usable.

**Quotations:**
- "And this man / Is now become a god, and Cassius is / A wretched creature and must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him." — Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Why should that name be sounded more than yours?" — Cassius to Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves" — Caesar on Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look." — Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2.

**Hidden significance:** The crucial point is that Cassius's jealousy is *accurate*. Everything he says about Caesar's body may be true; Caesar may well have the falling sickness and may well have cried for water in Spain. Jealousy here is not delusion but a lens that selects the true facts which hurt. That makes it far more dangerous dramatically than a lie, because Brutus can check the facts and find them sound. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: Shakespeare shows that a republic can be destroyed by a man telling the truth for the wrong reason. A second point: Caesar's "lean and hungry look" is a physical description of jealousy — a man eaten thin by comparison — and Caesar's prescription, men "that are fat" and "such as sleep a-nights", is a portrait of the contented. Caesar understands the disease precisely and misjudges only its reach.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch reports that Caesar said he did not fear fat, long-haired men but the pale, thin ones, meaning Brutus and Cassius — Shakespeare moves the remark to apply to Cassius alone, which sharpens the theme and protects Brutus. Plutarch also records the praetorship grievance mentioned above. Ancient writers themselves differed about Cassius's motive: some saw principle, some private hatred of Caesar, and Shakespeare preserves that uncertainty.

**Literary significance:** Jealousy is written in antithesis — "this man" against "Cassius", "a god" against "A wretched creature", "that name" against "yours". The posture words do the emotional work: "bend his body", "nod", "underlings", "Walk under his huge legs". Cassius's rhetoric constantly measures: write the names, sound them, weigh them. A jealous man is a man with scales in his head. Caesar's description, meanwhile, is a character sketch in the manner of a Theophrastan 'character' — a type drawn from the outside — and it is one of the finest in Shakespeare.

**ICSE keywords:** jealousy, envy, resentment, comparison, inferiority, rivalry, antithesis, motive, rationalisation, lean and hungry, self-deception, character sketch.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 4 Scene 3, where Cassius's resentment turns on Brutus himself, in the quarrel over Lucius Pella and the gold, and where he says in effect that Brutus loved Caesar more than he ever loved Cassius — the Act 1 wound reopened. Glance ahead to Act 5 Scene 3, where Cassius's misreading of Titinius, the comrade he named in the fever story, ends his life.

::: insight Jealousy as a political engine
The Republic's constitution ran on jealousy. Two consuls, each able to veto the other; ten tribunes; annual elections; a horror of any man standing above his peers — the whole machine was designed on the assumption that aristocrats would watch one another with suspicious eyes and that this mutual watchfulness would keep anyone from becoming a king. Cassius's jealousy is, in that light, not a private vice but the republican reflex working as designed: a senator who feels sick when he sees a greater man than himself is exactly the senator the system needs. The tragedy is that by 44 BC the system had been overtaken by one man, so that the reflex no longer had a lawful outlet — no veto could touch a dictator for life — and it turned into a dagger. This is why Cassius can experience his jealousy as patriotism without lying to himself: in Rome, for five centuries, they had been the same feeling. The reading is LITERARY INTERPRETATION built on HISTORICAL RECORD, and it is the one that lets you write about Cassius without the schoolroom choice between 'villain' and 'patriot'. He is a republican organ working in a body that has already changed.
:::

## 8. Political Anxiety

**Definition:** Political anxiety is the fear that the state is about to change in a way you cannot control — fear not of a particular event but of an unknown future. It is the emotional weather of Act 1. Nobody in the Act is in power except Caesar, and even Caesar is anxious; everyone else is listening for shouts, reading faces, asking what happened. The theme is closely tied to the fact that almost every important event in the Act is *heard about* rather than seen.

**Act 1 evidence:** Listen to the Act's sounds. Brutus interrupts Cassius at the first shout — "What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king" — and again at the second, guessing at "some new honours". Fear here precedes information. When Caesar's train returns, Brutus reads it like a weather report: "The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow, / And all the rest look like a chidden train" (a chidden train is a scolded retinue); "Calpurnia's cheek is pale"; Cicero has "ferret and such fiery eyes". Caesar himself, who should be the Act's one secure man, wants "men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights" — an anxious man's wish for untroubled company — and then walks away talking about the man he does not fear. Casca's news that the tribunes "are put to silence" lands quietly, but it is the Act's first proof that disagreement now carries a cost. In Scene 3 anxiety becomes atmosphere: a sky nobody should walk in, a challenge in the dark — "Who's there?" "A Roman." — and men who hide when footsteps approach: "Stand close awhile, for here comes one in haste." Cassius's soliloquy ends the second scene on the Act's keynote: "let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure."

**Important scenes:** The shouts and Caesar's return in Act 1 Scene 2; Casca's news of the tribunes; the opening and the furtive meetings of Act 1 Scene 3.

**Characters:** Brutus is the Act's most anxious man, and his anxiety is about Rome rather than himself. Caesar is anxious and will not admit it. Casca carries the rumours. Cassius converts anxiety into action. Cicero's "ferret" eyes show that even the detached man is disturbed.

**Quotations:**
- "What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." — Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Let me have men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights." — Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Indeed, they say the Senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king" — Casca, Act 1 Scene 3.
- "let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure." — Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2.

**Hidden significance:** Brutus's fear arrives before Cassius's persuasion. He hears a shout and fears a king without a word of prompting — so Cassius does not plant the anxiety; he harvests it. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: the conspiracy is less Cassius's creation than the organised form of a fear that was already general, and Act 1 is careful to show the fear in Brutus, in Caesar, in Casca and in the tribunes before it shows it in a plot. A second point: "they say" — Casca's rumour of a crowning is unsourced. The Act's decisive political fact is hearsay, and no one checks it. A third: Caesar's anxiety and the conspirators' are the same anxiety. He fears thin, watchful men; they fear a Colossus. Both are frightened of the other's future.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: in early 44 BC Caesar had just been made dictator for life, a diadem had been placed on his statue and removed by the tribunes, and rumours circulated of a plan to make him king outside Italy — Shakespeare's Casca repeats a version of that rumour. It is also HISTORICAL RECORD that in 1599 England was governed by a queen of sixty-six with no named heir; audiences may have recognised the sensation of a state waiting for a change it could not name. Do not claim more than 'may have'.

**Literary significance:** The theme is created by offstage sound — the Folio's flourishes and shouts — and by the device of reported action, so that the audience shares the characters' position of guessing. Faces become texts to be read: "spot", "cheek", "eyes". Scene 3's night setting, the challenge-and-password exchange and the whispered names give the Act a conspiratorial register that it did not have in the daylight of Scene 1. The rhyming couplet that closes Scene 2 — "sure" / "endure" — is a formal signal: a decision has been reached.

**ICSE keywords:** anxiety, instability, rumour, hearsay, uncertainty, foreboding, tension, reported action, offstage sound, conspiratorial atmosphere, suspense, succession.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 1, where Brutus's anxiety becomes insomnia and a reasoning that condemns Caesar for what he might become. Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 4, where Portia's panic in the street is political anxiety at its most physical. Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 1, where the conspirators' fear that Popilius Lena knows their plan nearly undoes them, and to Act 4 and Act 5, where anxiety becomes the mood of two armies waiting for a battle neither is sure of.

## 9. Leadership

**Definition:** Leadership is the capacity to make others follow — by command, by example, by reputation or by persuasion. The theme in Act 1 is a comparison of *kinds*: Caesar leads by presence and command, Cassius by argument in private, Brutus by a reputation he has not yet used, the tribunes by shaming a crowd. The question the Act poses is not 'who is the best leader?' but 'what does each kind of leadership cost, and what does it fail to see?'

**Act 1 evidence:** Caesar's leadership is a grammar of imperatives — "Set on", "Set him before me", "Pass" — answered by Antony's maxim, "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed." It is also a performance of invulnerability: "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar." Yet in the same breath he says "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf" — the leader who claims to fear nothing has to arrange where his advisers stand so that he can hear them. Casca's report shows the public face of this leadership: Caesar offers the crowd his throat, falls, and then asks "their Worships to think it was his infirmity" — a leader managing his own weakness as theatre, and succeeding, since "Three or four wenches where I stood cried 'Alas, good soul!'". Cassius's leadership is the opposite: private, patient, tailored to each listener. Brutus's leadership is the Act's great unused asset — "he sits high in all the people's hearts" — and Cassius's arithmetic of it, "Three parts of him / Is ours already", shows a leader being acquired like territory.

**Important scenes:** Caesar's commands and his conversation with Antony in Act 1 Scene 2; Casca's report of the marketplace; Cassius's handling of Brutus and then Casca; the end of Act 1 Scene 3.

**Characters:** Caesar — command and performance. Cassius — persuasion and organisation. Brutus — reputation without yet a single act of leadership. Antony — the perfect subordinate, which is a kind of leadership in waiting. Flavius and Marullus — leadership by rebuke, which clears a street and costs them their voices.

**Quotations:**
- "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed." — Antony, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar." — Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "he sits high in all the people's hearts" — Casca on Brutus, Act 1 Scene 3.
- "Three parts of him / Is ours already, and the man entire / Upon the next encounter yields him ours." — Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3.

**Hidden significance:** Caesar's leadership is shown to depend on *not being seen closely*. From the crowd's distance he is a god; at Cassius's distance he is a man who nearly drowned; at Antony's distance he is a man with a deaf ear. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: Shakespeare constructs leadership as a function of distance, which is why the Act never puts Caesar in a private, equal conversation — the nearest thing is his aside to Antony, and it is about fear. A second point: Brutus's leadership is entirely reputational, and reputational leadership can be *borrowed*. Casca's alchemy speech makes that explicit — the conspirators want his name to make their deed look like virtue. A leader whose authority is his good name is a leader who can be spent by others.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Caesar's authority in 44 BC rested on offices he held — dictator for life, consul, *pontifex maximus* — on an army personally loyal to him, and on an immense popularity with the urban plebs built on games, grain and the spectacle of triumphs. Plutarch records his epilepsy, and Shakespeare preserves it; the deafness is Shakespeare's addition and has no basis in the ancient sources, which makes it a deliberate piece of characterisation rather than a historical fact.

**Literary significance:** The imperative mood is Caesar's signature, and his illeism — speaking of himself as "Caesar" — makes the man sound like his office. The Colossus is the Act's governing image of leadership-as-height, and Flavius's falcon image in Scene 1 gives the same idea a predatory edge: a leader who would "soar above the view of men". Shakespeare arranges a structural contrast between Caesar's public leadership, which we only hear about, and Cassius's private leadership, which we watch in detail — so that the audience knows the conspiracy far better than it knows the dictator.

**ICSE keywords:** leadership, authority, command, charisma, reputation, imperative, illeism, performance, public image, subordinate, acquisition, distance.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 1, where Brutus takes command of the conspiracy on his first night in it and overrules Cassius three times — the leader by reputation becoming a leader by decision, with consequences. Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 2, where leadership passes, in a single afternoon, to whoever can hold the Forum. Glance ahead to Act 4 Scene 3 and Act 5 Scene 1, where Brutus's instinct to overrule Cassius on tactics decides the war.

## 10. Individual vs State

**Definition:** This theme concerns the claims a state can make on a person and the claims a person can make against it: when should a citizen put Rome before his own peace, his friendships and his life — and when does a man's refusal to bow become not selfishness but a public duty? In Rome the conflict had a special shape, because the Republic's whole identity was the refusal to let one individual stand above the community. Act 1 therefore contains two versions of the theme at once: Caesar as the individual who has outgrown the state, and Brutus as the individual the state is about to consume.

**Act 1 evidence:** Brutus states the theme in a single sentence of self-sacrifice: "If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently" (aught means anything; indifferently means impartially). His parting shot to Cassius chooses Rome over comfort: "Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us." Cassius's attack on Caesar is framed entirely as individual against community: "Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man" — the pun on Rome and room says that one man has taken all the space. His appeal to ancestry — "There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king" (brooked means tolerated) — turns Brutus's family into a public obligation. And his contempt for the present — "Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish" (sufferance means patient endurance) — says that individuals who submit have ceased to be Romans. Casca's pledge in the storm is the theme in action: "Be factious for redress of all these griefs, / And I will set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest" (factious means ready to form a faction).

**Important scenes:** Brutus's honour declaration and the villager speech in Act 1 Scene 2; Cassius's Rome-and-room and ancestral-Brutus passages; the dagger speech and Casca's pledge in Act 1 Scene 3.

**Characters:** Brutus is the individual who will subordinate himself to the state — and who has not yet seen that the state, as Cassius presents it, is an abstraction that will demand his friend. Caesar is the individual the state can no longer contain. Cassius argues for the community while serving a private wound. The Cobbler and his friends are individuals for whom the state is simply a holiday.

**Quotations:**
- "If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other" — Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions" — Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man." — Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "That part of tyranny that I do bear / I can shake off at pleasure." — Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3.

**Hidden significance:** The two words Brutus uses — "the general good" — are an abstraction, and the Act gives him no concrete content for it; he never says what Caesar has actually done to Rome. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: Brutus's commitment to the state is sincere and empty at the same time, and Cassius's genius is to pour his own material into it. The second point concerns Cassius's "I can shake off at pleasure": the individual's ultimate weapon against the state, in his account, is his own death — which means that the republican resistance Act 1 describes is, at its root, a willingness to die rather than a plan to govern. Nobody in Act 1 says a word about what Rome will be after Caesar.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: the Republic was founded, by tradition, in 509 BC when Lucius Junius Brutus drove out the last king, and Marcus Brutus claimed descent from him; the Roman hatred of the title *rex* was a cultural fact, not a slogan. Caesar's perpetual dictatorship had broken the one rule that kept individuals subordinate to the state — the time limit on power. Cato the Younger's suicide in 46 BC, which Brutus's family circle revered, had made death-rather-than-submission a living republican example. The 1599 audience, by contrast, lived under a doctrine that placed the monarch above individual judgement; the play's sympathy for republican individuals may have been felt as daring. Say 'may have'.

**Literary significance:** The theme is written in antithesis and in puns: Rome/room, honour/death, villager/son of Rome. Cassius's "yoke" is the Act's image of the state as something an individual wears — an ox's harness — and his "bondage" and "bondman" extend it. Brutus's "villager" is a small, concrete word placed against the grand abstraction "son of Rome", and the modesty of it is the point. Structurally, Shakespeare gives the two most selfless-sounding speeches in the Act — Brutus's honour declaration and Cassius's dagger speech — to the two men whose private motives are most different, so that the audience learns to hear the same public language in two keys.

**ICSE keywords:** individual, state, citizenship, public duty, private conscience, republicanism, tyranny, self-sacrifice, abstraction, pun, antithesis, tyrannicide.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 2 Scene 1, where Brutus resolves the theme in favour of the state in his orchard — and where Portia, kneeling, insists that his private self has claims too. Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 2, where Brutus tells the crowd he loved Rome more than Caesar, which is this Act's honour speech turned into a public defence. Glance ahead to Act 5 Scene 5, where the individual pays the state's bill in full.

## 11. Mob Psychology

**Definition:** Mob psychology is the study of how people in a crowd think and feel differently from the way they do alone — more emotionally, more suggestibly, more quickly, and with a loss of individual judgement. Shakespeare had no such term; what he had was a set of Elizabethan words — "rabblement", "the vulgar", "tag-rag people", "the common herd" — and an extraordinary ear for how a crowd sounds. The Act's crowd is heard far more than it is seen: in Scene 1 it stands before us as two tradesmen and their friends; after that it is a noise offstage and a report.

**Act 1 evidence:** The commoners in Scene 1 have come to cheer Caesar; Marullus reminds them that they once sat all day on walls and chimney-tops to cheer Pompey, making "an universal shout, / That Tiber trembled underneath her banks / To hear the replication of your sounds" (replication means echo). His insult — "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!" — is a theory of the mob: it is matter without mind. And it works: the crowd that arrived laughing leaves in silence, "tongue-tied in their guiltiness". In Scene 2 the crowd is three shouts offstage, and then Casca's report, which is the Act's most savage description of a crowd: "the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps" and produced such "stinking breath" that Caesar swooned. They cheer Caesar for refusing a crown, forgive him for fainting, and would, Casca says, have done the same "if Caesar had stabbed their mothers". His comparison is theatrical: they clap and hiss "as they use to do the players in the theatre". Cassius's version is animal: Caesar would not be a wolf "But that he sees the Romans are but sheep; / He were no lion, were not Romans hinds" (hinds means female deer — and, punningly, servants). Rome is "trash", "rubbish", "offal" — kindling for one man's fire.

**Important scenes:** The whole of Act 1 Scene 1; the shouts and Casca's report in Act 1 Scene 2; Cassius's sheep-and-lion speech in Act 1 Scene 3.

**Characters:** The Carpenter and the Cobbler are the mob with faces — witty, cheerful, immovable in their wish for a holiday. The tribunes are the crowd's would-be masters. Casca is its contemptuous observer, and his disgust is physical. Cassius is its theorist, and his theory conveniently excuses the people he intends to lead.

**Quotations:**
- "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!" — Marullus, Act 1 Scene 1.
- "They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness." — Flavius, Act 1 Scene 1.
- "If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre, I am no true man." — Casca, Act 1 Scene 2.
- "he sees the Romans are but sheep; / He were no lion, were not Romans hinds." — Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3.

**Hidden significance:** Every description of the crowd in Act 1 comes from someone who despises it — tribunes, Casca, Cassius — and every one of those men wants to use it. The only crowd members who speak for themselves, in Scene 1, are not senseless at all; the Cobbler out-talks two magistrates. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: Shakespeare lets the elite describe the mob as stupid while showing us a mob that is quick, humorous and perfectly aware of what it wants. The gap between the crowd's reputation and its reality is itself a piece of appearance versus reality. A second point: the crowd's fickleness is demonstrated twice before it is ever named — shamed out of its holiday by Marullus, then cheering and forgiving Caesar an hour later. The Act establishes that the Roman crowd can be *turned*, and turned fast, by whoever speaks to it last. That is not a detail; it is the mechanism of the whole play.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch's account of the Lupercalia says the crowd applauded feebly when the diadem was offered and loudly when Caesar refused it — the historical crowd, in other words, was republican in sentiment, and Shakespeare's Casca sneers at exactly the behaviour Plutarch records as sound. The urban plebs of the late Republic were a political force courted with grain, games and triumphs, and Caesar's popularity with them was real. London's own apprentices and playgoers in 1599 were a crowd Shakespeare knew from the inside of the theatre, which may be why Casca's comparison is to "players".

**Literary significance:** The crowd is created by sound effects — the offstage flourishes and shouts — and by a lexicon of contempt: "rabblement", "vulgar", "tag-rag", "herd", "rout". Marullus's rhetorical questions and tricolon of "And do you now…" show how a skilled speaker moves a crowd by shame; Casca's prose shows how a cynic turns the same crowd into comedy; Cassius's animal imagery shows how a conspirator turns it into an excuse. Three registers, one crowd. The prose/verse division in Scene 1 — commoners in prose, tribunes in verse — is the class structure of Rome made audible.

**ICSE keywords:** mob, fickleness, crowd psychology, manipulation, demagogue, rhetorical question, tricolon, contempt, animal imagery, prose and verse, public opinion, volatility.

**Connection to later Acts:** Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 2, the scene the whole of this theme exists to prepare: the crowd that is turned by Brutus and then turned back by Antony inside a quarter of an hour. Glance ahead to Act 3 Scene 3, where the mob tears Cinna the poet apart for his name — the "blocks" and "stones" of Scene 1 become killers. Glance ahead to Act 4 Scene 1, where the men who used the crowd divide the world among themselves without consulting it.

::: insight The crowd as a mirror of the leaders' anxieties
Watch what each leader sees when he looks at the crowd, and you will learn more about the leader than about the crowd. Marullus sees ingratitude, because he is a tribune whose office exists to speak for a people that has stopped listening to him. Casca sees a theatre audience, because he is a man who performs — his bluntness is "tardy form", a costume — and he suspects everyone else of acting too. Cassius sees sheep and kindling, because he needs the people to be passive in order to believe that one strong will can change Rome, and he needs them to be base so that his contempt for Caesar's popularity has somewhere to go. Caesar, in Casca's report, sees a crowd whose forgiveness he can win by offering his throat, because he is a man who has learned to turn weakness into theatre. Not one of them sees the Cobbler: a tradesman with a safe conscience and a day off, who wants to see a triumph and then go home. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: Shakespeare gives the crowd no interior of its own in Act 1 precisely so that it can function as a glass in which the ruling class looks at its own fears — fear of being forgotten, of being fooled, of being outnumbered, of being seen to fall. In Act 3 the glass will show them something they did not expect.
:::

::: icse WRITING A THEME PARAGRAPH
EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE. First, the honesty. Act 1 belongs to Class IX, and a Class X board question will not ask for 'superstition and omens in Act 1' as such. But a question on Calpurnia's dream, on Caesar's rejection of the augurers, on Cassius's eagles and ravens, or on the ghost will expect you to show where the play's omens began, and a good paragraph about Act 1 is the model for the paragraph you will write about Act 2, Act 4 or Act 5. The recipe is the same at every level: a *claim* about what the play does with the theme; *evidence*, meaning a short quotation with speaker and scene; *analysis* of how the words work and what they reveal; a *link* back to the claim or forward to the play. Avoid two habits that lose marks: retelling the storm as if it were a story, and ending with a moral ('this shows that superstition is bad'). Here is a worked paragraph.

'Shakespeare uses superstition and omens in Act 1 not to show whether the gods speak but to show how men choose what to hear. Caesar is the first example: he insists that Antony touch Calpurnia during the Lupercal race so that she may "Shake off their sterile curse", yet within a few lines dismisses the Soothsayer's "Beware the ides of March" with the single word "Pass". The juxtaposition reveals a selective believer — he accepts the ritual that promises an heir and rejects the warning that threatens his life. The storm of Act 1 Scene 3 widens this into a debate. Casca reads the burning hand and the lion as proof that men should "fear and tremble" before the gods' "tokens"; Cicero replies that "men may construe things after their fashion", warning that every reading of the sky is made by a man with a motive; and Cassius proves Cicero right by declaring the prodigies "instruments of fear and warning" aimed at Caesar, so that an omen becomes a recruiting speech. The fire imagery running through the scene — torches, men "all in fire", a tempest "dropping fire" — creates an atmosphere of a world turned against its nature, which mirrors a Rome where fathers' minds are dead. By the end of the Act, therefore, the omens have told the audience less about the future than about the readers: Caesar's pride, Casca's fear and Cassius's design. This is the pattern the play will repeat when Caesar overrules Calpurnia's dream and when Cassius, who mocked Casca, comes to credit the ravens over his own army.'

Notice what that paragraph does. It opens with an argument, not a topic. Every quotation is short, attributed by implication to a speaker and a scene, and followed by a comment on the words themselves — "selective", "recruiting speech". It names one device — juxtaposition, imagery — and says what it achieves. It connects the theme to character, because the examiner's descriptors reward analysis of theme *through* character. It looks forward to later Acts without quoting them, which is exactly the kind of connection a Class X answer needs when it reaches back to Act 1. And it never claims to know what Shakespeare 'believed' about fate. Reproduce the shape, not the sentences.
:::
