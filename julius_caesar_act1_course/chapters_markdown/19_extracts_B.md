# Extract-Based Training (Part Two): The Second Half of Scene 2 and Scene 3

*What this chapter does: it takes the six passages from the second half of Act 1 Scene 2 and from Scene 3 that teachers and examiners return to most often, and trains you to answer on them the way an ICSE evaluator wants — precisely, from the text, and with a sense of why each line exists. For every extract you get the verified words, a thirteen-label commentary that climbs from plain fact to advanced interpretation, a full ICSE-pattern question set with Class IX-style reference-to-the-context questions and MCQs, and marked model answers that show exactly where the marks come from. Read it slowly, with a pen in your hand. This is a workout, not a summary.*

## Before You Begin: The Thirteen Labels and the Five Levels

In Part One of this training (the chapter 'Extract-Based Training (Part One): Scene 1 and the First Half of Scene 2') you met the method: print the extract, walk through thirteen fixed labels, then answer an ICSE-pattern question set. This chapter uses the identical method on harder material — the passages in which Caesar judges Cassius, Casca reports the crown, Cassius reveals his forgery plan, the storm breaks, and the conspiracy takes shape. The labels are not decoration. Each one is a mark-earning move that an evaluator recognises, and together they walk you up the five levels of understanding that this whole book is built on.

| Labels | Level | What the evaluator is testing | Typical question stem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Context, Speaker, Listener, Before, After | Level 1 — What happened? | Recall and accurate placement in the scene | 'Where does this take place? What has just happened?' |
| Meaning, Keywords | Level 2 — What does it mean? | Understanding; paraphrase in your own words | 'Explain the phrase…' |
| Subtext, Character, Device | Level 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way? | Analysis of motive, character and craft | 'What does this reveal about…?' |
| Theme, Historical context, Scene connection | Level 4 — How does it connect to the whole play? | Application across scenes and acts | 'How is this borne out later…?' |
| The [4] sub-question | Level 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer? | Evaluation and supported personal response | 'Do you think…? Give a reason.' |

One habit above all: never write 'the Meaning' before you have fixed 'the Context'. Evaluators can tell within two lines whether a candidate knows where the extract sits in the scene. A student who begins 'Caesar says this after returning from the games, having refused the crown three times' has already signalled Level 1 mastery; a student who begins 'Caesar is a proud man' has signalled nothing yet.

## Extract 1 — Caesar Reads Cassius: "Let me have men about me that are fat"

::: extract Act 1 Scene 2 — Caesar to Antony, as the procession returns from the games
> **Caesar:** Let me have men about me that are fat,
> Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights.
> Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look.
> He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous.
> **Antony:** Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous.
> He is a noble Roman, and well given.
> **Caesar:** Would he were fatter! But I fear him not.
> Yet if my name were liable to fear,
> I do not know the man I should avoid
> So soon as that spare Cassius. […]
> I rather tell thee what is to be feared
> Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar.
> Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf,
> And tell me truly what thou think'st of him.
:::

**Context:** Act 1 Scene 2, the day of the Lupercal. Caesar's procession has returned from the games and is crossing the stage on its way out. Brutus has just observed that "The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow" and that Calpurnia and Cicero look shaken. Caesar draws Antony aside for this private exchange in the middle of a public crowd — the two men are, in effect, talking behind their hands while Brutus and Cassius watch from a distance, waiting to catch Casca.

**Speaker:** Caesar, with one short interjection from Antony. This is Caesar's longest speech in Act 1 and his only extended private conversation in the act.

**Listener:** Antony — the man who, according to Casca a few moments later, was the one who offered Caesar the crown. Brutus and Cassius are on stage but out of earshot, which matters: the audience hears Caesar's judgement of Cassius while Cassius stands there unaware of it.

**Before:** Cassius has spent the whole middle of the scene working on Brutus — the mirror image ("I, your glass"), the Tiber story, the fever in Spain, the Colossus, the argument from names ("'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar.'"). Brutus has asked for time and left Cassius with "chew upon this". Offstage the crowd has shouted three times. Then the procession re-enters and Brutus reads the faces before anyone speaks.

**After:** Caesar and his train exit. Casca remains because Brutus has pulled him by the cloak, and delivers his report of the crown (our Extract 2). Notice the design: the audience receives Caesar's private opinion of Cassius, then immediately receives Casca's private opinion of Caesar. Scene 2 is a scene of assessments — everyone is reading everyone else.

**Meaning:** Caesar tells Antony that he prefers to be surrounded by contented men — fat, "Sleek-headed" (smooth-haired, well-groomed, at ease), and able to sleep at night ("a-nights"). Cassius, over there ("Yond Cassius"), looks thin and unsatisfied — "lean and hungry" — and he thinks too much; men like that are dangerous. Antony reassures him: Cassius is not dangerous, he is a noble Roman and "well given" (of good disposition). Caesar wishes Cassius were fatter, then insists he does not fear him — but if his name were "liable to fear" (open or subject to fear), Cassius is the man he would avoid first. The lines the extract skips explain why: Cassius "reads much", is "a great observer", and "looks / Quite through the deeds of men"; he "loves no plays", "hears no music", and "Seldom he smiles" — and when he does, it is as if he mocked himself for smiling. Such men are never at ease while they see someone greater than themselves. Caesar then closes: he is describing what ought to be feared, not what he fears, because he is always Caesar — and he asks Antony to move to his right side because his left ear is deaf, and to tell him honestly what he thinks of Cassius.

**Subtext:** Two things run beneath the surface. First, Caesar's reading of Cassius is exactly right — the audience has just watched Cassius do everything Caesar describes — and yet Caesar refuses to let his own insight alter his conduct. The line "Would he were fatter! But I fear him not" is the hinge: the wish admits the danger, the denial cancels it. Second, "for always I am Caesar" is the statement of a man who has begun to treat his name as a guarantee. Shakespeare places the boast one line before a confession of physical infirmity: "this ear is deaf". The god who cannot fear cannot hear either. A strong reading (though not the only one) is that Caesar's pivot to the deaf ear is not incidental staging but the play's quiet joke — he asks Antony to "tell me truly" what he thinks of Cassius, but Antony has already told him, and was wrong, and Caesar is now turning his working ear towards the flatterer.

**Character:** Caesar emerges as a superb judge of men and a poor judge of himself. The shrewdness is genuine: he notices Cassius's reading, his observation, his refusal of pleasure, his mirthless smile — a psychological portrait of the ascetic malcontent that Cassius's own speeches have confirmed. But the shrewdness is swallowed by the public role; Caesar speaks of himself in the third person ("always I am Caesar") as though the office had replaced the man. Antony is shown as loyal, generous and easy — "Fear him not" — a man who takes people at face value, which the whole play will test. Cassius, seen from outside for the first time, is confirmed as the thinker and watcher he has shown himself to be — which means Caesar's error is not in perception but in pride.

**Theme:** Appearance versus reality (Caesar sees the reality and prefers his self-image); leadership and its blind spots; the public self versus the private man; fate and character (Caesar's downfall is prepared by a decision he takes knowingly); ambition observed from the top rather than from below.

**Device:** Dramatic irony — the audience knows Cassius is dangerous and hears Caesar dismiss the danger. Antithesis structures the speech: fat against lean, sleeping against thinking, men who love plays against the man who hears no music, "what is to be feared" against "what I fear". Illeism — referring to oneself in the third person — in "always I am Caesar", which the play repeats whenever Caesar is most vulnerable. Physiognomy, the old belief that the body reveals the mind, underlies "lean and hungry look". And the deaf ear is a physical symbol: an infirmity placed exactly where a boast of invulnerability has just been made. Note also that this is verse, formal and balanced, immediately before Casca's jagged prose.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch reports that Caesar said he did not fear fat, long-haired men but the pale, thin ones, meaning Brutus and Cassius; Shakespeare has taken the remark and turned it into a scene. Cassius and Brutus were both praetors in 44 BC, and Brutus received the more prestigious urban praetorship — ancient sources make this a grievance of Cassius, which sits behind Shakespeare's "Caesar doth bear me hard" later in the scene. Antony was Caesar's consular colleague in 44 BC. The deafness is different: no ancient source gives Caesar a deaf ear, and scholars treat it as Shakespeare's addition. That makes it doubly important for you — a detail Shakespeare chose rather than inherited is a detail he wanted you to notice.

**Scene connection:** Everything Caesar says here is verified within a hundred lines. Cassius's soliloquy (Extract 4) shows the man who "thinks too much" thinking his way to forgery; Scene 3 shows him baring his chest to the lightning and declaring that "dangers are to me indifferent". Caesar's confidence that he cannot be threatened by his own name is the same confidence that, in Act 3 Scene 1, keeps him in the Senate House until the daggers are out. Antony's misjudgement here is also the first note of the Antony–Cassius antagonism that shapes Acts 3 to 5: Cassius, who reads men "Quite through", will want Antony dead; Brutus will spare him.

**Keywords:** shrewd, perceptive, physiognomy, complacent, hubris, self-mythologising, dramatic irony, infirmity, misjudgement, foreshadowing, public persona, ascetic.

::: model Model answer (i)
Caesar and Antony are in a public place in Rome on the feast of Lupercal; Caesar's procession is passing through on its way home from the games, where Antony has run his course and where (as Casca will report) a crown was offered to Caesar three times. Just before Caesar speaks, Brutus has observed that "The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow", that the followers look "like a chidden train", that Calpurnia's cheek is pale, and that Cicero has the fiery, "ferret" eyes he wears when crossed by senators. Cassius has told Brutus to "pluck Casca by the sleeve" as they pass, so that Casca, in his "sour fashion", can tell them what happened.

**Why this answer earns marks:** It places the extract exactly (Lupercal, the returning procession), answers each of the three parts separately, and supports two of them with short, accurate quotations. An evaluator awards the three marks quickly because nothing has to be searched for.
:::

::: model Model answer (ii)
Caesar wants to be surrounded by comfortable, contented men — well-fed, well-groomed, able to sleep soundly at night — because a satisfied man has no reason to plot. The "lean and hungry look" suggests that Cassius is thin, restless and unsatisfied, hungry not for food but for something he lacks — power, recognition, or Caesar's downfall. Caesar believes such a man is dangerous because "He thinks too much": a man who broods, observes and is never at ease is the kind of man who turns his discontent into action.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The paraphrase is in the candidate's own words, the metaphor of hunger is actually explained rather than repeated, and the third part links the adjective to Caesar's stated reason. Explaining a phrase means unpacking the image; this answer does that.
:::

::: model Model answer (iii)
In the omitted lines Caesar says that Cassius "reads much", that he is "a great observer" who "looks / Quite through the deeds of men", that he "loves no plays" and "hears no music" as Antony does, and that he "Seldom he smiles" — and when he does, it is as if he mocked himself for smiling. Caesar adds that such men are never at ease while they see someone greater than themselves. The rest of Act 1 most clearly confirms the last of these: Cassius's speeches to Brutus are consumed by the injustice that Caesar has "become a god" while Cassius "must bend his body", and his soliloquy shows a man who reads other men accurately enough to plan how to seduce Brutus.

**Why this answer earns marks:** Three reasons are stated with textual precision (more than three, in fact, which protects the candidate if one is judged weak), and the second part does not merely name a reason but shows it being confirmed with evidence from elsewhere in the act. That cross-referencing is what separates a two-mark from a three-mark answer.
:::

::: model Model answer (iv)
Antony's "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given" reveals a man who is loyal, generous in his judgements, and inclined to take people at their public face value; he sees a fellow noble and assumes good nature. He is also a soothing presence for Caesar rather than a challenging one. His judgement is proved wrong within the same scene, when Cassius alone on stage announces that he will forge letters in "several hands" to work on Brutus and that "we will shake him, or worse days endure", and again in Scene 3, when Cassius wins Casca to the conspiracy and sends Cinna out with the forged papers.

**Why this answer earns marks:** It infers character from the exact words rather than asserting it, it names two qualities plus a role (the reassurer), and it answers 'how is he proved wrong' with two specific events in the correct order. The evaluator can tick recall, inference and connection.
:::

::: model Model answer (v)
The irony is that Caesar claims a godlike constancy — he is not a man who fears, he is simply "Caesar", a name that stands above fear — and in the very next breath confesses an ordinary human weakness: one ear cannot hear. The boast of being above human limits is undercut by a bodily limit, and the deaf ear becomes a symbol of a man who will not hear warnings — he has already dismissed the Soothsayer as "a dreamer". There is further irony in the fact that he turns his good ear towards Antony, who has just misjudged Cassius, and asks him to "tell me truly". In my view, Caesar's refusal to fear Cassius is a weakness, because the refusal is not based on information — his information is excellent — but on image: he says "Would he were fatter!", which admits the danger, and then overrides his own judgement to protect the idea of himself as fearless. A leader who can see a threat and chooses not to act on it, in order to seem invulnerable, has made himself more vulnerable, and the play confirms this when the man he correctly suspects leads the conspiracy against him.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The four marks are spread deliberately: two for the irony (the boast against the infirmity, plus the extra layer of turning the ear to Antony), one for a clear, committed opinion, and one for a reason anchored in a quotation ("Would he were fatter!") and in the outcome of the play. An evaluator rewards a personal response that is argued, not merely stated.
:::

## Extract 2 — Casca Reports the Crown: "he would fain have had it"

::: extract Act 1 Scene 2 — Casca to Brutus and Cassius, after Caesar's procession has left
> **Casca:** Why, there was a crown offered him; and, being offered him, he put it by with the back of his hand, thus, and then the people fell a-shouting.
> […]
> **Brutus:** Tell us the manner of it, gentle Casca.
> **Casca:** I can as well be hanged as tell the manner of it. It was mere foolery; I did not mark it. I saw Mark Antony offer him a crown (yet 'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets), and, as I told you, he put it by once; but for all that, to my thinking, he would fain have had it.
> Then he offered it to him again; then he put it by again; but to my thinking, he was very loath to lay his fingers off it.
> And then he offered it the third time. He put it the third time by, and still as he refused it the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps and uttered such a deal of stinking breath because Caesar refused the crown that it had almost choked Caesar, for he swooned and fell down at it.
:::

**Context:** Act 1 Scene 2, immediately after Caesar's train has gone. Brutus has pulled Casca by the cloak, and Casca — surprised that Brutus, who was in the procession, needs to ask — explains what happened at the games while Brutus and Cassius were talking. Between the two halves of the extract lie the quick exchanges in which Brutus asks what the second and third shouts were for and Casca answers "Why, for that too", and confirms that the crown was offered three times and refused "every time gentler than other".

**Speaker:** Casca, in prose. Every other noble in the scene speaks verse; Casca's choice of prose is itself a characterising device, the 'sour fashion' Cassius promised.

**Listener:** Brutus and Cassius — the two men most anxious to know whether Rome has just acquired a king. Brutus asks the eager, factual questions ("Was the crown offered him thrice?"); Cassius supplies the count ("They shouted thrice").

**Before:** The three offstage shouts punctuated Cassius's temptation of Brutus, and Brutus interpreted them fearfully: "I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." Then Caesar returned looking angry and, in Extract 1, privately named Cassius as the man to be feared.

**After:** Cassius asks, "What, did Caesar swoon?" and Casca describes the fit in the market-place — "foamed at mouth and was speechless" — which leads into Extract 3: the "falling sickness" pun, Cicero's Greek, and the news that Marullus and Flavius have been "put to silence".

**Meaning:** Casca says a crown was offered to Caesar and Caesar pushed it away "with the back of his hand, thus" — Casca mimes the gesture as he speaks — at which the people shouted. Pressed for the manner of it, he claims he would as soon be hanged as describe it, that it was nonsense ("mere foolery") and he paid no attention ("I did not mark it"). Then he describes it in detail. Antony offered Caesar a crown — though, Casca sneers, it was not a real crown but one of these little "coronets" — and Caesar refused it; but in Casca's opinion ("to my thinking") he "would fain have had it" — would gladly have accepted it. Offered again, he refused again, but was "very loath" — extremely reluctant — to take his fingers off it. Offered a third time, he refused a third time, and each time he refused, the mob ("rabblement") hooted, clapped their rough, cracked ("chopped") hands, threw up their sweaty caps and let out such a quantity of foul breath because Caesar had refused that it nearly suffocated him — for he fainted ("swooned") and fell down.

**Subtext:** Casca is not reporting; he is interpreting, and he tells you so twice with "to my thinking". Strip that phrase out and the facts are: a crown offered three times, refused three times, applause each time, a collapse. Put it back and the account becomes a charge of hypocrisy — Caesar wanted the crown and staged the refusals. Notice too what Casca does to the crowd: he cannot admit that ordinary Romans cheered a principled refusal, so he turns their joy into a physical assault of smell and sweat that literally knocks Caesar down. The comic causation — Caesar fainting from the people's breath — is a joke, but a political one: Casca finds both the ruler and the ruled contemptible. And his opening pose of boredom ("I did not mark it") is contradicted by the precision of what follows; a man who noticed the reluctance in Caesar's fingers was watching very closely indeed.

**Character:** Casca appears blunt, cynical and snobbish, with a gift for deflating grandeur — the coronet, the sweaty nightcaps, the swoon. Yet he is also a shrewd observer whose scepticism about Caesar's motives makes him natural material for Cassius; Brutus's later remark that he was "quick mettle when he went to school" tells us the bluntness is a manner, not a limit. Caesar, seen through Casca, is a man performing humility while (perhaps) desiring the crown, and a man whose body fails him in public. Antony is the instrument of the offer — whether as a loyal friend testing the crowd or as a partner in a piece of theatre, the text does not say. Brutus, through his questions, is revealed as anxious and dependent on others' eyes for what he did not see.

**Theme:** Ambition and its concealment; appearance versus reality; public opinion and the fickleness of the crowd; politics as performance; the unreliability of report.

**Device:** Reported action — an offstage event delivered through a biased narrator, which is the central device of this passage. Repetition builds the structure ("Then he offered it… then he put it by again… And then he offered it the third time") and the doubled "to my thinking" flags the narrator's bias. Anticlimax and bathos deflate the ceremony: a crown becomes "one of these coronets"; the divine Caesar faints from bad breath. The sensory imagery is deliberately coarse — tactile ("chopped hands"), visual ("sweaty nightcaps"), olfactory ("stinking breath") — and hyperbole turns applause into near-suffocation. The gesture in "with the back of his hand, thus" is a stage direction hidden in the dialogue: the actor playing Casca reproduces Caesar's hand. And the whole thing is prose, set against the verse that surrounds it, which makes Casca sound like a man who refuses to dignify what he saw.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch describes the Lupercalia of 15 February 44 BC: Antony, running as one of the Luperci while serving as consul, offered Caesar a diadem wreathed in laurel; the crowd applauded thinly at the offer and loudly at the refusal; Caesar finally ordered the diadem carried to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitol. In Plutarch the offer and refusal happen twice; Shakespeare's Casca makes it three times, a number with a stronger rhythm on stage. Shakespeare also adds what Plutarch does not supply — the cynical inner reading, "he would fain have had it". The word "coronets" is worth a moment: Romans loathed the title of king (rex) so intensely that even the object had to be diminished; Casca's sneer echoes an ancient Roman anxiety. Whether the historical Caesar wanted the diadem is debated by historians, and the play deliberately leaves it open too.

**Scene connection:** This report completes the three shouts Brutus and Cassius heard but could not interpret. It also completes Scene 1: the "coronets" and the crowd's cheering are the same populace the tribunes scolded, now cheering Caesar's refusal exactly as they cheered his triumph. Looking forward, Antony will remind the Roman crowd in his funeral oration in Act 3 Scene 2 that Caesar three times refused a crown at the Lupercal, using the identical event to prove that Caesar was not ambitious — the same facts, the opposite reading. Casca's "to my thinking" is thus the first battle in a war of interpretation that the whole play fights.

**Keywords:** reported action, unreliable narrator, cynicism, political theatre, anticlimax, bathos, mob, fickle, class contempt, scepticism, ambiguity, prose.

::: insight How examiners reward 'reported action' awareness
The crown is never seen. Everything you know about it comes from Casca, a witness who announces his bias ("to my thinking") and his contempt for the crowd, and who has already been introduced to you by Cassius as a man who speaks in a "sour fashion". Weak answers write 'Caesar refused the crown three times but secretly wanted it' as though the play had shown this. Strong answers write 'According to Casca, Caesar refused the crown three times; Casca believes he wanted it' — and then go further, asking why Shakespeare kept the event offstage at all. Three effects are worth knowing. First, ambiguity: because we never see Caesar's face, his desire for the crown remains an open question, and the play can keep Caesar poised between tyrant and victim. Second, alignment: the audience is placed exactly where Brutus and Cassius stand — dependent on a report — so we experience the conspiracy's own epistemic problem, deciding a man's guilt on hearsay. Third, thematic rehearsal: Julius Caesar is a play about how events are read — Cicero will say in Scene 3 that "men may construe things after their fashion" — and Casca's report is the first great demonstration of construing. An ICSE evaluator marking a [4] question on this passage is looking for a candidate who can separate what Casca says happened from what Casca thinks it meant, and who can name a reason for Shakespeare's choice. That separation is not pedantry; it is the difference between reading the play and reading a summary of it.
:::

## Extract 3 — The Falling Sickness, Greek, and Silence

::: extract Act 1 Scene 2 — Cassius, Casca and Brutus, continuing Casca's report
> **Cassius:** But soft, I pray you. What, did Caesar swoon?
> **Casca:** He fell down in the market-place and foamed at mouth and was speechless.
> **Brutus:** 'Tis very like; he hath the falling sickness.
> **Cassius:** No, Caesar hath it not; but you and I
> And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness.
> **Casca:** I know not what you mean by that, but I am sure Caesar fell down. If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre, I am no true man.
> […]
> **Cassius:** Did Cicero say anything?
> **Casca:** Ay, he spoke Greek.
> **Cassius:** To what effect?
> **Casca:** Nay, an I tell you that, I'll ne'er look you i' th' face again. But those that understood him smiled at one another and shook their heads. But for mine own part, it was Greek to me. I could tell you more news too: Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence. Fare you well.
:::

**Context:** Act 1 Scene 2, the tail of Casca's report. Cassius has caught the word "swooned" at the end of Extract 2 and stops Casca to ask about it. The passage runs from the fit in the market-place to the last item of Casca's news, after which Cassius invites him to supper and Casca leaves.

**Speaker:** Three speakers share the passage — Cassius asking, Casca reporting, Brutus interpreting — but it is Casca's prose that carries the news and Cassius's verse that carries the argument.

**Listener:** Each man is listening for something different. Cassius listens for ammunition; Brutus listens for facts and offers the kindest available explanation; Casca listens to himself and refuses to be drawn.

**Before:** Casca has just described the three refusals of the crown and Caesar's collapse — he "swooned and fell down at it". In the lines the extract omits, he adds that Caesar, seeing the crowd glad at his refusal, "plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut", and that on recovering he asked the crowd to blame his "infirmity" — while "Three or four wenches" cried "Alas, good soul!" and forgave him.

**After:** Cassius asks Casca to supper "tonight" (refused: "I am promised forth") and to dinner "tomorrow" (accepted, with Casca's characteristic conditions). Casca exits; Brutus and Cassius assess him; Brutus leaves; and Cassius, alone, delivers the soliloquy that is Extract 4.

**Meaning:** Cassius interrupts — "But soft" (wait a moment) — to check that Caesar actually fainted. Casca confirms it plainly: Caesar fell in the market-place, foamed at the mouth and could not speak. Brutus says this is very likely ("'Tis very like"), because Caesar suffers from "the falling sickness", the Elizabethan name for epilepsy. Cassius turns the diagnosis inside out: it is not Caesar who has the falling sickness, but Brutus, Cassius and "honest Casca" — meaning that they, and Rome, are the ones who have fallen, sunk into servility before one man. Casca declines to interpret the remark — he says he does not know what Cassius means — and restates the plain fact: Caesar fell. He then compares the crowd to a theatre audience: the ragged, motley ("tag-rag") people clapped and hissed Caesar exactly as they are accustomed ('use') to clap and hiss actors ('players'), depending on whether he pleased or displeased them — and if that is not so, Casca says, he is no honest man. Asked whether Cicero said anything, Casca reports that Cicero "spoke Greek". Asked what he meant, Casca refuses — if he told them that, he would never be able to look them in the face again — but notes that those who understood Cicero smiled at each other and shook their heads; for his own part, Casca says, it was Greek to him, incomprehensible. Finally, almost as an afterthought, he adds that Marullus and Flavius have been "put to silence" — silenced, removed from public voice — for stripping the decorative "scarves" from Caesar's statues.

**Subtext:** Cassius's pun is the most economical piece of propaganda in the act: a medical fact about Caesar is converted, in one line, into a moral indictment of everyone who lets Caesar stand over them. Brutus offers the humane reading; Cassius supplies the political one; the audience is asked to choose. Casca's "I know not what you mean by that" is left open by the text. One reading is that a blunt soldier genuinely misses the wordplay; a stronger reading is that Casca, in a public place, in the presence of a man he does not yet know to be a conspirator, is refusing to be drawn into treasonous talk — he retreats to "I am sure Caesar fell down", a fact no one can be punished for repeating. The theatre comparison carries its own sting: if the people are an audience, then Caesar is a player, and the crown scene was a performance — which is exactly what Casca has been implying. The Greek episode adds a class layer: Rome's educated elite exchanged an opinion of the show over the heads of the crowd, in a code the crowd could not break, and their smiles and head-shakes are a quiet vote of no confidence. Casca claims membership of the uneducated ("it was Greek to me"), which we may doubt, since Brutus remembers him as "quick mettle when he went to school"; the pose of philistinism is another of his masks. And then the news that ends the passage. Two tribunes — officers whose very persons were sacred — have been silenced for touching a statue, and Casca reports it in the tone of a man mentioning the weather, before saying good night. The nonchalance is the horror.

**Character:** Cassius: quick, bitter, opportunistic, unable to hear a fact without turning it into an argument for revolt. Brutus: literal, fair-minded, inclined to the generous explanation — the man who will later insist on the conspiracy's purity is here the man who diagnoses rather than accuses. Casca: guarded and sardonic, a witness who mocks everything and commits to nothing — yet whose final item of news, delivered so flatly, is the most damning thing anyone says about Caesar in the scene. Caesar: physically frail, publicly humiliated, and — through the silencing of the tribunes — quietly ruthless.

**Theme:** Appearance versus reality (the divine Caesar foaming on the ground); public opinion and the crowd as audience; liberty against tyranny (the silenced tribunes); the body politic — a diseased Rome in Cassius's pun; the gap between the educated few and the many.

**Device:** Pun and wordplay on "the falling sickness" — literal epilepsy against political prostration. Extended metaphor of the theatre: crowd as audience, Caesar as player, politics as performance, which also winks at the Elizabethan audience who were themselves clapping and hissing. Euphemism and understatement in "put to silence", where the mild phrase covers an act of repression. Irony in Casca's professed ignorance of Greek. Contrast of media: Cassius's pointed verse against Casca's flat, refusing prose. And the passage coins an idiom that English still uses — 'it's all Greek to me' — which is worth knowing but not worth more than a sentence in an answer.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch and Suetonius both record that Caesar suffered from epilepsy; modern historians debate the diagnosis, but Shakespeare took it straight from his source. Cicero was Rome's greatest orator and a lover of Greek learning; Greek was the language of philosophy and culture among the Roman elite, so Casca's detail is historically apt even though this particular moment is Shakespeare's invention. The tribunes Gaius Epidius Marullus and Lucius Caesetius Flavus really did remove a diadem from Caesar's statue and arrest men who hailed him as king, and Caesar had them deposed from office (Suetonius adds that they were expelled from the Senate). Historically this happened in January 44 BC, before the Lupercalia; Shakespeare compresses it into the same day. What "put to silence" means is left open by the play — deposition, banishment or death — and you should say so rather than choosing for Shakespeare. Remember, too, that tribunes were sacrosanct: to silence one was to strike at the office that had protected ordinary Romans for four and a half centuries.

**Scene connection:** This passage pays off Scene 1 exactly: Flavius ordered "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies", Marullus asked nervously "May we do so?", and here is the answer. Casca's account of Caesar's fit corroborates, from a second witness, the picture of physical weakness that Cassius painted for Brutus in the fever-in-Spain story — the god who "did shake" and cried for drink "As a sick girl". Cicero's disapproving Greek prepares us for his sceptical presence in Scene 3 (Extract 5). And the theatre metaphor is the seed of Act 3 Scene 2, where the Roman crowd will be swung first one way and then the other by two orators, precisely like an audience responding to rival players.

**Keywords:** pun, wordplay, body politic, servility, theatre metaphor, performance, euphemism, repression, sacrosanct, corroboration, philistinism, nonchalance.

## Extract 4 — Cassius Alone: "Well, Brutus, thou art noble"

::: extract Act 1 Scene 2 — Cassius, alone on stage, after Brutus has gone
> **Cassius:** Well, Brutus, thou art noble; yet I see
> Thy honourable mettle may be wrought
> From that it is disposed. Therefore it is meet
> That noble minds keep ever with their likes;
> For who so firm that cannot be seduced?
> Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus.
> […] I will this night,
> In several hands, in at his windows throw,
> As if they came from several citizens,
> Writings, all tending to the great opinion
> That Rome holds of his name, wherein obscurely
> Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at.
> And after this, let Caesar seat him sure,
> For we will shake him, or worse days endure.
:::

**Context:** The last fifteen lines of Act 1 Scene 2. Casca has left for his supper, Brutus and Cassius have exchanged their opinions of him, and Brutus has gone home with an appointment for tomorrow — "Come home to me, and I will wait for you." The stage, which began the scene crowded with a festival procession, is now empty except for one man, and he turns to the audience. This is the only soliloquy in Act 1, and the first in the play.

**Speaker:** Cassius — and for the first time Cassius without an audience to work on. Everything he has said so far in the scene has been said to Brutus and shaped for Brutus. Here he speaks for himself.

**Listener:** Nobody on stage; the audience in the theatre. The Elizabethan convention, which you can take as a working rule for this play, is that a character alone on stage tells the truth as he understands it. Soliloquy is not a lie to the audience. That is why this passage is so valuable to you: it is the one place in Act 1 where Cassius's own account of his motives and methods can be checked against what he has just been saying aloud.

**Before:** The long temptation of Brutus (Extracts in Part One of this training), the return of Caesar, Casca's report (Extracts 2 and 3), and then a short conversation about Casca himself: Brutus thinks him grown "a blunt fellow", Cassius defends him — "This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit" — and Brutus leaves with Cassius's parting instruction ringing: "Till then, think of the world."

**After:** The scene ends on a rhyming couplet, and the act's time-scheme jumps. Scene 3 opens a month later, on the stormy night before the ides of March, with Casca and Cicero (Extract 5); by its end Cassius has recruited Casca, given Cinna the forged letters to plant, and announced that Brutus is "Three parts" won. In Act 2 Scene 1 Brutus will read one of these letters in his orchard and finish it in his own head — so the plan hatched here is the plan that actually works.

**Meaning:** Cassius addresses the absent Brutus. 'Well, Brutus, you are noble; yet I see that your honourable nature ("mettle" — temperament, spirit; the old spelling is 'metal', and the pun on metal that can be beaten into shape is live) can be worked ("wrought") away from what it is naturally inclined to ("disposed"). Therefore it is fitting ("it is meet") that noble minds should always keep company with their own kind, because who is so firm that he cannot be led astray ("seduced")? Caesar bears a grudge against me ("doth bear me hard"), but he loves Brutus.' The two lines the extract omits make the comparison explicit: 'If I were Brutus and he were Cassius, he would not win me over ("humour me" — indulge, coax) as I am winning him.' Then the plan. 'Tonight I will throw in at his windows, in several different handwritings ("several hands"), as if they came from a number of different citizens, writings all pointing to ("tending to") the high opinion Rome holds of his name, in which Caesar's ambition will be hinted at ("glancèd at") indirectly ("obscurely"). After that, let Caesar sit as securely as he likes ("seat him sure" — often glossed as a riding image, keeping one's seat in the saddle), for we will shake him — or suffer worse days.'

**Subtext:** Read the first sentence twice. "Thou art noble" is praise; "yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought" turns the praise into a vulnerability. Cassius admires Brutus's honour exactly as a metalworker admires good iron — for what can be made of it. The maxim that follows, "noble minds keep ever with their likes", is spoken by the man who has spent the scene making sure that Brutus does not keep with his likes but with Cassius. He knows, in other words, that he is the bad company he is warning against, and he says so without shame. The rhetorical question "who so firm that cannot be seduced?" is half boast and half philosophy: there are no incorruptible men, only men not yet worked upon. Then the comparison with Caesar — "he loves Brutus" — lets a private grievance surface for a moment: Caesar's favour goes to Brutus, not to Cassius, and the forged letters will exploit precisely the reputation that Cassius lacks and Brutus has. The plan itself is a confession. The letters will seem to come from "several citizens"; they will not; they will praise Brutus and only hint at Caesar; they are designed, that is, to make Brutus believe that Rome is asking him to act. The 'voice of the people' that persuades Brutus in Act 2 is Cassius's handwriting.

**Character:** This is the clearest window into Cassius in the whole play, and it shows a mind that is at once acute, resentful and self-aware. Acute, because his diagnosis of Brutus — noble, therefore malleable through his nobility — is exactly how Brutus will be moved. Resentful, because "Caesar doth bear me hard" and the imagined role-reversal betray personal wounds beneath the republican speeches. Self-aware, because he never pretends to himself that the letters are honest. Whether he is simply a cynic is a different question, and the text leaves it open. Many critics read the soliloquy as proof that the conspiracy is founded on fraud; others point to the final line, "or worse days endure", as a sign that Cassius genuinely believes Rome's alternative is worse — a man can be a manipulator and a patriot at once. What you cannot do is read this speech and still call Cassius a straightforward idealist. For Brutus, the speech is a verdict delivered in his absence: he is a man whose virtue can be used as a handle.

**Theme:** Manipulation and the ethics of persuasion; honour as a weakness as well as a strength; appearance versus reality (anonymous letters that are not what they seem); friendship and its betrayal (Cassius trades on Brutus's trust); jealousy (Caesar's love for Brutus); the manufacture of public opinion.

**Device:** Soliloquy — the convention by which a character's private mind is made audible; in this play Shakespeare uses it sparingly, which makes each one weigh more. Apostrophe — Cassius addresses the absent Brutus directly — "thou art noble" — in the familiar form that sounds intimate and is here faintly predatory. Metaphor of metalwork in "mettle may be wrought", with the pun on 'metal'; metaphor of seduction in "seduced", which casts the political persuasion as a courtship; a riding or sitting image in "seat him sure". Rhetorical question ("who so firm…?"). Antithesis in "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus". The closing rhyming couplet ("sure" / "endure") is the Elizabethan signal that a scene is ending, and the hard rhyme gives the threat the sound of a decision already taken. Finally, dramatic irony of the deepest kind: for the rest of the play the audience knows what Brutus does not — that the letters he will read in Act 2 were written here.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch's Life of Brutus reports that anonymous messages appeared in Rome urging Brutus to act: on the statue of his ancestor Lucius Junius Brutus, who had driven out the last king in 509 BC, and on the praetor's tribunal where Brutus sat as urban praetor in 44 BC, with words to the effect of 'Brutus, are you asleep?' and 'You are no true Brutus'. Plutarch presents these as the work of citizens who wanted Brutus to lead; it is Shakespeare who makes Cassius their forger. That change is the whole point of the soliloquy, and you should be able to name it. HISTORICAL RECORD also explains "bear me hard": in 44 BC both men were praetors, but Brutus received the more prestigious urban praetorship, and the ancient sources treat Cassius's disappointment as a real grievance. "Several hands" would have been a practical detail for an Elizabethan audience used to letters as the main medium of political intrigue — forged correspondence was a known weapon in the politics of the 1590s, though you should not claim that Shakespeare had any particular case in mind.

**Scene connection:** Backwards, the soliloquy explains the scene you have just watched: the mirror image ("I, your glass"), the Tiber story and the Colossus were not spontaneous but the opening moves of the man who now plans the next ones. Forwards, it is the hinge of the whole conspiracy plot. Scene 3 shows the letters handed to Cinna ("look you lay it in the praetor's chair… throw this / In at his window; set this up with wax / Upon old Brutus' statue") — the same three delivery points that Plutarch gives for the real messages. Act 2 Scene 1 shows a letter read and misread by Brutus. And the question Cassius raises — whether honourable men can be honourably persuaded by a lie — hangs over Brutus's claim in Act 2 and Act 3 that the conspirators act purely, and over the quarrel between the two men in Act 4. Caesar's judgement in Extract 1, that Cassius "thinks too much", is here confirmed by the thinking itself.

**Keywords:** soliloquy, convention, manipulation, forgery, malleable, exploit, grievance, self-aware, cynical, idealist, dramatic irony, couplet, pretext.

::: insight Why the only soliloquy in Act 1 belongs to Cassius
Shakespeare could have given Brutus the first soliloquy — the troubled man "with himself at war" is the obvious candidate, and he gets one at the start of Act 2. Instead the first private voice the audience hears is the manipulator's, and the choice shapes how you watch everything after it. Once you have heard Cassius plan the letters, you cannot see Brutus read one in his orchard without knowing it is forged; Brutus's most solemn moment of decision is, for the audience, a moment of being deceived. That is dramatic irony used not for a single laugh or shock but as a structural condition of the play. A second effect concerns sympathy. Soliloquy usually draws an audience towards a character — we are trusted with his secrets — and yet the secret here is a plan to deceive a friend. The result is a peculiar double feeling that the whole tragedy depends on: we are inside Cassius's intelligence and outside his morality at once. Weak answers call the speech 'evil'. Strong answers notice that it is lucid, that its diagnosis of Brutus is correct, and that its last line admits fear of "worse days" — and then argue about what that mixture means. An examiner marking a [4] question on this passage is rewarding exactly that willingness to hold two judgements together with evidence for each.
:::

## Extract 5 — Casca and Cicero in the Storm: "men may construe things after their fashion"

::: extract Act 1 Scene 3 — Casca to Cicero, in a street at night, with thunder and lightning
> **Casca:** Are not you moved, when all the sway of earth
> Shakes like a thing unfirm? O Cicero,
> I have seen tempests when the scolding winds
> Have rived the knotty oaks, and I have seen
> Th' ambitious ocean swell and rage and foam
> To be exalted with the threat'ning clouds;
> But never till tonight, never till now,
> Did I go through a tempest dropping fire.
> Either there is a civil strife in heaven,
> Or else the world, too saucy with the gods,
> Incenses them to send destruction.
> […]
> **Cicero:** Indeed, it is a strange-disposèd time.
> But men may construe things after their fashion,
> Clean from the purpose of the things themselves.
:::

**Context:** The opening of Act 1 Scene 3. The stage direction calls for thunder and lightning, and the scene begins with Cicero meeting Casca in the street and asking two questions: whether he has brought Caesar home, and why he is breathless and staring. Casca's reply is the first speech of the extract. A month has passed since Scene 2 without the play saying so — Cicero's question a few lines later, "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?", fixes this as the night before the ides of March.

**Speaker:** Casca, then Cicero. Notice at once that Casca speaks verse. In Scene 2 he was the only noble who spoke prose — flat, mocking, refusing to be impressed. Here, in the storm, he speaks in elevated blank verse full of personification and alliteration. The text does not explain the change; many critics read it as fear stripping away his pose, others as Shakespeare using the register the scene needs regardless of consistency. Either way, you should notice it and be ready to comment on it.

**Listener:** Cicero — Rome's greatest orator and a man of learning, who in Scene 2 was reported to have spoken Greek at the games and to have looked at Caesar with "ferret and such fiery eyes". He is the only character in Act 1 who hears a list of omens and declines to interpret it. He leaves before Cassius arrives and plays no further part in the conspiracy; when Cassius later proposes recruiting him in Act 2, Brutus will refuse.

**Before:** Scene 2 ended with Cassius's soliloquy and the plan of the forged letters (Extract 4). Between the scenes, offstage and unmentioned, the letters have presumably begun their work — by the end of this scene Cassius will say that Brutus is "Three parts" won. Casca's bringing Caesar home tells us he has been in Caesar's company that evening, which is how he knows that Caesar has sent word to Cicero about tomorrow.

**After:** Cicero asks whether Casca has seen anything "more wonderful", and Casca gives his catalogue of prodigies: a slave whose left hand "did flame and burn / Like twenty torches joined" without being scorched; a lion met "Against the Capitol" which "went surly by" without harming him; a hundred terrified women who swore they saw "Men all in fire walk up and down the streets"; and "the bird of night" — the owl — sitting at noon in the market-place, "Hooting and shrieking". He insists that when such prodigies "conjointly meet" no one should say they are natural: "I believe they are portentous things / Unto the climate that they point upon." Cicero answers with the two lines that close the extract, asks about tomorrow, remarks that this "disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in", and goes. Cassius enters, and the scene turns into Extract 6.

**Meaning:** Casca asks Cicero whether he is not shaken when the whole established order of the earth ('sway' — rule, dominion, balance) shakes like something unsteady ('unfirm'). He has seen storms in which scolding winds split ('rived') the gnarled ('knotty') oaks, and he has seen the ocean, ambitious to climb, swell and rage and foam as if to be raised up ('exalted') to the level of the threatening clouds; but never until tonight has he walked through a storm that rains fire. He offers two explanations, both supernatural: either the gods are fighting one another — "a civil strife in heaven" — or the world has grown so insolent ('saucy') towards the gods that it has provoked ('Incenses') them to send destruction. After the catalogue of omens, Cicero replies with careful courtesy: yes, it is a strangely ordered ('strange-disposèd') time; but men may interpret ('construe') things according to their own inclination ('after their fashion'), completely apart from ('Clean from') what the things themselves actually mean ('the purpose of the things themselves').

**Subtext:** Casca's two explanations are both political metaphors wearing religious dress. "Civil strife in heaven" is civil war among the gods — and civil war is exactly what Rome has just lived through and is about to live through again. A world "too saucy with the gods" is a world in which a man has risen too high — and Cicero, who looked at Caesar with fiery eyes, does not need to be told who. Casca never names Caesar in this scene until Cassius forces him to, but his storm is already a political storm. Cicero's reply is the subtlest moment in Act 1, and you must get it right. He does not say the omens mean nothing; he does not say the time is ordinary — "Indeed, it is a strange-disposèd time" grants Casca his alarm. What he says is that the meaning men find in such things is their own: a frightened man reads a storm as a threat, an ambitious man as an opportunity, a conspirator as a summons. Within a hundred lines Cassius will prove him right by reading the same storm as a portrait of Caesar and a licence to act. Cicero's lines are therefore a warning placed exactly before the thing they warn against — and they are also, a strong reading suggests, Shakespeare's instruction to the audience about how to watch the rest of the play: everyone in it construes, and the construing is the tragedy.

**Character:** Casca here is frightened, superstitious and eloquent — a different man from the sneering prose-speaker of Scene 2, or the same man with the mask off. The fear is real: he has not put up his sword, he is breathless, he reasons from the omens to a coming catastrophe. Yet it is this frightened man whom Cassius, minutes later, will recruit by shaming him for his fear. Cicero is the detached intellectual: courteous, sceptical, unwilling to be hurried into meaning, and at the same time practical — his real interest is whether Caesar goes to the Capitol tomorrow. His scepticism is admirable and also, within the play, useless; he sees clearly and does nothing, and Shakespeare removes him from the stage before the conspiracy forms. Caesar, who never appears in the scene, is present in every image: the fire from the sky, the lion at the Capitol, the owl at noon.

**Theme:** Superstition and omens against reason and scepticism; fate and free will (do the prodigies foretell, or do men make them foretell?); appearance versus reality in its sharpest form — the gap between things and the meanings men give them; the disorder of nature mirroring disorder in the state; fear as the raw material of politics.

**Device:** Pathetic fallacy and the Elizabethan doctrine of correspondences — the storm in the heavens matches the coming storm in the state, a pattern Shakespeare uses again in Macbeth and King Lear. Personification throughout: "scolding winds", "Th' ambitious ocean", a sky that drops fire, gods who are incensed. Alliteration binds the pictures together — "swell and rage and foam", "Shakes like a thing unfirm". Hyperbole and accumulation in the catalogue of prodigies ('twenty torches', 'a hundred ghastly women'). Rhetorical question in the opening line. Antithesis in Cicero's couplet — things against the construing of things, their "purpose" against men's "fashion" — expressed as an aphorism, a compact general truth that stands apart from the scene and can be quoted alone. Dramatic irony: Cicero's warning against misconstruction is immediately followed by the play's great misconstruer, Cassius. And the shift from prose to verse in Casca is itself a device of register, if you choose to read it so.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch and other ancient writers list portents before Caesar's death: lights in the sky, strange noises at night, birds of ill omen in the Forum, and the memorable detail of men on fire who were not burned; Shakespeare's catalogue draws on this stock of prodigies and adds the lion. Cicero's historical reputation fits his lines here — he was Rome's most famous orator and a writer on philosophy, including a work on divination that treats the reading of omens with scepticism — though you should not claim that Shakespeare had read it. The real Cicero was not a conspirator; he welcomed Caesar's death but was not told of the plot in advance, and he was killed on the orders of the triumvirs in December 43 BC. The ELIZABETHAN CONTEXT also matters: audiences in 1599 lived with a widespread belief that disorder in the heavens signalled disorder in the state, and with real anxiety about the succession to an ageing, childless queen. Audiences may have felt the storm as more than scenery. The dating is Shakespeare's compression: Scene 3 is the night of 14 March 44 BC, and the Capitol to which Caesar is to come tomorrow is Shakespeare's relocation of an assassination that historically took place in the Curia of Pompey.

**Scene connection:** Backwards, Casca's fear completes the picture of him begun in Scene 2 — the cynic was never as untouched as he pretended — and Cicero's smiling, head-shaking Greek is here translated into plain Latin: he distrusts how people read things. Forwards, within the scene, Cassius will take the identical prodigies and construe them "after their fashion" as a description of Caesar (Extract 6). In Act 2 Scene 1 Brutus will read a forged letter and construe it as the voice of Rome; in Act 2 Scene 2 Calpurnia and Caesar will construe the same night's omens in opposite ways, and Decius Brutus will reinterpret Calpurnia's dream to lure Caesar out; in Act 3 Scene 2 Antony will construe Caesar's wounds for the crowd. Cicero's couplet is the key that opens every one of those scenes.

**Keywords:** omen, prodigy, portent, superstition, scepticism, construe, interpretation, pathetic fallacy, correspondences, aphorism, register, detachment, foreboding.

::: insight Cicero's two lines are the most useful sentence in Act 1 for a Class X candidate
No extract from this scene will appear in your board paper, but Cicero's remark will serve you in almost any Act 3–5 answer that touches interpretation, deception or the crowd — and most of them do. Consider how often the later play turns on someone construing: Brutus reading a forged letter as Rome's plea; Decius Brutus turning Calpurnia's dream of blood into a vision of Rome revived; Antony turning a corpse into an argument; the plebeians turning a poet named Cinna into a conspirator because of his name; Cassius, at Philippi, reading the birds over his army as a sign of doom. A candidate who can write 'Shakespeare has already warned us through Cicero in Act 1 that men may construe things after their fashion' and then show how a later character does exactly that has demonstrated two things evaluators prize: knowledge of the whole play, and a grasp of the design behind the plot. Be careful with the quotation. It is often misremembered as 'construe things after their own fashion' — the word 'own' is not there. Learn the two lines exactly, and learn where they come from: Cicero, to Casca, in the storm, the night before the ides. A misattributed quotation earns less than no quotation at all, because it tells the evaluator that the candidate is working from memory of a summary rather than from the text.

The scene takes place in a street in Rome, at night, during a violent storm of thunder and lightning. About a month has passed since the Lupercal of Scene 2: the audience learns this not from any announcement but from Cicero's question "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?", which fixes the night as the eve of the ides of March, the date the Soothsayer named. The link to the Soothsayer shows that the candidate sees the act as a whole.

::: model Model answer (ii)
Casca offers two supernatural explanations. The first is that there is "a civil strife in heaven" — the gods themselves are at war with one another, and the storm on earth is the overflow of their quarrel. The second is that the world has become "too saucy with the gods" — so insolent and presumptuous towards them — that it has provoked them into sending destruction as punishment. Applied to Rome, the first explanation mirrors the civil wars that have torn the Republic — Caesar against Pompey, and the new division now forming between Caesar's friends and his enemies — so that heaven's civil strife reflects Rome's. The second fits Caesar himself: a man who has risen above his fellow citizens, is treated as a god and is about to be offered a crown may be the 'sauciness' that has angered heaven — or, from Caesar's side, the insolence could be the plotting against him. Casca does not name Caesar, but both explanations point towards him.

**Why this answer earns marks:** Each explanation is paraphrased with its key word glossed, and each is then mapped onto Rome in a specific way, with the candidate noting that the second explanation can cut either way. That final caution shows the evaluator that the candidate is reading, not forcing, the political parallel.

::: model Model answer (iii)
Casca reports that a common slave held up his left hand, which "did flame and burn / Like twenty torches joined", yet the hand felt no fire and "remained unscorched"; that near the Capitol he met a lion, which "glazed upon me and went surly by" without attacking him; that a hundred terrified women, "Transformèd with their fear", swore they had seen "Men all in fire walk up and down the streets"; and that the previous day "the bird of night" — the owl — sat in the market-place at noon, "Hooting and shrieking". From the fact that these prodigies all occur together — "conjointly meet" — Casca concludes that no one should dismiss them as natural events with natural causes; he believes they are "portentous things", omens of disaster for the region or country ("the climate") towards which they point.

**Why this answer earns marks:** Four prodigies are described accurately (more than the three asked for), each with the detail that makes it strange, and the conclusion is given in Casca's own logic — the coincidence of the signs, not any single sign, is what convinces him. Glossing "the bird of night" and "climate" shows the candidate understands the words rather than copying them.

::: model Model answer (iv)
Cicero means that people interpret events according to their own temperament and wishes, and that the meaning they find may be entirely different from — "Clean from" — the true nature or purpose of the events themselves. A storm is simply a storm; a frightened man makes it a threat, a hopeful man a promise. His attitude differs from Casca's in two ways. Casca is certain that the prodigies are messages from the gods and reasons straight from them to coming destruction; Cicero grants that the time is strange — "Indeed, it is a strange-disposèd time" — but refuses to say what, if anything, it means. And where Casca is overwhelmed by fear, Cicero remains detached and practical, turning at once to the real question of the night: whether Caesar will come to the Capitol tomorrow.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The aphorism is explained in plain language with a concrete illustration, and the contrast is drawn on two axes — belief and temperament — each supported by a phrase from the extract. Separating 'what he thinks' from 'how he behaves' gives the answer the structure that makes three marks easy to award.

::: model Model answer (v)
As soon as Cicero has gone, Cassius enters and reads the identical storm in the opposite way. Where Casca sees "dreadful heralds" sent by the gods to make men "fear and tremble", Cassius calls it "A very pleasing night to honest men", claims to have bared his breast to the lightning, and then construes the whole night as a portrait of one man — a man who "thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars / As doth the lion in the Capitol" — so that the omens become an argument for killing Caesar. The same facts, a different fashion of construing: Cicero's warning is proved true within minutes. In my view Shakespeare wants the audience to hold both responses rather than choose. The omens are not empty — Caesar does die on the ides, and the Soothsayer is right — so Casca's fear is justified by events; but the play also shows, again and again, men bending signs to their own purposes, as Decius Brutus bends Calpurnia's dream in Act 2 and Antony bends Caesar's wounds in Act 3, so Cicero's scepticism about interpreters is justified too. The portents are real; the readings of them are human and fallible. That is why the play puts the two speeches side by side.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The first part answers 'how' with specific quotations from Cassius and a clear statement of the reversal. The second part takes a position that is argued rather than merely chosen, and supports it from two different acts of the play, which is exactly what 'with reference to what happens later' invites. A candidate who simply picked Casca or Cicero would earn less than one who explains why the play needs both.

## Extract 6 — Cassius Wins Casca: "Be factious for redress of all these griefs"

::: extract Act 1 Scene 3 — Cassius and Casca, in the storm, after Casca has agreed that every bondman can cancel his captivity
> **Cassius:** And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then?
> Poor man, I know he would not be a wolf
> But that he sees the Romans are but sheep;
> He were no lion, were not Romans hinds.
> Those that with haste will make a mighty fire
> Begin it with weak straws. What trash is Rome,
> What rubbish, and what offal when it serves
> For the base matter to illuminate
> So vile a thing as Caesar! […]
> **Casca:** You speak to Casca, and to such a man
> That is no fleering telltale. Hold. My hand.
> Be factious for redress of all these griefs,
> And I will set this foot of mine as far
> As who goes farthest.
:::

**Context:** Act 1 Scene 3, roughly its middle. Cicero has gone; Cassius has entered, identified Casca "by your voice", and spent some sixty lines turning the storm into an argument. The extract begins at the moment Cassius's argument reaches its sharpest point and ends with Casca's handshake. The lines between the two speeches — Cassius's pretended alarm at having spoken treason before "a willing bondman", and his declaration "But I am armed, / And dangers are to me indifferent" — are discussed below under **Meaning:**.

**Speaker:** Cassius, then Casca. This is the Cassius of the soliloquy now at work on his second victim, and because we have heard the soliloquy we can watch the method rather than be taken in by it.

**Listener:** Casca — the frightened man of Extract 5, whom Cassius has already told "You are dull, Casca" and accused of lacking "those sparks of life / That should be in a Roman". The shaming has prepared him: a man told that his fear is un-Roman will be eager to prove his courage.

**Before:** Cassius has told Casca that he walked bareheaded and unbuttoned ("unbracèd") through the storm and offered his chest to the lightning. He has then redirected the omens: heaven has made these monstrous things "instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state", and there is a man "Most like this dreadful night" who is "no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action, yet prodigious grown". Casca has guessed the name — "'Tis Caesar that you mean, is it not, Cassius?" — and reported the rumour that the senators mean tomorrow to make Caesar king everywhere "save here in Italy". Cassius has answered with the speech of the dagger: he knows where he will wear it, "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius", and no tower, wall, dungeon or chain "Can be retentive to the strength of spirit", for life can always "dismiss itself". Casca has agreed: "So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity."

**After:** Cassius declares "There's a bargain made" and tells Casca that he has already moved "Some certain of the noblest-minded Romans" to join an enterprise "Of honourable-dangerous consequence", and that they wait for him at "Pompey's Porch". Cinna arrives; Cassius gives him the forged papers to plant in "the praetor's chair", "In at his window" and "Upon old Brutus' statue"; and the scene ends with Casca's speech about Brutus — "that which would appear offence in us / His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness" — and Cassius's resolve to "be sure of him" before day.

**Meaning:** 'Then why should Caesar be a tyrant at all?' Cassius asks — if every man can free himself by his own hand, Caesar's power exists only because men allow it. 'Poor man, I know he would not be a wolf, except that he sees the Romans are nothing but sheep; he would be no lion if Romans were not deer ('hinds' — female deer, timid prey; the word could also mean peasants or servants, and both senses sting). Those who want to make a great fire quickly start it with weak straws. What trash Rome is, what rubbish and what refuse ('offal' — waste, the discarded parts of a carcass), when it serves as the base, worthless fuel ("base matter") to light up and make glorious ("illuminate") so vile a thing as Caesar!' In the omitted lines Cassius checks himself with a show of alarm: 'But O grief, where have you led me? Perhaps I am saying this in front of a man who is content to be a slave ("a willing bondman"); then I know I shall have to answer for it ("My answer must be made"). But I am armed, and dangers mean nothing to me.' Casca replies: 'You are speaking to Casca, and to the kind of man who is no sneering ("fleering" — grinning, mocking) tell-tale. Here — my hand. Form a party ("Be factious" — be active in raising a faction) to put right ("redress") all these grievances, and I will go as far as the man who goes farthest.'

**Subtext:** The argument has a hidden pivot. Up to this point Cassius has attacked Caesar; here he turns and attacks Rome — and, by implication, Casca. Caesar is only a wolf because the Romans are sheep; he is only a lion because Romans are deer; Rome is trash, rubbish, offal. The insult is the recruitment. If Casca is not to be one of the sheep, he must do something, and the only something on offer is the conspiracy. The fire image carries a second, quieter meaning: Caesar's blaze was kindled with "weak straws", that is, with the acquiescence of worthless men — but Cassius is also, at this very moment, kindling a fire of his own, and he too begins with the weak material to hand, a frightened man in a storm. Then the pretended retreat: "But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?" — as if passion had carried him into treason unawares. Nothing in Cassius is unaware. The feigned slip is a test, and it is also bait: it offers Casca the dignity of reassuring a brave man. "But I am armed" closes the trap — Cassius dares Casca to betray him, which no Roman who has just been called a sheep can now do. Casca's reply gives Cassius exactly what the trap was built to extract: not merely silence ("no fleering telltale") but commitment — the hand, and the promise to go "As who goes farthest". Casca does not ask what the enterprise is. He has joined it before it has been described.

**Character:** Cassius: the master persuader in his most aggressive mode — contemptuous, theatrical, calculating. With Brutus in Scene 2 he flattered and reasoned; with Casca he shames and dares. The difference tells you that Cassius tailors his method to the man, which is the mark of the true manipulator. Yet his contempt for Rome — "What trash is Rome" — sounds too furious to be entirely tactical; many readers hear real disgust, and the line raises a question the play never settles: does Cassius love Rome, or only hate Caesar's Rome? Casca: proud, impulsive, more courageous than his fear suggested, and politically shallow — he pledges himself to go farthest without asking where. His speech has the directness of a soldier ("Hold. My hand."), and history confirms the portrait: it was Casca who struck the first blow on the ides. Caesar, absent again, is described as a "Poor man" — a sarcasm that nonetheless admits something true: Caesar's power is a relationship, not a possession, and it depends on the people who consent to it.

**Theme:** Tyranny and its causes — the ruled as much as the ruler; liberty and servitude ("bondman", "yoke", "captivity"); manipulation and persuasion; honour and manliness as levers of politics; public opinion (Rome as "base matter"); conspiracy as a bargain between men.

**Device:** Animal imagery — the wolf and sheep, the lion and hinds — a pair of antitheses that reduce the political question to predator and prey, and that answer Casca's own lion at the Capitol in Extract 5. Extended metaphor of fire: Caesar's greatness is a blaze, Rome the "weak straws" and "base matter" that fuel it; the metaphor is also an unwitting self-portrait of the conspiracy being lit before our eyes. Accumulation and rhetorical escalation in "What trash… / What rubbish, and what offal", with the climax "So vile a thing as Caesar!" Rhetorical question in the opening line. Apostrophe and feigned self-interruption — "But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?" — a calculated pause that is itself a persuasive device. Sarcasm in "Poor man". Alliteration in "fleering telltale"; the heavily stressed monosyllables of "Hold. My hand." mark the gesture of the oath. And a stage picture: two men clasping hands in a lightning storm, which the audience reads without a word being needed.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Publius Servilius Casca Longus was a real conspirator and, according to the ancient sources, the first to strike Caesar; he became tribune in 43 BC and died after Philippi. The rumour Casca has just reported — that Caesar was to be given the title of king everywhere outside Italy — appears in the ancient biographers as talk circulating in Rome before the ides; whether any such proposal was really to be put to the Senate is uncertain, and Shakespeare treats it as hearsay ("they say"). "Pompey's Porch" is the portico of the Theatre of Pompey, built in 55 BC, part of the complex in whose Senate-house Caesar was actually killed — so the conspirators in the play meet, unknowingly, at the real scene of the crime, which Shakespeare has moved to the Capitol. The historical Cassius was an Epicurean, a philosophy sceptical of divine signs; the Cassius of this scene reads the storm as a message, though he does so for Casca's benefit rather than his own. Cassius's claim that "life, being weary of these worldly bars, / Never lacks power to dismiss itself" reflects the Stoic admiration for suicide as the final freedom — LITERARY INTERPRETATION notes that it also quietly foretells how both he and Brutus will die after Philippi in 42 BC.

**Scene connection:** Backwards, the wolf-and-sheep argument is the Colossus argument of Scene 2 re-made for a cruder listener: there Cassius said "we petty men / Walk under his huge legs"; here the petty men are sheep and deer. "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves" is the philosophical form of what "the Romans are but sheep" says with contempt. Forwards, Casca's handshake and his promise to go farthest are honoured in Act 3 Scene 1, where he strikes first; and Cassius's confession that he may have spoken before "a willing bondman" is echoed in the conspirators' later fear of betrayal. The alchemy speech that follows this extract — Brutus's reputation turning the conspiracy's "offence" into "virtue" — states the reason Brutus is needed and the fatal assumption behind the whole plot: that a good name can make a killing good. Antony will test that assumption to destruction in Act 3 Scene 2.

**Keywords:** tyranny, servility, bondman, predator and prey, animal imagery, fire metaphor, base matter, feigned, bait, recruitment, impulsive, bargain, alchemy, reputation.

::: model Model answer (i)
Cicero has just left the stage, having told Casca that the "disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in". Cassius has argued that a man can always free himself from tyranny by his own hand, since no prison "Can be retentive to the strength of spirit" and life can dismiss itself at will; Casca has agreed that "every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity" — every slave carries in his own hand the means to end his slavery. Casca has reported the rumour that the senators mean tomorrow to make Caesar a king, so that he will wear his crown "by sea and land / In every place save here in Italy".

**Why this answer earns marks:** Three separate facts are required and all three are given, each anchored in the text; the paraphrase of the "bondman" line shows understanding of the argument Casca has accepted, and the rumour is reported with its crucial qualification about Italy. Nothing is vague.
:::

::: model Model answer (ii)
Cassius says, with heavy sarcasm in "Poor man", that Caesar would never behave like a wolf — a predator preying on the city — if he did not see that the Romans behave like sheep, timid and obedient; and he would be no lion if the Romans were not deer, creatures made to be hunted. In other words, the cause of Caesar's power is not Caesar's strength but the Romans' weakness. A tyrant exists only because his subjects consent to be ruled; take away the sheep and there is nothing for the wolf to be. This is the practical conclusion of the argument Casca has just accepted — that every bondman can cancel his own captivity — turned into an accusation against the Romans, and by implication against Casca himself.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The two animal pairs are unpacked separately and the tone ("Poor man") is noticed, which shows attention to how the lines are spoken, not only what they say. The second part states Cassius's thesis in one clear sentence and connects it to the preceding argument, demonstrating that the candidate follows the logic of the whole speech.
:::

::: model Model answer (iii)
The fire image means that anyone who wants to build a great blaze quickly starts it with weak, worthless material — dry straw catches at once. Applied to Rome, Caesar's greatness is the "mighty fire", and the Romans are the "weak straws" whose easy compliance has set it alight. Cassius then intensifies the image: Rome is "trash", "rubbish" and "what offal" — waste, refuse, the discarded parts of a butchered animal — because it has let itself be used as "base matter", cheap fuel, whose only function is to make Caesar shine. He insults Rome rather than Caesar at this point because his purpose is recruitment, not analysis: abusing Caesar would merely invite agreement, whereas abusing Rome forces Casca to decide whether he is one of the sheep, the straws, the rubbish. The insult leaves Casca only one way to prove that he is not — to act.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The image is explained first literally and then in its application, the three nouns are glossed rather than repeated, and the final part answers a 'why' question with Cassius's strategy, supported by the effect the insult has in the scene. Reading the rhetoric as a tactic is the mark of a Level 3 answer.
:::

::: model Model answer (iv)
Cassius exclaims "But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?" as though his passion had carried him into treason without his noticing, and says that perhaps he has spoken "Before a willing bondman" — a man content with slavery — in which case he knows he will have to answer for his words; but he is armed, and "dangers are to me indifferent". He hopes to gain two things. The pretended slip tests Casca — if Casca were likely to betray him, his reaction would show it now — and it dares him: a man who has just been called a sheep cannot bear to be taken for a "willing bondman", and so is pushed to prove his courage. Casca's reply shows the tactic has worked completely: he protests that he is "no fleering telltale", offers his hand as a pledge, and promises to go "As who goes farthest" in redressing Rome's grievances — all before Cassius has told him what the enterprise is.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The omitted lines are reported accurately and in order, the purpose is analysed on two levels (test and dare), and Casca's response is read not merely as agreement but as proof of the specific effect Cassius intended, with the telling observation that Casca commits himself blind. Each part earns its mark visibly.
:::

::: model Model answer (v)
Casca means that Brutus is so loved and respected by the people — "he sits high in all the people's hearts" — that his support, his very face or approval ("countenance"), will transform the conspiracy in the public eye. What would look like a crime ("offence") if the others did it alone will, with Brutus's name attached, appear as virtue and worthiness, just as the alchemist was believed to turn base metal into gold. The image admits that the deed is base; only Brutus can gild it. In my view the conspirators are wrong to rely on this, and the play says so. Brutus's reputation does protect them briefly — the crowd in Act 3 Scene 2 is ready to honour him after his speech — but the same reputation leads Brutus to insist on sparing Antony and letting him speak, and Antony turns the "honourable" Brutus into a byword for betrayal within a single oration. Reputation can change how a deed looks; it cannot change what the deed is, and once Antony shows the crowd Caesar's body, no alchemy holds. Casca's image, meant as reassurance, is in fact the conspiracy's fatal miscalculation stated in advance.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The image is explained with its key words glossed and its implication drawn out (the deed is admitted to be base). The opinion is clear, and the reason is argued from the course of the play with specific reference to Act 3, acknowledging the short-term truth of Casca's claim before showing its long-term failure. That is an evaluative answer anchored in evidence, which is what the [4] demands.

## Using the Six Extracts Together

You have now worked through the six passages that carry the second half of Scene 2 and the whole of Scene 3. Before you close the chapter, see them as one design rather than six exercises, because the questions that earn most — the [4] questions here, and the Act 3–5 questions in the board paper that reach back to Act 1 — reward exactly that kind of seeing.

```chain
Extract 1 — Caesar reads Cassius correctly and refuses to act on it
↓
Extract 2 — Casca reads Caesar's refusals as a performance ("to my thinking")
↓
Extract 3 — Cassius reads Caesar's fit as Rome's disease; the tribunes are silenced
↓
Extract 4 — Cassius, alone, reads Brutus as metal to be wrought and plans the letters
↓
Extract 5 — Casca reads the storm as doom; Cicero warns that men construe after their fashion
↓
Extract 6 — Cassius reads the storm as Caesar, Rome as sheep, and wins Casca's hand
```

Every link in that chain is an act of interpretation, and most of them are wrong, biased or self-serving. That is not an accident of the extracts I chose; it is the subject of these two scenes. Caesar misreads his own safety; Casca misreads, or at least slants, the crown; Cassius deliberately misreads everything for others while reading Brutus with perfect accuracy for himself; and Cicero, the one man who names the problem, walks off into the night. When a question asks you what Act 1 'prepares' or 'foreshadows', this is the deepest honest answer: it prepares a play in which the characters will keep construing — a letter, a dream, a corpse, a name, a flight of birds — and will keep dying of their constructions.

::: icse Three habits to carry out of this chapter
First, the fact-opinion split. Whenever a passage is reported rather than shown — the crown, the fit, the Greek, the senators' plan for a crown outside Italy — write 'according to Casca' or 'Casca says' before the fact and 'in Casca's view' before the opinion. It costs you four words and it is the single most reliable signal of a careful reader that an evaluator can receive. Second, the soliloquy test. When you are asked what a character 'really' thinks or wants, go first to any soliloquy — in Act 1 that means Cassius's — and quote it, because a soliloquy is the one kind of evidence whose sincerity the convention guarantees. Third, the construing sentence. Learn Cicero's two lines exactly, know who says them to whom and when, and practise attaching them to a later scene in one sentence. None of this guarantees a particular question; all of it raises the quality of whatever answer you write, in Class IX on Act 1 itself and in Class X on the acts that Act 1 makes possible.
:::
