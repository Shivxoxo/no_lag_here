# Caesar in Act 1 — A Character Masterclass

*What this chapter does: It takes the one character everybody thinks they understand — Caesar, the man who gives the play its name — and shows you how little of him you have actually been shown, and how much Shakespeare makes you infer. We examine seventeen characteristics one by one, each with its evidence, its explanation, the counter-evidence that complicates it, and its dramatic purpose. Then we look at Caesar from three angles, work through the idea of his 'two bodies', trace every Act 1 seed into Acts 2 and 3, and finally learn to write about him in examination English that never falls back on the word 'arrogant'.*

## Before We Begin: How to Read a Character Who Barely Speaks

Here is a fact that surprises most students when they count. In the whole of Act 1 Caesar speaks fewer than forty lines. All of them fall in Scene 2. He is absent from Scene 1 and from Scene 3, and he is never given a soliloquy — not here, and not anywhere in the play; the three lines he speaks alone at the opening of Act 2 Scene 2, before he calls his servant, are the nearest thing to one. Yet ask anyone what Act 1 is about and they will answer 'Caesar'. The tribunes in Scene 1 set out to strip his decorations from the statues before we have seen his face. Cassius spends the long middle of Scene 2 building a hostile biography of him. The storm of Scene 3 is read, by Cassius, as a portrait of him. Caesar is the subject of almost every sentence in the act and the speaker of very few.

This tells you how to read him. Most of what you 'know' about Caesar in Act 1 reaches you second-hand, through witnesses who have reasons to shade the truth: two tribunes loyal to the memory of Pompey; a rival who once carried Caesar out of a river and has never forgiven him for it; a reporter whom Cassius himself describes as speaking "after his sour fashion". Only in one short passage — Caesar's return from the games, when he turns to Antony and anatomises Cassius (dissects him, part by part) — do we hear him think aloud. Everything else is ceremony, command or rumour.

So the discipline of this chapter is to keep three kinds of knowledge apart and to say which one you are using. There is what Caesar himself says and does (direct SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT). There is what others say about him (mediated testimony, whose bias you must weigh). And there is what you conclude from the gap between the two (LITERARY INTERPRETATION, which must be argued, never merely asserted). An examiner rewards the student who can tell these apart. A weaker student writes 'Caesar is weak because he has epilepsy'. A stronger one writes 'Casca, a hostile witness, reports a swoon; Brutus, a sympathetic one, gives it a medical name; Cassius turns the name into a political metaphor'. That is one fact handled three ways, and the handling is where the marks are.

::: insight Why Shakespeare gives Caesar so few lines yet lets him dominate Act 1
LITERARY INTERPRETATION. Power in this play is measured not by how much a man speaks but by how much is spoken about him. Shakespeare builds Caesar the way the Romans of the play experience him: as a name before a body, a rumour before a voice. Scene 1 quarrels over "Caesar's trophies" and "Caesar's images" while the man is elsewhere; when he finally enters, a single word from him — his wife's name — is enough to stop a festival, and Casca has to hush the crowd so that Caesar can be heard at all. The rationing is deliberate. A character who talks constantly becomes familiar, and familiarity shrinks a man; Caesar has to stay large enough to fill Cassius's image of a Colossus. By giving him few lines Shakespeare also makes each one disproportionately heavy, so that "for always I am Caesar" and "this ear is deaf", in consecutive lines, can carry an entire theory of the man. There is a further effect. Because we hear so much about Caesar from his enemies before he says anything of substance, we arrive at his one real speech already primed to look down on him — and the speech quietly overturns the priming, for the supposed dotard (a feeble old man) reads Cassius with a precision nobody else in the act can match. Shakespeare gives Caesar so few lines because the play is about the space between a name and a man, and that space is exactly where the conspirators do their work.
:::

## The Method in Miniature: Five Levels on One Speech

Before the trait-by-trait work, let me show you the whole method on the single passage where Caesar thinks aloud. He has just returned from the games. Brutus notes that "The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow" and that his followers "look like a chidden train" ('chidden' means scolded — the whole entourage looks as if it has been told off). Caesar calls Antony to him.

::: quote Caesar reads Cassius
> **Caesar:** Let me have men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights. / Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous.
> **Antony:** Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given.
> **Caesar:** Would he were fatter! But I fear him not. / Yet if my name were liable to fear, / I do not know the man I should avoid / So soon as that spare Cassius.
:::

Difficult words first, because the speech turns on them. 'Sleek-headed' means smooth-haired, well-groomed, and by extension untroubled. 'A-nights' means at night. 'Yond' is 'that one over there'. 'Well given' means well-disposed, of good character. 'Liable to' means open to or subject to. 'Spare' means thin. The speech continues: Cassius "reads much", is "a great observer", looks "Quite through the deeds of men", loves no plays, hears no music, and "Seldom he smiles, and smiles in such a sort / As if he mocked himself and scorned his spirit / That could be moved to smile at anything." Then the conclusion: "Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves, / And therefore are they very dangerous." And the coda: "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar. / Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him."

::: level LEVEL 1 — What happened?
Caesar, returning from the games in a bad temper, tells Antony he distrusts Cassius because of his lean, brooding look. Antony says Cassius is harmless. Caesar disagrees, describes Cassius's habits in detail, insists that he personally fears nobody, and then draws Antony to his right side — because his left ear is deaf — and asks him privately for his honest opinion of Cassius.
:::

::: level LEVEL 2 — What does it mean?
The fat/lean opposition is a theory of politics. Fat, sleek, well-slept men are contented; they have appetite for food, not for power. The lean man is hungry in every sense. Caesar's portrait of the dangerous man is precise: he reads (he is educated in ideas), he observes (he is watching you), he sees through deeds to motives, he takes no pleasure in plays or music (he cannot be distracted or softened), and when he smiles it is at himself, bitterly. Above all, he "be never at heart's ease" while anyone is above him. In one word: envy. Caesar is diagnosing the psychology of a conspirator before there is a conspiracy.
:::

::: level LEVEL 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way?
Placement is everything. We have just watched Cassius work on Brutus for over a hundred lines, so every clause of Caesar's diagnosis lands on the audience as already proven: this is dramatic irony (the audience knowing more than a character) turned inside out, because here the character knows almost as much as we do and still does nothing. Shakespeare also changes his source. HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch reports Caesar saying he did not fear fat, long-haired men but pale, thin ones — meaning Brutus and Cassius together. Shakespeare narrows the remark to Cassius alone and leaves Brutus out. That single edit gives Caesar a blind spot exactly the shape of the man who will strike the deepest blow. Finally, the movement from "I fear him not" to "this ear is deaf" in the space of two lines is a small masterpiece of construction: the boast and the frailty are stitched together so that neither can be heard without the other.
:::

::: level LEVEL 4 — How does it connect to the whole play?
In Act 2 Scene 2 Caesar will fail to read Decius Brutus, who flatters him into going to the Capitol; in Act 3 Scene 1 he will fail to read the kneeling conspirators around him. The one man he reads perfectly in Act 1 is the one who is not deceiving him at all — Cassius is openly lean and hungry. Antony's misjudgement here ("he's not dangerous") is mirrored by Brutus's misjudgement of Antony in Act 2 Scene 1, where the conspirators decide he is no threat. And Cassius's "lean and hungry look" becomes the play's permanent image of envious intelligence, against which Brutus's honourable slowness is measured.
:::

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE. Be honest with yourself about the syllabus: Act 1 is the Class IX portion, and the Class X board paper examines Acts III–V. You should therefore not expect an extract from this speech in the board paper. But it is one of the most useful Act 1 passages for Class X answers, because any question on Caesar's fall in Act 3 Scene 1 — why he ignores warnings, whether he is blind or wilful — can be sharpened with a sentence like: 'Caesar is not blind; in Act 1 he reads Cassius exactly, calling him one who "looks / Quite through the deeds of men", and his tragedy is that his insight never becomes action because "always I am Caesar" forbids him to act on fear.' In a Class IX extract question on this passage, the marks lie in explaining the fat/lean contrast, identifying what Caesar gets right about Cassius, and noticing the deaf ear.
:::

## Caesar Trait by Trait

Now the systematic work. Each characteristic below is examined under four fixed headings. **Textual evidence** gives you the words to quote. **Explanation** tells you what they mean and why they matter. **Contradiction** gives the counter-evidence or the tension — because a character trait in Shakespeare is never a flat fact; it is always in argument with something. **Dramatic significance** tells you what Shakespeare is doing with the trait for the play as a whole.

### Authority

**Textual evidence:** His first word is a name — "Calpurnia" — and it is enough to stop a festival: "Peace, ho! Caesar speaks." Everything that follows is a command: "Stand you directly in Antonius' way", "Forget not in your speed, Antonius", "Set on and leave no ceremony out", "Set him before me. Let me see his face", "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." Antony gives the formula of that authority in a single line: "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed."

**Explanation:** Caesar's authority is grammatical before it is political. He speaks almost entirely in the imperative mood — the form of the verb that gives orders — and the few questions he asks ("Ha! Who calls?", "What man is that?") are demands for information, not requests. Notice how he names people. He calls his friend "Antonius", the full Latin form, where the stage directions and most other speakers say 'Antony': in Caesar's mouth even a friend's name becomes formal. The ceremonial trumpets that frame his appearances — the 'flourish' (a fanfare announcing a person of rank) and the 'sennet' (a longer trumpet signal for a procession's entry or exit) — wrap his words in sound designed to signal power. And his authority is enacted through other people. He does not hush the crowd; Casca does it for him, twice ("Bid every noise be still. Peace, yet again!"). A man of real power does not raise his own voice.

**Contradiction:** The same passage shows the limits of that authority. Caesar cannot control who speaks to him. A nameless Soothsayer's voice, "shriller than all the music", cuts through the ceremony, and Caesar has to halt the whole procession to deal with it. Authority that needs Casca to do the shushing is authority that depends on staff. Meanwhile Scene 1 has shown us two tribunes stripping his decorations from statues in his absence: his authority, like his name, is being contested where he cannot see it. By the end of Scene 2 Casca reports that Marullus and Flavius "are put to silence" — which restores the authority but exposes its method. Authority in this Rome works by silencing.

**Dramatic significance:** Shakespeare needs the audience to feel, within a dozen lines, exactly the Caesar the tribunes feared in Scene 1 — the man who would "soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness". The clipped imperatives achieve that. But by making the authority so visibly staged — trumpets, hushing, ritual — he also plants the question the whole act pursues: is this power, or the performance of power? Cassius will later insist that the man is "no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action", and the audience, having watched authority manufactured by trumpets and by Casca's voice, has already been handed reasons to wonder.

### Confidence

**Textual evidence:** Told twice to "Beware the ides of March", Caesar's verdict is instant: "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." Warned about Cassius, he insists: "Would he were fatter! But I fear him not." And he grounds that fearlessness in his own name: "for always I am Caesar."

**Explanation:** Confidence in Caesar is not loudness; it is speed. He hears the prophecy, looks at the face, hears it again and disposes of it in nine words. 'Dreamer' is a precise insult — it labels the Soothsayer a man who sees things that are not there — and "Let us leave him. Pass." is the whole procession ordered forward as if nothing had happened. The confidence has a theory behind it. 'Caesar' is treated as a condition rather than a name, and a condition of that kind cannot be "liable to fear". This is what critics mean when they say that Caesar has begun to believe his own legend: the man has adopted the public estimate of himself as a private certainty.

**Contradiction:** Look at what he actually does with the Soothsayer. A genuinely indifferent man would not stop. Caesar halts the procession, demands "Set him before me. Let me see his face", and asks to hear the warning a second time: "What sayst thou to me now? Speak once again." That is four lines of close attention paid to a man he then calls a dreamer. The Cassius speech follows the same pattern: he says "I fear him not" and then spends seventeen lines describing, with great care, exactly why Cassius ought to be feared. Confidence that talks this much about the thing it is not afraid of begins to look like its opposite. The text does not settle which it is; it lets the boast and the behaviour stand side by side.

**Dramatic significance:** The gap between the confident sentence and the anxious behaviour is Shakespeare's engine of dramatic irony throughout Caesar's part. The audience knows that the ides of March will matter — the history was famous — so every dismissal is heard twice, once as Caesar means it and once as the audience knows it. Act 1 also teaches the rhythm that Act 2 Scene 2 and Act 3 Scene 1 will repeat with mounting dread: a warning is attended to, weighed, and overridden. By the time Caesar overrides Calpurnia's dream and then brushes aside Artemidorus's letter at the Capitol, we have already watched him do it once, and we know what the pattern costs.

::: keyword Terms you will need for this chapter
**Imperative mood** — the verb form used for commands ("Set on and leave no ceremony out"). **Third-person self-reference** — a speaker naming himself as if describing someone else ("Caesar is turned to hear"). **Public persona** — the version of himself a person presents to others, as distinct from the private self. **Self-mythologising** — building a legend about oneself and then living inside it. **Dramatic irony** — when the audience knows something a character does not. **Mediated testimony** — information that reaches us through a witness rather than directly, and which therefore carries that witness's bias. **Hubris** — a Greek critical term for the overconfidence that invites a fall; use it sparingly and only with evidence.
:::

### Pride

**Textual evidence:** "Speak. Caesar is turned to hear." "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar." Cassius's caricature (a mocking, exaggerated portrait) of the same quality: "that tongue of his that bade the Romans / Mark him and write his speeches in their books".

**Explanation:** The clearest signature of Caesar's pride is that he speaks of himself in the third person, like a chronicler recording the movements of a great man. This is not simple pomposity. He is announcing that 'Caesar' is a public office he occupies and a standard he must maintain. The Roman word for what he is protecting is dignitas — the standing a man had earned and was expected to defend — and HISTORICAL RECORD suggests the real Caesar took it with deadly seriousness: in his own account of the civil war he presented the defence of his dignitas as a reason for fighting. Pride here, then, is a Roman virtue pressed to its dangerous edge. What Cassius mocks — the tongue that told Romans to write his speeches down — is a man who knows he is already history and behaves accordingly.

**Contradiction:** Pride and courtesy coexist in the same scene. Caesar's handling of the Soothsayer is oddly gentle for a supposed tyrant: he asks to see the man's face, hears him twice, and calls him a dreamer rather than a traitor. And Casca's report tells us that after the swoon Caesar apologised to the crowd — "he desired their Worships to think it was his infirmity" — a proud man asking working people to excuse him and addressing them by an honorific (a title of respect). Pride in Caesar is a public posture; the man who has just fainted in the marketplace is capable of humility, or at least of performing it convincingly. Casca's contempt for the wenches who forgave him should not blind you to the fact that the apology worked.

**Dramatic significance:** Shakespeare makes the pride audible in grammar so that the audience can hear it grow. In Act 1 the habit appears only once in its pure form — "Caesar is turned to hear" — with "always I am Caesar" as its first-person cousin, where the 'I' is swallowed at once by the name; in Act 2 Scene 2 and Act 3 Scene 1 it thickens until Caesar seems to speak almost entirely as 'Caesar', and his last words in Act 3 Scene 1 name himself rather than say 'I'. Act 1 is where the habit is planted quietly enough to be noticed but not yet to be absurd. That restraint matters: if Caesar were ridiculous here, his death could not be tragic.

::: insight Why Caesar speaks of himself in the third person
HISTORICAL RECORD first. The real Caesar wrote his account of the wars in Gaul in the third person, referring to himself as 'Caesar' throughout, as though a neutral observer were recording his deeds. Whether or not Shakespeare knew this, he gives his Caesar the same habit. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: the habit does three things at once. First, it splits the man from the name. When Caesar says "Caesar is turned to hear", the speaker reports on 'Caesar' as an institution — and the whole tragedy lives in that split, because the conspirators will kill the man in the belief that they are abolishing the institution. Second, it is self-mythologising: the speaker steps into the position of posterity (future generations) and describes himself as future generations will. Third — and this is the point students miss — it is a defence. "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar" uses the name as a shield: fear can be discussed as a topic because 'Caesar', by definition, does not experience it. The third person is where Caesar goes to hide. Notice that even in "always I am Caesar" the 'I' is absorbed at once into the name, whereas the one weakness he confesses directly, "this ear is deaf", is left in plain first person with no name attached: the body is 'I', the legend is 'Caesar'. When you write about this, do not say Caesar is 'arrogant'; say that he has adopted the public estimate of himself as a private identity, and that the grammar shows it.
:::

### Superstition

**Textual evidence:** "Forget not in your speed, Antonius, / To touch Calpurnia, for our elders say / The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse." "Set on and leave no ceremony out." Against these: "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass."

**Explanation:** Caesar's first substantive act on stage is a fertility rite. At the Lupercalia the runners — the 'course' is their race — struck bystanders, especially women, with strips of goat-hide, and the touch was believed to cure barrenness. Caesar positions his wife to be struck. The phrase 'sterile curse' shows that childlessness is being understood as a supernatural affliction with a ritual remedy. Notice, though, the care of the wording: "our elders say". He attributes the belief to tradition rather than to himself, keeping a small distance from it even as he acts on it. Then "leave no ceremony out": the man who governs Rome wants every rite complete. This is a mind that takes ritual seriously — either because it believes, or because it knows that a watching city expects its leader to be seen believing.

**Contradiction:** The same man, within thirty lines, waves away a direct prophecy about his own death. One reading is that Caesar's superstition is selective: he trusts the ritual that promises what he wants (an heir) and dismisses the omen that threatens what he has (his life). Another reading is that this is not superstition at all but public piety — a head of state observing the civic religion, much as a modern politician attends a ceremony without necessarily believing in it, and that a Soothsayer in a crowd is simply beneath the notice of such a man. The text leaves both open. What is certain is that Shakespeare has placed belief and disbelief so close together that you cannot fail to notice the inconsistency, and inconsistency in a character is always an invitation to think.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the seed of Act 2 Scene 2, where Caesar orders a sacrifice, hears the augurers' bad report, yields to Calpurnia's fears, and then reverses himself under Decius's flattering reinterpretation of her dream — the whole scene is a longer version of the wavering we see here in miniature. It also feeds the play's largest argument, between fate and free will, which the chapter 'Themes of Act 1 (Part Two)' takes up in full. Caesar's relationship with omens is the hinge on which that argument turns: a man who half-believes is more dramatically interesting than either a believer or a sceptic, because his choices remain genuinely his own.

::: history The Lupercalia touch and Caesar's childlessness
HISTORICAL RECORD. The Lupercalia fell on 15 February and was an ancient purification and fertility festival. Its priests, the Luperci, ran through the city striking bystanders — women especially — with thongs of goat-hide to promote fertility; a third college of Luperci, the Juliani, had been created in Caesar's honour in 44 BC, and Antony ran as a Lupercus while serving as consul. Calpurnia had married Caesar in 59 BC and the marriage was childless. Caesar's only legitimate child, his daughter Julia, had died in 54 BC. When his will was read after his death it adopted his great-nephew Gaius Octavius, the future Augustus. So the 'sterile curse' was a real dynastic anxiety — and in a Republic, where power was not supposed to be inherited, that anxiety was itself politically suspect. Elizabethan resonance: in 1599 Elizabeth I was sixty-six, unmarried and without a named heir. Audiences may have felt the question of succession pressing on this moment, but we should say 'may have felt', not assert what they thought.
:::

### Vulnerability

**Textual evidence:** From Caesar's own mouth: "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf". From Cassius: "Caesar cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!'" From Casca: the crowd's noise "had almost choked Caesar, for he swooned and fell down at it". From Brutus, reading faces: "The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow" and "Calpurnia's cheek is pale".

**Explanation:** Caesar is vulnerable in three distinct ways, and a good answer separates them. He is bodily vulnerable — a deaf ear he volunteers himself, a seizure others report. He is dependent — his knowledge of Cassius rests on Antony's counsel ("tell me truly what thou think'st of him"), his fertility hopes rest on Antony's touch, his crowd management rests on Casca's hushing. And he is socially vulnerable: the swoon happens in the marketplace before the common people. Casca's grotesque explanation — that the crowd's "stinking breath" choked him — is comic exaggeration, but it carries a poetic truth. The people's approval or disapproval is literally the air Caesar breathes; when they roar, he falls. Brutus's remark about the "angry spot" belongs here too: a man whose feelings show on his brow so plainly that a bystander can read them is not in complete command of himself.

**Contradiction:** Caesar converts vulnerability into display. The deaf ear is stated without embarrassment, as a fact of logistics, and in the same breath as "always I am Caesar" — weakness announced by a man who does not appear to think it diminishes him. Offering his throat to the crowd is vulnerability turned into a dare. And nearly all the evidence of weakness reaches us from hostile witnesses — Cassius, who wants Brutus to despise Caesar, and Casca, who despises everyone. The only weakness we hear from Caesar himself is the one he chooses to tell.

**Dramatic significance:** A tragedy needs a victim vulnerable enough to be killed and grand enough that killing him feels like sacrilege. Act 1 supplies both halves. It also establishes the pattern by which the conspirators will justify themselves: they collect Caesar's frailties as though frailty were an argument about fitness to rule. The play invites you to test that logic. By Act 3 Scene 2, Antony will display Caesar's wounded body to the crowd and turn its vulnerability into the most powerful political weapon in the play.

### Physical Weakness (Epilepsy, Deafness, the Swoon, the Fever in Spain)

**Textual evidence:** Cassius on Spain: "He had a fever when he was in Spain, / And when the fit was on him, I did mark / How he did shake. 'Tis true, this god did shake." And: "His coward lips did from their colour fly, / And that same eye whose bend doth awe the world / Did lose his lustre." And the cry: "'Alas,' it cried 'Give me some drink, Titinius' / As a sick girl." Casca on the marketplace: "He fell down in the marketplace and foamed at mouth and was speechless." Brutus's diagnosis: "'Tis very like; he hath the falling sickness." Caesar himself: "this ear is deaf".

**Explanation:** Four ailments, four sources, and you must weigh each source. The fever in Spain comes from Cassius, the most hostile witness in the act, and every detail is chosen to unmake the god: the shaking, the colour fleeing the lips ('coward lips' is Cassius's adjective, not a fact), the famous eye losing its 'lustre' (brightness; 'bend' means the direction of a glance), the groan, the childlike cry for a drink, and the humiliating simile 'as a sick girl'. That simile belongs to Cassius's obsession with manliness — in Scene 3 he will call Roman submissiveness "womanish" — and tells you as much about the speaker as the subject. The swoon comes from Casca, who was present but claims "I did not mark it" and then recounts every detail. The medical name comes from Brutus, who is neither hostile nor cynical; 'the falling sickness' was the ordinary English term for epilepsy, and Brutus supplies it in a tone of sympathy. The deaf ear comes from Caesar alone. Gloss the harder words: a 'fit' is a seizure; 'feeble temper' means a weak constitution; to "get the start of the majestic world" is to outrun everyone; to "bear the palm alone" is to take the prize unshared.

**Contradiction:** The Tiber episode cuts both ways, and Cassius does not seem to notice. It was Caesar who proposed the swim — "Dar'st thou, Cassius, now / Leap in with me into this angry flood" — and a man with a weak body who dares a rival into a torrent is reckless, but he is not feeble in spirit. The fever is a fever: a soldier on campaign in Spain fell ill, which proves nothing about his fitness to govern. And Cassius's own conclusion — that "A man of such a feeble temper should / So get the start of the majestic world / And bear the palm alone" amazes him — is less evidence of Caesar's weakness than of Cassius's bewilderment at his own inferiority. Meanwhile the deaf ear is Shakespeare's invention, and inventions must be asked what they are for. One strong reading: it lets the audience hear the pun when a man who cannot hear on one side turns his other ear and asks to be told the truth. Caesar's deafness is literal and figurative at once.

**Dramatic significance:** Cassius performs the crucial move when Brutus names the disease: "No, Caesar hath it not; but you and I / And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness." He transfers the illness from Caesar's body to the body of Rome — the Romans are the ones who have 'fallen', into servility. This is the act's method in one line: physical weakness is converted into a political argument. The play then spends four more acts testing whether the argument was sound. The dead Caesar of Acts 3 to 5 turns out to be mightier than the living one, which is a way of saying that the conspirators mistook the body they were attacking.

::: textnote Editions differ on a few words in these reports
Where Folger has "he swooned and fell down at it", the Globe-based text many ICSE editions follow reads 'swounded' — the older spelling of the same word. Folger's "clapped their chopped hands" appears in other editions as 'chapped hands'; both mean cracked and roughened by work. Folger prints "theater" where British editions print "theatre". The 'falling sickness' is the same in all editions. None of these variants changes the meaning; if your school edition has the older spelling, use it.
:::

::: history What the ancient sources actually say about Caesar's body
HISTORICAL RECORD. Caesar's epilepsy — the 'falling sickness' — is attested in Plutarch and Suetonius, though modern historians debate the diagnosis. His deafness has no basis in any ancient source; it appears to be Shakespeare's addition. Plutarch's account of the Lupercalia contains the diadem offered and refused, but no fainting fit; editors generally trace Caesar's excuse of illness to a separate episode in Plutarch, when Caesar gave offence by failing to rise for the Senate and afterwards blamed his condition, and they suggest Shakespeare folded that episode into the crown scene. Treat this last point as the consensus of editors rather than as a certainty.
:::

### Political Intelligence (His Reading of Cassius)

**Textual evidence:** "He reads much, / He is a great observer, and he looks / Quite through the deeds of men." "Seldom he smiles, and smiles in such a sort / As if he mocked himself and scorned his spirit / That could be moved to smile at anything." "Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves, / And therefore are they very dangerous."

**Explanation:** This is the most accurate character sketch anyone gives of anyone in Act 1, and you can test it against what you have just watched. Cassius has indeed looked "Quite through" Brutus, observing him "now of late" and reading the trouble in his face. He has delivered a long speech of bitter self-mockery, calling himself "A wretched creature". And the motive Caesar names — the inability to bear a greater — is exactly what Cassius confessed to Brutus: "I had as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself." Even the small details hold. While the games were on and the crowd shouted, Cassius was not watching or listening; he was working on a man. Caesar's portrait is not prejudice. It is diagnosis, and the play has already supplied the symptoms.

**Contradiction:** Intelligence without action is the tragic form of intelligence. Caesar diagnoses and then does nothing: "But I fear him not." Worse, he asks the wrong adviser. Antony — the man Caesar himself teases as a lover of plays and music — has just pronounced Cassius "well given", and Caesar overrides that judgement, then turns and asks for it again. Worst of all is the blind spot. HISTORICAL RECORD: in Plutarch, Caesar's remark about not fearing fat men but pale, lean ones pointed at Brutus and Cassius both. Shakespeare's Caesar names only Cassius. His intelligence works perfectly right up to the edge of the man who loves him, and stops there. Love, in this play, is what disables perception.

**Dramatic significance:** This is dramatic irony at its most concentrated — the audience, fresh from Cassius's seduction of Brutus, hears every clause confirmed — but it also reframes the tragedy. If Caesar were simply blind, his death would be pathetic. Because he sees clearly and chooses to remain 'Caesar' anyway, his death becomes something graver: a man destroyed not by ignorance but by the refusal to let knowledge alter conduct. Hold on to this when you reach Act 3 Scene 1, where he refuses to read a letter that would have saved him.

### Public Performance (the Crown Refusals, Offering His Throat)

**Textual evidence:** Casca's report: "he put it by with the back of his hand, thus, and then the people fell a-shouting"; "he put it by thrice, every time gentler than other"; "to my thinking, he would fain have had it"; "to my thinking, he was very loath to lay his fingers off it"; "he plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut"; and the crowd behaved "as they use to do the players in the theatre".

**Explanation:** The most important thing about the crown scene is that you do not see it. Shakespeare keeps it offstage and gives it to Casca to narrate, so that Caesar's most public act reaches us through the least generous eyes in Rome. Gloss the words: to 'put by' is to push aside; 'fain' means gladly; 'loath' means reluctant; a 'coronet' is a small crown (Casca is careful to reduce even the object); the 'rabblement' is the mob; 'chopped' hands are chapped from work; 'nightcaps' are the common people's caps; a 'doublet' is an Elizabethan jacket; 'ope' is open. Casca reads the three refusals as theatre — each "gentler than other", as though the hand were increasingly unwilling to push the thing away — and the throat gesture as a further piece of stagecraft: a dare to the crowd, meaning 'kill me if you think me ambitious'. The theatre simile completes the picture: Caesar is a player, the crowd an audience who "clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them". The anachronistic 'doublet' — a Roman in an Elizabethan jacket — quietly reminds the Globe audience that they too are watching an actor play a man who is acting.

**Contradiction:** The performance is interrupted by a body. Shakespeare places the seizure at the exact climax of the show, so that the script Caesar is following is torn up by the one thing he cannot direct. And the reading of the performance as insincere rests entirely on Casca, who says "to my thinking" twice and has been described by Cassius as speaking "after his sour fashion"; even Brutus is struck by how "blunt" he has become. An alternative reading is available and you should give it: a man who wanted the crown had three chances to take it and took none; the crowd cheered each refusal; Caesar read their mood and correctly declined. The play does not adjudicate (act as judge between the two). It hands you a cynic's account and asks whether you trust the cynic.

**Dramatic significance:** The whole of Julius Caesar is about political theatre — Brutus and Antony will perform to this same crowd in Act 3 Scene 2 — and Act 1 establishes the Roman people as an audience and Caesar as the first performer they judge. Casca's verdict that the crowd would have forgiven Caesar even "if Caesar had stabbed their mothers" is the play's first statement of the mob's malleability (how easily it can be shaped), which Cassius understands ("he sees the Romans are but sheep") and Antony will exploit. Note the historical shift, too: HISTORICAL RECORD says Plutarch's diadem was offered and refused twice; Shakespeare's Casca makes it three times, the folk-tale number, so that the refusals themselves feel like a rehearsed routine.

### Fear (What He Fears and How He Hides It)

**Textual evidence:** In the exchange with Antony the word 'fear' sounds five times in under twenty lines: Antony's "Fear him not, Caesar", then Caesar's "But I fear him not", "Yet if my name were liable to fear", and "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear". Then the private request: "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him."

**Explanation:** What Caesar fears is specific: the thin, reading, observing man who cannot bear a superior — envy with a brain. He may also fear the future, since he does stop for the Soothsayer. How he hides it is the more interesting question, and the text shows four techniques. He uses the third person, so that 'Caesar' cannot fear even if the man does. He converts fear into a lecture — "I rather tell thee what is to be feared" makes him the teacher of fear rather than its sufferer. He uses conditional grammar: "if my name were liable to fear" admits the hypothesis only in order to deny it. And he takes the conversation private, drawing Antony to his good ear and away from the train. A man who whispers his question about Cassius is a man who does not want the answer overheard.

**Contradiction:** Fear is not cowardice, and you must not let Cassius's phrase "coward lips" settle the matter. A man who dares a rival into an angry flood and bares his throat to a mob does not lack physical courage. Caesar's fear is political, and it is accurate: the man he fears is the man who is at this moment plotting his death. The hiding, moreover, fails only for the audience, who know too much; Antony, standing beside him, plainly does not perceive it, since he calls Cassius harmless. Caesar hides his fear well from his friends and badly from us.

**Dramatic significance:** The pattern — denial that reveals — is the key the audience needs for Act 2 Scene 2, where Caesar will speak at length about cowardice and danger while visibly wavering over whether to leave the house. Act 1 trains us to read those later speeches correctly: the louder the denial, the deeper the fear. It also sets up a bitter irony of structure: the play's most fearless-sounding character is the one who dies, while the man who feared nothing openly — Cassius, presenting himself to the lightning — ends, in Act 5, half-believing the omens he once scorned.

### Leadership

**Textual evidence:** "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed." The Cobbler: "we make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph." Casca on the crowd: they forgave him "with all their hearts". Cassius: "he sees the Romans are but sheep". Brutus: "all the rest look like a chidden train".

**Explanation:** Act 1 shows Caesar leading in three modes. There is command, captured in Antony's formula of instant obedience. There is ceremony — the procession, the trumpets, the ordered ritual — which is leadership through spectacle. And there is popularity: working men take an unlicensed holiday to see him, and women forgive him for fainting. Marullus's insults ("You blocks, you stones") cannot break that hold. Brutus's observation about the "chidden train" adds a fourth element: the leader's mood governs the mood of everyone around him. When Caesar is angry, Rome's great men look scolded.

**Contradiction:** The leader is led. Casca's account has the crowd applauding and hissing "according as he pleased and displeased them", and the refusals shaped by the crowd's reaction — Caesar reads the room and gives it what it cheers. Cassius's animal image says the same thing from the other side: Caesar "would not be a wolf / But that he sees the Romans are but sheep"; the followers make the leader. Add two further shadows: leadership by suppression (the tribunes "put to silence") and leadership by delegation (Antony runs the course, offers the crown, is asked for judgement). Strip these away and how much of Caesar's leadership is Caesar?

**Dramatic significance:** Act 1 sets the baseline against which the play measures the leadership vacuum after the assassination — Brutus's inability to hold the crowd and Antony's mastery of it in Act 3 Scene 2. It also puts a question that the whole play refuses to answer simply: whether a republic of 'sheep' deserves any better than a wolf. The chapter 'Themes of Act 1 (Part One)' pursues that under 'Leadership' and 'Public Opinion'.

### Ambition (What the Text Shows versus What Cassius Alleges)

**Textual evidence:** What is shown or reported as fact: a triumph, a ceremonial procession, a crown refused three times, an apology to the crowd, and — in the passive voice, with no agent named — the tribunes "are put to silence". What is alleged: Cassius's forged letters, in which "obscurely / Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at"; Cassius's question to Casca, "And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then?"; and Casca's hearsay, "they say the Senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king".

**Explanation:** Keep this distinction razor-sharp, because it is the one that separates a thinking answer from a copied one. The noun 'ambition' is applied to Caesar exactly once in Act 1 — by Cassius, in the same breath as a forgery, and with the word "obscurely" (hinted at, not stated). Casca's belief that Caesar "would fain have had it" is prefaced twice with "to my thinking". The report of the Senate's plan begins "they say". Every piece of evidence for Caesar's ambition is either inference, rumour or fabrication, and every piece is voiced by someone who wants Brutus or Casca to believe it. What Caesar visibly does is refuse a crown. HISTORICAL RECORD adds a twist: the real Caesar had been made dictator for life in February 44 BC, and Shakespeare never mentions the title in Act 1. The play's Caesar is judged on less evidence than the historical one.

**Contradiction:** The mediated evidence is not nothing, and a fair answer says so. Refusing "every time gentler than other" is at least suggestive. Marullus's charge that Caesar "comes in triumph over Pompey's blood" — celebrating victory over fellow Romans — is a fact of the play's world, not a rumour. The silencing of the tribunes happened. "Caesar is turned to hear" is the speech of a man who has already crowned himself in language. And Brutus — no cynic and no forger — fears that "the people / Choose Caesar for their king", which shows that the anxiety about monarchy is in Rome's air and not only in Cassius's mouth.

**Dramatic significance:** Shakespeare keeps ambition unproven in Act 1 so that Act 3 Scene 2 can stage its trial: Brutus asserting to the crowd that Caesar was ambitious, Antony asking whether the man's conduct looked like ambition. The audience must judge with the same incomplete evidence the Romans have. Had Act 1 shown Caesar grasping at a crown, the play would be a melodrama about a tyrant; because it shows only refusal and rumour, it becomes a tragedy of interpretation. EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE: when a Class X question on Act 3 Scene 2 asks whether Caesar was ambitious, the strongest answers say that Act 1 supplies no direct proof and that both orators are arguing over a gap.

### Reputation

**Textual evidence:** Cassius: "Except immortal Caesar"; "this man / Is now become a god"; and the tests he runs on the name — "Write them together, yours is as fair a name; / Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; / Weigh them, it is as heavy". Flavius: "Let no images / Be hung with Caesar's trophies." Casca's wenches: "Alas, good soul!"

**Explanation:** Caesar's reputation has detached from his body and become a thing in itself — a name that can be written, sounded, weighed and conjured with, a set of trophies hung on statues, a public event people take a holiday to see. Cassius's sarcastic 'immortal' and 'god' are testimony to a reputation so inflated that it has become theological, and his need to prove that "Brutus" will "start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar'" shows that he knows reputation, not soldiers, is the real power in Rome. The wenches' "Alas, good soul!" shows the other face of it: genuine popular affection, which Casca cannot bear and cannot deny.

**Contradiction:** Reputation is exactly what the conspiracy plans to fight with — but by attacking Caesar's through the manipulation of Brutus's: the forged letters play on "the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name". In this play reputations are made of paper and shouting, and can be counterfeited. Caesar's own is double: the same man is a Colossus in one Cassius speech and a "sick girl" in the previous one. A reputation that can be both is a reputation that other people are writing.

**Dramatic significance:** Act 3 Scene 2 is a battle over Caesar's posthumous reputation, won by Antony with a will and a bloodied cloak. Act 1 shows why reputation is the true throne of Rome: nobody in the act fights over Caesar's armies or offices; they fight over his images, his name and what the crowd thinks of him.

### Mortality

**Textual evidence:** "Beware the ides of March." Cassius's memory: "Caesar cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!'" Casca: "foamed at mouth and was speechless". Flavius's image of "growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing". Cassius in the storm: a man like the night "That thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars".

**Explanation:** The Soothsayer names a date, and from that moment the play has a clock. Everything in Act 1 then circles Caesar's mortality without naming it: the swoon is a rehearsal of death (speechless, foaming, fallen in the marketplace); the Tiber episode is a near-drowning; Flavius's image plucks a soaring bird down to "an ordinary pitch" — the height of ordinary men, which is also the height at which men die; and Cassius's storm 'opens graves'. The act is full of Caesar's death before anyone has proposed it.

**Contradiction:** Set against all this is a vocabulary of deathlessness: "immortal Caesar", "Is now become a god" and above all "always I am Caesar" — 'always' is a claim against time itself. The man tells his procession to move on past the one person who has told him when he will die. The contradiction is not resolved; it is the tragedy in miniature.

**Dramatic significance:** Because the audience knows the history, every mention of the ides produces double vision — the man who cannot die and the date on which he does. Act 3 Scene 1 will pay this off when Caesar, entering the Capitol, reminds the Soothsayer that the ides have come and receives the reply that they have not yet gone. The chapter 'The Soothsayer, the Ides of March, and the Supporting Cast' traces the date through the play.

### Succession (No Heir; Touching Calpurnia)

**Textual evidence:** "Stand you directly in Antonius' way / When he doth run his course." "The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse."

**Explanation:** Caesar's first anxiety on stage is dynastic (about founding a line of heirs): before he has spoken to the crowd, before the Soothsayer, he is arranging for a child. Why does Shakespeare open his part this way? Because in a republic there is nothing to inherit. A man who worries publicly about an heir is a man already thinking like a king, and the anxiety is itself a political tell — a small, human, damning detail placed before any accusation has been made. Notice too that it is performed in public: a private grief made into civic ritual, with Antony — historically the consul of that year — running the course and the crowd watching the wife.

**Contradiction:** The play's Caesar wants an heir while refusing a crown. If he has no ambition for kingship, why does the heir matter so much? One answer is that Shakespeare is quietly exposing the monarch beneath the republican performance. Another is that the desire for a child is simply human, and the moment is there to make Caesar a man before Cassius makes him a monster. Both readings are available; a good answer offers both and says which it finds stronger. HISTORICAL RECORD, for context: Brutus's mother Servilia was Caesar's long-time mistress, and the ancient rumour that Caesar was Brutus's father is rejected by modern scholars on grounds of chronology. Shakespeare never mentions it, and you should not import it into the play as fact.

**Dramatic significance:** 'Barren' and 'sterile' open the play's imagery of fertility and blood that Act 2 will develop. Caesar's childlessness also deepens the betrayal to come: the man he "loves" is a younger Roman who might have been a son, and the news at the end of Act 3 Scene 2 that Octavius has arrived in Rome will begin to answer the succession question this moment raised — by adoption, not by birth.

### Relationship with Antony

**Textual evidence:** "Antonius." — "Caesar, my lord." "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed." "He loves no plays, / As thou dost, Antony; he hears no music". "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him." Casca: "Who offered him the crown?" — "Why, Antony."

**Explanation:** The relationship is master and instrument, but it is also intimacy. Caesar knows Antony's tastes well enough to tease him — the playgoer, the music-lover — and the teasing is affectionate, not contemptuous. He confides in him alone, and he trusts him with the most delicate public act of the day, the offering of the crown. Antony's side is total loyalty expressed as total obedience: his formula makes Caesar's word and its performance a single event.

**Contradiction:** Caesar overrules Antony's judgement of Cassius and then asks for it again, which suggests either that he values Antony's honesty more than his accuracy, or that he is looking for reassurance rather than counsel. And Antony's loyalty formula is precisely the "servile fearfulness" the tribunes dreaded — the free Roman reduced to a hand that performs. The friend who loves Caesar best is the clearest evidence of what Caesar's Rome is becoming.

**Dramatic significance:** Antony's misjudgement of Cassius here is matched by the conspirators' misjudgement of Antony in Act 2 Scene 1, when Brutus argues he is harmless. Caesar's playful dismissal of the pleasure-lover thus foreshadows the conspirators' fatal underestimate of the same man. Everything Antony does in Act 3 — the grief, the negotiation, the oration — grows from the loyalty planted in these few Act 1 lines.

### Relationship with Cassius

**Textual evidence:** Cassius: "Caesar doth bear me hard" ('bears a grudge against me'); "If Caesar carelessly but nod on him"; the Tiber dare, "Dar'st thou, Cassius, now / Leap in with me into this angry flood"; and the rescue, "so from the waves of Tiber / Did I the tirèd Caesar". Caesar: "Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look"; "I do not know the man I should avoid / So soon as that spare Cassius."

**Explanation:** This is a relationship of mutual, unequal watching. Cassius remembers saving Caesar's life and resents having done it; his comparison of himself to Aeneas carrying old Anchises out of Troy casts Caesar as an aged burden on a younger man's back. Caesar, for his part, has studied Cassius closely enough to describe his reading habits and his smile. HISTORICAL RECORD supplies a grievance the play never names: for 44 BC Brutus received the more prestigious urban praetorship and Cassius the lesser one, and ancient sources treat this as a source of Cassius's resentment.

**Contradiction:** Cassius claims Caesar barely notices him — a careless nod is all he gets — yet Caesar has noticed him more precisely than anyone in Rome. The contempt Cassius feels is answered not by indifference but by attention. And Cassius's contempt is shot through with awe: the "eye whose bend doth awe the world" is his phrase, and the sarcasm of "this god" cannot quite hide that he once believed in the god.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the one relationship in which Caesar's political intelligence functions completely — and it changes nothing. Cassius ends Scene 2 with "we will shake him", and the shaking he mocked in Spain ("this god did shake") returns as a threat. The conspiracy begins with a man Caesar has correctly identified and chosen not to remove.

### Relationship with Brutus

**Textual evidence:** Cassius: "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus." Brutus: "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well." The one direct exchange between them: "A soothsayer bids you beware the ides of March." Cassius, weighing the love: "If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me."

**Explanation:** Caesar and Brutus speak directly to each other exactly once in Act 1, and the content is a warning: the man who will strike him tells him to beware. Everything else about their bond exists in other people's mouths. Cassius vouches for Caesar's love; Brutus vouches for his own. Both use the strong word 'love'. Cassius treats that love as a lever, and his sentence is ambiguous in a way editors still debate — either 'if Caesar loved me as he loves Brutus, I would not let his favour soften me' or 'if our roles were reversed, Brutus could not work on me as I am working on him'. On either reading Cassius regards Caesar's affection for Brutus as a weakness to be exploited. HISTORICAL RECORD: Brutus had fought for Pompey and been pardoned by Caesar after Pharsalus; Caesar then advanced him to the urban praetorship for 44 BC. The love had a history of mercy behind it.

**Contradiction:** Brutus's sentence puts the political refusal first and the love second — "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well" — and that 'yet' is the hinge of his tragedy: the love is real, but it is already conditional. Caesar's love is unconditional and therefore blind; he fears Cassius and never thinks of Brutus. In this play love disables intelligence on both sides — it stops Caesar seeing his killer and it stops Brutus seeing what he is becoming.

**Dramatic significance:** Caesar's dying words to Brutus in Act 3 Scene 1, and Antony's insistence in Act 3 Scene 2 that Brutus's blow was the cruellest, depend entirely on this seed. EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE: when a Class X question asks why Caesar's death at Brutus's hand is the play's deepest betrayal, this is the Act 1 evidence to cite — one line from Cassius, one from Brutus, both using 'love', and the fact that Brutus was the one who relayed the warning.

## Caesar from Three Angles

The trait-by-trait work gives you the parts. Now assemble them from three standpoints, because an examiner's question about Caesar is nearly always, underneath its wording, a question about *whose* Caesar you mean: the one he projects, the one his observers construct, or the one the audience pieces together from the gap between those two.

### Caesar as He Presents Himself

Take only the thirty-nine lines Caesar speaks and ask what self they advertise. The first thing they advertise is order. He arranges bodies in space — "Stand you directly in Antonius' way" — and arranges the day's ritual — "Set on and leave no ceremony out" — like a man for whom Rome is a procession he is directing. The second is completeness: nothing about him is allowed to be casual, which is why the deaf ear is presented not as a lapse but as a logistical fact to be managed by walking round to the other side. The third is fearlessness, stated as a definition rather than a feeling: "for always I am Caesar". He never says 'I am brave'; he says 'I am Caesar', and leaves you to deduce the bravery. The fourth is discernment. The one long speech he is given is an act of judgement — a man weighing another man — and he wants to be seen judging well. The fifth, quieter than the rest, is piety: the elders' custom is honoured, the ceremonies observed. What he does not present is appetite. In Act 1 Caesar never asks for anything for himself — not the crown, not applause, not even sympathy after the swoon, where (on Casca's report) he asks only to be excused. The self Caesar presents is finished, public and unwanting: a monument that happens to walk and speak. Hold on to that word 'unwanting', because everyone else in the act is going to insist that he wants a great deal.

### Caesar as Others See Him

**Flavius and Marullus.** To the tribunes Caesar is a threat to the Republic measured in bird-flight: "Who else would soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness". They never mention his virtues, his victories against foreigners or his popularity except as a danger; their Caesar is the man who "comes in triumph over Pompey's blood", a Roman celebrating the killing of Romans. Notice that their hostility is institutional, not personal. They are tribunes, officers whose historic job was to protect the people against overmighty men, and they see in Caesar the office-holder's nightmare: a single man growing larger than the constitution. Their Caesar is a political abstraction with feathers. The ironic answer to them comes offstage, in a single sentence of Casca's: "Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence."

**The Cobbler.** To the working man Caesar is a holiday. "We make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph" — the triumph is an event, like a festival or a fair, and the man at its centre is a spectacle to be enjoyed. The Cobbler's Caesar has no politics attached; he knows nothing of Pompey's blood and is baffled to be told he should. He also, in his jokes, treats the whole occasion as good for trade ("to wear out their shoes, to get myself into more work"). This is the Caesar of the crowd that will shout three times at the games: adored, uncomprehended, and loved as entertainment rather than as a ruler. The tribunes think this is a disgrace. The play is less sure: the Cobbler's affection is real and the tribunes' republic is already a memory.

**Cassius.** Cassius's Caesar is a construction built for a purpose, and you must always read it as such. It has two faces that the speaker never reconciles. One is the Colossus who "doth bestride the narrow world" — gigantic, deified, the man whose name can be conjured with. The other is the sick swimmer who cried "Help me, Cassius, or I sink!" and the fever patient with "coward lips" who whimpered like "a sick girl". Cassius needs both: the giant to make Brutus feel the humiliation of being an underling, the invalid to make the giant seem a fraud. The join between them is the confession that gives the game away — "I had as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself" — because it tells you Cassius's real complaint is not that Caesar is unfit but that Caesar is not Cassius. In Scene 3 his Caesar changes shape again, becoming a monster "Most like this dreadful night" and then, in the next breath, a "Poor man" who is only a wolf because Romans are sheep. A portrait that alters this often is a portrait of the painter.

**Brutus.** Brutus's Caesar is almost invisible, and the invisibility is the point. He never describes Caesar's character; he describes his own feelings about Caesar's position. "I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king" — the fear attaches to the people's choice and to the word 'king', not to anything Caesar has done. "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well" — the love is stated and the objection is to a hypothesis. The one time he reads Caesar's face, he sees a mood — "The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow" — and, when he hears of the swoon, he gives it a medical name without malice. Brutus's Caesar is a friend who happens to stand where a king might stand. This is why Cassius must work so hard: there is no resentment in Brutus to inflame, only a principle to exploit.

**Casca.** Casca's Caesar is an actor, and a poor one. Everything in his report reduces the man: the crown is "one of these coronets", the refusal is a routine performed "every time gentler than other", the ambition is visible to anyone with eyes ("to my thinking, he would fain have had it"), the collapse is almost comic. Yet Casca cannot help reporting things that contradict his contempt — the throat offered to the crowd, the apology, the women who "forgave him with all their hearts" — and he shields himself from his own evidence with "It was mere foolery; I did not mark it." His Caesar is the Caesar of a man who despises everybody, the crowd most of all, and whose cynicism (the habit of assuming the worst motive) is a way of never being impressed. The audience is meant to enjoy him and to distrust him in equal measure.

**Antony.** Antony's Caesar is simply 'my lord'. He answers to his name with the title, promises obedience in the famous formula — "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed" — and, asked about Cassius, answers with loyal optimism: "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous." Antony's Caesar is a master worth serving without question, and the view is so uncomplicated that the act gives him no reason to examine it. It is also, in Act 1, the view that is most wrong about the immediate danger and most right about the man's worth — a combination that will make Antony's grief in Act 3 both sincere and politically devastating.

### Caesar as the Audience Gradually Discovers Him

Shakespeare releases Caesar to the audience in a controlled sequence, and the order of discovery is itself an argument. First we hear the *name* in Scene 1, fought over by tribunes and commoners, so that before Caesar exists he is already a dispute. Then we see the *ceremony* at the start of Scene 2: trumpets, hush, commands, a wife positioned for ritual. Then we hear the *warning* and watch him examine and dismiss it — our first glimpse of the mind behind the ceremony, and it looks confident to the point of carelessness. Then, for the long middle of the scene, Caesar is absent and Cassius builds the *biography*: the swimmer, the fever, the Colossus. Because we have no other information, this biography fills the vacuum. Then Caesar returns, and for the first time we hear him *think*, and the thinking is sharper than anything Cassius led us to expect; the audience, having been taught to look down on him, is made to look up again. Then Casca's report gives us the *spectacle* we were not allowed to see, filtered through contempt, and finally Scene 3 gives us Caesar as *weather*: a storm that other men interpret.

What the audience discovers, then, is not a character but a method of character-making: the realisation that every account of Caesar is a performance by someone with an interest, including Caesar's own. By the end of Act 1 you know less about Caesar than about the people describing him — which is precisely the knowledge you need for Acts 2 and 3, where the question 'was Caesar ambitious?' is decided not by evidence but by who speaks last.

## The Two Bodies of Caesar

LITERARY INTERPRETATION. Medieval and Tudor political thought held that a king has two bodies: a 'body natural', which ages, sickens and dies like anyone's, and a 'body politic', the undying public office that passes from one holder to the next. The historian Ernst Kantorowicz gave this idea its classic description in *The King's Two Bodies* (1957), drawing on Elizabethan lawyers who wrote in exactly these terms. Shakespeare had no need of the treatise; the doctrine was in the air of his time, and *Julius Caesar* dramatises it with unusual clarity — in a republic, where there is officially no king at all.

Caesar's body politic is the Colossus. It is built of a name that can be conjured with, images hung with trophies, a procession that moves at "Let us leave him. Pass", a people that takes a holiday to see it, and a grammar in which "Caesar is turned to hear" and "always I am Caesar". This body does not swim, sweat or shake. It is the thing Cassius means when he sneers at "immortal Caesar" and the thing the tribunes fear when they see a man soaring "above the view of men". Caesar's body natural is the one Cassius dragged from the Tiber, the one that shook in Spain and "foamed at mouth and was speechless" in the marketplace, the one with a deaf ear and no child. Act 1 is constructed so that we meet the two bodies in alternation: ceremony, then the Soothsayer; the Colossus speech, then the fever; the man who fears nobody, then the man who cannot hear on one side.

The brilliance of the design is that Caesar himself lives in the public body and treats the natural one as an inconvenience to be announced and ignored. "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf" is the body natural speaking; "for always I am Caesar", in the previous line, is the body politic. He does not seem to feel the contradiction, and that is his tragedy in miniature: a man who has moved house into his own legend. Cassius, meanwhile, makes the opposite mistake — he believes that if he can expose the natural body (the sick girl, the swooner) the political body will collapse with it. Casca's report tells us that this does not happen: the crowd saw Caesar fall, foam and apologise, and loved him more.

::: insight The conspirators kill the wrong body
LITERARY INTERPRETATION. Here is what a school guide will not tell you about Act 1: it is a long demonstration that Caesar's power does not live in Caesar's body, delivered to men who are about to stab the body. Every piece of evidence the conspirators gather — the near-drowning, the fever, the deafness, the swoon — belongs to the body natural, and every piece of evidence of his power — the name, the images, the holiday, the shouting, the silenced tribunes — belongs to the body politic. Cassius's own tests prove the point against him: he asks Brutus to "Write them together", "Sound them", "Weigh them", and the whole exercise is about names, not men. So when the daggers go in during Act 3 Scene 1 they go into the only Caesar that was ever killable, and the other Caesar — the name, the will, the wounds displayed in the marketplace, the spirit that haunts Brutus to Philippi — proves to be the one that mattered. This is why the man who speaks fewer than forty lines in Act 1 gives the play its title: the title names the body politic, which is on stage for five acts, not the body natural, which leaves in the third. When you write about Caesar's weakness, make this move: show that the weaknesses are real, then show that they are beside the point, and your answer will have done what Cassius could not.
:::

## Writing About Caesar in the Examination

EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE. First, the honest frame. Act 1 is the Class IX portion; the Class X board paper sets its drama extracts from Acts III–V, and no question can be predicted or guaranteed. The Caesar of Act 1 earns you marks in two ways: directly, in Class IX reference-to-the-context questions on this act; and indirectly but constantly in Class X, where any sub-question about Caesar's fall, Brutus's motives or Antony's oration (an extract question is worth 16 marks, split in the 2025 paper into sub-parts of 3, 3, 3, 3 and 4) is answered better by a student who can reach back to the seeds planted here. The second thing to get right is vocabulary. 'Arrogant' is the word every weak answer reaches for, and it fails for a precise reason: it describes a mood, not a mechanism. The examiner wants to see you explain *how* Caesar's self-regard works and *what it costs him*. The bank below gives you words that do that.

::: icse How to write about Caesar without saying 'arrogant'
**Authority** — the power to command and be obeyed. *Sample:* 'Caesar's authority is shown in the grammar of his speech: nearly every line is an order, and Antony's "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed" turns his word and its execution into a single event.'

**Self-mythologising** — building a legend about oneself and living inside it. *Sample:* 'Caesar has adopted Rome's estimate of him as his private identity; "for always I am Caesar" treats the name as a condition that cannot admit fear.'

**Third-person self-reference** — speaking of oneself by name, as an outsider would. *Sample:* 'When Caesar says "Caesar is turned to hear", he reports on himself as if on an institution, splitting the man from the office — the split the conspirators will fatally misunderstand.'

**Public persona** — the self presented to others, as distinct from the private man. *Sample:* 'The crown refusals, the offered throat and the apology to the crowd are all parts of a public persona; Casca's "to my thinking" reminds us that we see the performance only through a hostile critic.'

**Vulnerability** — openness to harm, bodily or political. *Sample:* 'Caesar's vulnerability is announced by himself ("this ear is deaf") and reported by his enemies (the fever, the swoon); the play asks whether a frail body is any argument against a man's fitness to rule.'

**Superstition versus overconfidence** — the tension between observing omens and dismissing them. *Sample:* 'The man who wants "no ceremony" left out of a fertility rite dismisses the Soothsayer as "a dreamer" within thirty lines; Act 2 Scene 2 will replay this wavering at length.'

**Political intelligence** — the ability to read men and motives. *Sample:* 'Caesar's reading of Cassius — one who looks "Quite through the deeds of men" — is the most accurate judgement in Act 1; his tragedy is that insight never becomes action.'

**Mediated testimony** — evidence reaching us through a biased witness. *Sample:* 'Almost everything we know of Caesar's weakness is mediated by Cassius and Casca, so a careful answer weighs the witness before it weighs the evidence.'

Two cautions. Use **hubris** only if you also show the fall it invites; otherwise it is 'arrogant' in Greek. And never write that Caesar 'is' ambitious in Act 1: write that ambition is *alleged* and show by whom.
:::

Now watch the vocabulary at work on a question that is set, in one form or another, in many Class IX papers and that underlies every Class X debate about the assassination: 'Is Caesar a tyrant in Act 1?' The weak paragraph is not foolish; it is what most students write. Compare it with the strong one and notice exactly what the difference consists of.

::: weak Is Caesar a tyrant in Act 1? — a weak paragraph
Caesar is shown as an arrogant and ambitious tyrant in Act 1. He speaks of himself in the third person and he ignores the Soothsayer's warning "Beware the ides of March", which shows his pride. Cassius tells Brutus that Caesar is weak and had a fever in Spain, and that he nearly drowned in the Tiber, so he is not fit to rule Rome. Casca says the crown was offered three times and Caesar wanted it. The tribunes are punished for taking down his decorations. All this shows that Caesar is a proud tyrant who wants to be king and that is why the conspirators decide to kill him.
:::

::: strong Is Caesar a tyrant in Act 1? — an excellent paragraph
Act 1 gives us the materials for calling Caesar a tyrant but is careful never to confirm the charge, and a precise answer must keep the two apart. The strongest direct evidence is the fate of Marullus and Flavius, who "are put to silence" for pulling scarves off his images — the one act of repression in the act, reported in a passive voice that names no agent. Beyond that, the case rests on witnesses with motives. Cassius, who confesses he cannot bear to live "In awe of such a thing as I myself", supplies the physical frailties and the Colossus image; Casca, whose every judgement is prefaced "to my thinking", reads three public refusals of a crown as disguised desire; and the only use of the word 'ambition' in the whole act is Cassius's plan to have it "glancèd at" in forged letters. What Caesar himself shows us is authority rather than tyranny: a grammar of command, a self-mythologising confidence — "for always I am Caesar" — and a public persona that gives the crowd back what it cheers. Even his superstition is civic piety as much as weakness. The honest conclusion is that Shakespeare presents a man whom republicans fear as a potential king and whom the text declines to convict, so that when Brutus kills him in Act 3 for what he 'may' become, the audience has been trained by Act 1 to notice the gap between evidence and verdict.
:::

The difference is five moves. The strong paragraph separates what Caesar does from what others say he does; it names and weighs its witnesses; it quotes short and exact, each quotation making a point; it uses the bank's words where the weak one uses 'arrogant', 'proud' and 'ambitious' as if they were facts; and it ends with a judgement of its own, tied to what the play does next. Practise those moves until they are automatic.

::: revision Caesar in Act 1 — what to carry away
1. Caesar speaks thirty-nine lines, all in Scene 2, and is given no soliloquy; most of what we 'know' of him is testimony from interested witnesses.
2. His own words show authority (imperatives), confidence ("He is a dreamer"), self-mythologising ("for always I am Caesar") and one volunteered frailty ("this ear is deaf").
3. His reading of Cassius is the most accurate judgement in the act — and it changes nothing, because 'Caesar' cannot act on fear.
4. His weaknesses (fever, Tiber, swoon, deafness) come from Cassius and Casca; the deafness is Shakespeare's invention, the epilepsy is in Plutarch.
5. The crown scene is reported, not staged; Casca's three refusals replace Plutarch's two, and his reading of them is opinion.
6. Ambition is alleged, never shown: one forged hint, one "to my thinking", one "they say".
7. Caesar wants an heir while refusing a crown — a human wish and a political tell at once.
8. He loves Brutus and fears Cassius; love is the blind spot where the fatal blow will land.
9. The two bodies: Caesar lives in the public one; the conspirators will kill the natural one and discover their mistake.
10. Write with the bank — authority, public persona, self-mythologising, vulnerability, mediated testimony — and never settle for 'arrogant'.
:::
