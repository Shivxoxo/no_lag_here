# The Soothsayer, the Ides of March, and the Supporting Cast

*This chapter takes the people of Act 1 whom school notes usually rush past, and shows you why Shakespeare put each of them there. Part A is a slow, professor-level reading of a single line, "Beware the ides of March": what it literally says, what the Ides were in the Roman calendar, and how one sentence spoken by a nameless man in a crowd sets the clock for the whole tragedy and opens the play's great argument about fate and free will. Part B turns to the supporting cast — Antony, Casca, Cicero, Cinna, the tribunes, the Cobbler and Carpenter, Calpurnia and the crowd itself — and asks of each one: what is the evidence, what is the function, and what will an examiner expect you to know? A foreshadowing map and a full character map close the chapter.*

## PART A — THE SOOTHSAYER AND THE IDES OF MARCH

### The moment itself: what actually happens

Before we analyse anything, you must be able to see the moment. Read it as a director would.

Caesar has just entered Rome's public square in procession. It is the feast of Lupercal (the Lupercalia, a February festival of purification and fertility at which young men ran through the city striking bystanders with goat-hide thongs). He gives two brisk domestic orders: Calpurnia must stand in Antony's way as he runs the ritual race, and Antony must remember to touch her so that she may "Shake off their sterile curse". Antony answers with a line of total obedience. Caesar closes the business with "Set on and leave no ceremony out." The procession begins to move.

Then a single voice cuts through the trumpets:

> **Soothsayer:** Caesar.

Caesar stops. "Ha! Who calls?" Casca, who has already once shouted the crowd into silence, does it again: "Bid every noise be still. Peace, yet again!" Caesar, now attending, speaks a line that tells us he has actually heard something unusual: "I hear a tongue shriller than all the music / Cry ‘Caesar.’ Speak. Caesar is turned to hear." And the Soothsayer says the five words that the entire play will circle:

> **Soothsayer:** Beware the ides of March.

Caesar does not understand at first — or pretends not to: "What man is that?" It is Brutus, of all people, who repeats the warning to him: "A soothsayer bids you beware the ides of March." Caesar demands to look at the man — "Set him before me. Let me see his face." — and it is Cassius, the future instigator, who fetches him: "Fellow, come from the throng. / Look upon Caesar." Caesar gives the man a second chance: "What sayst thou to me now? Speak once again." The Soothsayer simply repeats himself, word for word: "Beware the ides of March." And Caesar delivers his verdict in three short sentences:

> **Caesar:** He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass.

A trumpet call (the stage direction says *Sennet*) and everyone leaves except Brutus and Cassius. The whole exchange is fifteen lines long. Shakespeare spends more time on the Cobbler's puns than on the prophecy of Caesar's death. That brevity is itself a choice, and we will come back to it.

**Difficult words in this passage** (learn them now; they recur in exam extracts):

| Word / phrase | Simple meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|
| soothsayer | a truth-teller; a fortune-teller or prophet | Old English *sooth* = truth. The word itself claims that what he says is true. |
| the press | the crowd, the crush of people | Not a newspaper. Caesar asks who "in the press" is calling him. |
| shriller | higher and more piercing | The voice is thin and sharp enough to cut through music. |
| ides | the middle marker-day of a Roman month | Explained fully below. |
| throng | crowd | Cassius tells the man to "come from the throng". |
| dreamer | someone who sees visions; also, dismissively, someone whose head is in the clouds | Caesar's word deliberately allows both meanings. |
| Sennet | a set of trumpet notes announcing a ceremonial entrance or exit | A stage direction, not spoken. |

### Literal meaning: what the sentence actually says

Take the sentence at its plainest. **Beware** means 'be on your guard against'. **The ides of March** is a date, and only a date: the fifteenth of March. So the literal instruction is: 'be careful on 15 March'.

Notice what the sentence does *not* say. It does not say what to fear, or from whom, or where, or how. It names a time and nothing else. This is the classic shape of a prophecy in literature — precise about one thing and silent about everything that would allow the hearer to act on it. A warning that only names a day is almost useless as advice; Caesar could stay at home on 15 March (and in Act 2 he very nearly does), but he cannot know what he is avoiding. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many critics observe that the vagueness of the warning is exactly what allows the play to test Caesar's character rather than his information. He is not given a fact to check; he is given a choice about whether to take a stranger seriously.

Notice, too, that the Soothsayer says it twice, in identical words. When Caesar asks him to "Speak once again", a lesser dramatist would have had the man elaborate. Shakespeare has him repeat. The effect is of a message that cannot be negotiated, expanded or bargained with. It simply is. The repetition is also practical stagecraft: an Elizabethan audience in a noisy open-air playhouse hears the key line twice, so no one misses it.

Finally, look at the grammar of the name. **Ides** looks like a plural, and in Latin (*Idus*) it is a plural noun — yet it names a single day. English keeps the plural form: we say 'the Ides of March *are* come', never 'is'. When you meet this in Act 3, do not think it is a mistake.

### Historical meaning: the Ides of March in the Roman calendar

::: history How Romans dated a month: Kalends, Nones, Ides
**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Romans did not count the days of a month from 1 to 30 as we do. Each month had three fixed marker-days, and every other date was reckoned by counting *backwards* from the next marker:

- **Kalends** — the first day of the month. The word comes from the Latin verb meaning 'to call out', because the priests originally announced the new month aloud. Our word *calendar* descends from it.
- **Nones** — the fifth day of most months, but the seventh in March, May, July and October. The name means 'ninth', because the Nones fell nine days before the Ides by the Roman inclusive way of counting.
- **Ides** — the thirteenth day of most months, but the **fifteenth** in March, May, July and October (the four months that were originally longer). In the oldest Roman calendar, which followed the moon, the Ides marked the full moon.

The Ides of every month were sacred to **Jupiter**, the king of the gods; a sacrifice was offered to him on that day. So the Ides of March means the fifteenth of March, a holy day of Jupiter, in the middle of what had once been the first month of the Roman year.

In **44 BC** the Ides of March was the day on which the real Julius Caesar was assassinated at a meeting of the Senate held in the Curia of Pompey, a hall attached to Pompey's great theatre complex — not, as Shakespeare has it, on the Capitol. The ancient biographers record that a seer — Suetonius names him **Spurinna**; Plutarch leaves him nameless — had warned Caesar that he faced danger which would come no later than the Ides of March. Shakespeare's Soothsayer has no name; he is Spurinna stripped down to a voice.
:::

Why does this matter to you as a reader? Because Shakespeare's Soothsayer is not inventing a superstition; he is naming a date that every educated person in the 1599 audience already knew. The Ides of March was, and remains, one of the few dates from antiquity that ordinary people can recite. That fact changes everything about how the line works in the theatre, which brings us to dramatic irony.

::: textnote The word 'ides' in your edition
Some school editions print 'Ides' with a capital letter, some without; Folger and the MIT text both use lower case within the line. Neither is wrong. In an answer, either is acceptable, but be consistent. What matters is never to write 'Ide of March' in the singular.
:::

### Dramatic irony: the audience knows the date; Caesar does not

**Dramatic irony** is the gap between what a character knows and what the audience knows. It is not the same as sarcasm or verbal irony (saying one thing and meaning another). It is a structural device: the playwright arranges for us to hold knowledge that the person on stage lacks, so that we watch every word and gesture with a double awareness.

Here the gap could hardly be wider. Caesar hears 'the ides of March' as an empty date — one more day in a busy calendar. We hear it as the day of his death. When he says "He is a dreamer", we know that the dreamer is the one person on stage who is fully awake to reality. When he says “Pass”, he is dismissing not a beggar but the truth. And there is a further twist that repays close reading: Caesar says "Caesar is turned to hear" and then does not, in any meaningful sense, hear. He demands to see the man's face — "Let me see his face" — and, having looked, sees nothing. The whole exchange is built around eyes and ears that do not do their job.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** this is one of the reasons the exchange sits so close to Cassius's mirror speech a few lines later, in which Brutus admits that "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things." Act 1 Scene 2 is full of failures of sight: Caesar looks at the Soothsayer and sees a dreamer; Brutus cannot see himself; Caesar reads Cassius's danger correctly but then boasts he does not fear him. The Soothsayer scene is the first and clearest instance of a leader looking straight at a warning and not seeing it.

The word **dreamer** rewards a second look. To an Elizabethan, a 'dreamer' could be a visionary — someone to whom truths are revealed in dreams — or simply an idle fantasist. Caesar means the second; the play proves the first. And there is a sting the audience may feel that Caesar cannot: the man who dismisses a dreamer will, in Act 2, be given a true dream by his own wife, and will dismiss that too. The pattern is set here.

There is one more layer. Dramatic irony works only if the audience genuinely knows. Shakespeare could rely on it because the death of Caesar was among the best-known stories in Europe: grammar-school boys read Caesar in Latin, Plutarch's *Lives* had been available in Sir Thomas North's English translation since 1579, and the title of the play itself promised a tragedy. In this play, then, dramatic irony is not a trick played on a few sharp spectators. It is the play's basic condition, shared by everyone in the house. You, reading it in Class 10 already knowing how Act 3 ends, are in exactly the position Shakespeare designed the play for. That is not a disadvantage; it is the intended experience.

### Foreshadowing: the difference between a prophecy and a plant

Students often use 'foreshadowing' and 'prophecy' as if they were the same thing. They are not, and the distinction earns marks.

A **prophecy** is something a *character* does: the Soothsayer predicts the future inside the story. **Foreshadowing** is something the *author* does: Shakespeare plants a hint early so that a later event feels prepared, inevitable, and rich with meaning when it arrives. The Soothsayer's line is both at once — a prophecy inside the fiction and a piece of foreshadowing in the design — which is why it is such a useful example to write about.

But the Soothsayer is only the loudest of several plants in Act 1. Some are spoken warnings; some are images; some are small facts that will grow. Flavius's fear that Caesar will "soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness" foreshadows the tyranny the conspirators claim to prevent. Brutus's confession that he is "with himself at war" foreshadows the sleepless orchard soliloquy (a speech spoken alone on stage, thinking aloud) of Act 2 and, ultimately, his self-destruction. Cassius's plan to throw "Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name" through Brutus's windows foreshadows the moment Brutus reads them. Casca's storm foreshadows Calpurnia's omens. Even the offhand news that Marullus and Flavius "are put to silence" foreshadows how this Rome will deal with dissent — with a knife, in the end, in both directions.

You will find all of these gathered in the **FORESHADOWING MAP** later in this chapter, with a third column telling you exactly what to say about each in an answer. For now, hold on to the principle: *foreshadowing is not a guessing game about the plot. It is the way a tragedy teaches its audience to feel that what happens had to happen.*

### Fate: the reading in which the Soothsayer is the voice of destiny

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** One strong and long-established reading treats the Soothsayer as the voice of **fate** — the Roman *fatum*, the thing that has been 'spoken' by the gods and cannot be unspoken. On this reading, the play's universe is one in which the future is fixed, the signs of it are everywhere (the Soothsayer, the storm, the lion, the owl at noon, Calpurnia's dream, the birds that will hover over Cassius's army before the battle of Philippi in Act 5), and human beings are free only to read the signs well or badly. Caesar reads them badly and dies. Cassius, who begins by denying the signs, ends by half-believing them, and also dies. Brutus pays the signs almost no heed until Caesar's ghost visits him in Act 4, and dies. The Soothsayer, in this reading, is the still point around which the whole machine turns: he knows, and knowing does him no good, because knowledge cannot alter fate.

There is a real philosophical puzzle buried here, and a good student should be able to state it. *Why warn a man of something that cannot be avoided?* A warning only makes sense if the future is open. Yet the play never gives Caesar a realistic chance of avoiding the Ides — every attempt to keep him home is overturned. So the Soothsayer's warning is at once an invitation to choose and a proof that choice is useless. Tragedy lives in exactly that contradiction. You do not have to resolve it. You have to be able to see it and say it.

### Free will: Cassius's counter-claim, and the play's central tension

Now set against the Soothsayer the most famous sentence Cassius speaks in Act 1, in the middle of his long attempt to win Brutus:

> **Cassius:** Men at some time are masters of their fates. / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.

**Stars** here means astrological destiny — the belief, very much alive in 1599, that the positions of the planets at one's birth shaped one's life. **Underlings** are inferiors, people beneath others. Cassius is saying: the reason we are Caesar's inferiors is not written in the heavens; it is our own fault, and therefore our own to correct. This is a declaration of **free will** — the doctrine that human beings choose and are responsible for their choices.

Here is the shape of the tension, laid out as a chain:

```chain
The Soothsayer (fate): 'the day is fixed; you can only beware'
↓
Caesar dismisses the fixed day → walks into it
↓
Cassius (free will): 'the fault is in ourselves, not our stars'
↓
Cassius acts freely → forges letters, builds a conspiracy, kills Caesar on the very day fate named
↓
The question the play leaves you with: did Cassius's free choice fulfil fate, or replace it?
```

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION — presented as interpretation, not fact.** Notice that the Soothsayer and Cassius never meet or argue, yet the play sets them side by side within a hundred lines of each other, as if to stage a debate without telling us who wins. Three observations are worth making in any essay:

1. *Cassius's free will produces exactly what fate predicted.* The conspirators choose the Ides of March for their own practical reasons, yet by choosing it they make the Soothsayer right. Free action and destiny arrive at the same place. Many critics read this as Shakespeare's refusal to decide between the two — or as his suggestion that human choices *are* the instrument through which fate works.

2. *Cassius does not even believe his own doctrine consistently.* In Act 1 Scene 3, on the night of the storm, the man who said the fault is not in our stars is busily reading the heavens: he tells Casca the prodigies (unnatural, ominous happenings) are heaven's way of making "instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state", and that the night resembles a man — Caesar. He uses omens when they are useful to his argument. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the real Cassius was a follower of Epicurus, a philosophy that dismissed omens; Shakespeare seems aware of this, because (GLANCE AHEAD to Act 5) Cassius will admit before Philippi that he has begun to give some credit to things that presage. The 'master of his fate' ends up superstitious.

3. *Brutus stands between them.* He belongs to neither camp: he is not a reader of signs like the Soothsayer and Casca, nor a proud denier of fate like Cassius. He simply acts on what he believes to be right and lets consequence follow. (GLANCE AHEAD) This is why his last words in Act 5, in which he speaks of the Ides of March as the beginning of a 'work' that the day of Philippi must end, are so moving: he adopts the Soothsayer's date as the name of his own free choice.

This is the tension that teachers and examiners usually label 'fate and free will', and *The Themes of Act 1 (Part Two)* chapter develops it across every scene. What you should take from this chapter is the pairing itself: the Soothsayer and Cassius are the two poles, and Caesar and Brutus are the men crushed between them.

### Caesar's response: three sentences and a gesture

> **Caesar:** He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass.

Read this aloud. Eleven words, three sentences, almost every word a single syllable. After the ceremonial fullness of "Set on and leave no ceremony out" and the alert curiosity of "Caesar is turned to hear", the rhythm snaps shut. Each sentence is shorter than the one before: a judgement, a decision, a command. The last word, “Pass”, is an order to the whole procession — and to the future — to move along.

What does this reveal? Three things, which you can use in any question on Caesar's character.

**First, the public Caesar.** He speaks of himself in the third person — "Caesar is turned to hear" — a habit that will grow through the play until he talks about 'Caesar' as if the name belonged to a statue rather than a man. The Soothsayer scene is where you first hear it clearly. It is the voice of a man who has become his own public image.

**Second, the judge by appearances.** Caesar's instinct, faced with a warning, is to *look at the man* — "Set him before me. Let me see his face." He believes that he can read danger in a face; he will say so at length about Cassius later in the scene, when he calls for "men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights." His reading of the Soothsayer's face produces the word 'dreamer'. It is the wrong reading, and the audience knows it; the play has quietly told us that Caesar's confidence in his own eye is misplaced, a full act before it kills him.

**Third, the man who cannot afford to be seen fearing.** Consider where he is: in the public square, on a feast day, with the crowd watching. A leader who stops a procession because a stranger shouted a date would look frightened. “Pass” protects the image. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many readers feel that Caesar's dismissiveness is not stupidity but performance — that he must be seen to disdain fear because, as he says later of himself, "always I am Caesar". This reading makes his fate more tragic, not less: he dies of the role he has to play.

**HISTORICAL RECORD, for a deeper point:** the real Caesar held the office of *pontifex maximus*, the chief priest of Rome, who presided over the state religion — including the colleges of priests whose business was the reading of omens. Shakespeare does not mention this, but the historically informed reader sees an additional irony: the head of Rome's official religion brushing aside a reader of signs.

Now look at who else speaks in this exchange, because it is the detail that separates a good answer from an excellent one.

> **Brutus:** A soothsayer bids you beware the ides of March.

Why Brutus? Shakespeare had a stage full of people who could relay the words; he chose the man who will strike Caesar on that day. There is nothing sinister in Brutus's tone — he is simply being helpful, standing close enough to Caesar to answer for him, which tells us how intimate the two men are. But the audience hears the future assassin pronouncing the sentence of death in the form of a courtesy. And then Cassius, the instigator, physically brings the prophet forward: "Fellow, come from the throng. / Look upon Caesar." The warning is framed between the two men who will make it come true. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the text leaves their inner states unreadable here — this is before Cassius has said a word of temptation — and that is precisely why the framing works. Neither man yet knows he is the danger being announced.

### Audience knowledge: why a play with no suspense is still gripping

A film director once distinguished between *surprise* and *suspense*. Surprise is a bomb going off under a table without warning: a few seconds of shock. Suspense is showing the audience the bomb under the table and then letting two characters chat about the weather for ten minutes: every trivial word becomes unbearable, because we know and they do not. *Julius Caesar* is a suspense play in this sense, and the Soothsayer is the moment Shakespeare shows us the bomb.

From this line onward the audience carries a date. Every scene in Acts 1 and 2 is measured against it. When Cicero asks Casca in the storm, "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?", the word *tomorrow* lands with a weight it would not otherwise have, because we can count. When Cassius tells Cinna it is "after midnight", we know which day has just begun. Shakespeare has turned his entire audience into soothsayers: we all know what Caesar does not, and we watch him with the helpless clarity of the man he called a dreamer.

::: insight Why the Soothsayer appears so early
Caesar has been on stage for barely a dozen lines — a summons to his wife, an order where she should stand, an instruction to Antony, a command to the procession — when the Soothsayer's voice interrupts. Shakespeare gives us the prophecy of the death before we have properly met the man who will die. Why not save it, build Caesar up, and then puncture him?

Three answers. First, *the play is not a mystery*. Shakespeare knows that his audience knows the ending; pretending otherwise would be false. By announcing the date at once he converts a plot into a countdown, and a countdown creates a far more intense kind of attention than a puzzle does. Second, *character before biography*. Shakespeare never shows us Caesar's conquests; he shows us Caesar dismissing a warning. The first substantial thing this Caesar does is refuse to listen, and that single act tells us more about why he will die than any account of Gaul could. Third, and most subtly, *the source of the warning*. The voice comes from "the press", from the anonymous crowd, not from a senator or a priest. Truth in this play speaks from below — a nameless man, later a poet in the street — and the great men do not hear it. Placing the Soothsayer in the first minutes of Caesar's entrance establishes the pattern that will destroy him: the people at the top of Rome are surrounded by information they are too grand to use.
:::

### The five levels: how to hold this moment in your head

::: level LEVEL 1 — What happened?
During the Lupercal procession, a soothsayer in the crowd calls out to Caesar and warns him, twice, to beware the ides of March. Brutus repeats the warning; Cassius brings the man forward; Caesar looks at him, calls him "a dreamer", and orders the procession to move on.
:::

::: level LEVEL 2 — What does it mean?
The Ides of March is 15 March, the historical day of Caesar's assassination. The warning names the day without explaining the danger. Caesar's dismissal shows his public confidence, his reliance on reading faces, and his refusal to appear afraid in front of the crowd.
:::

::: level LEVEL 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way?
Because the audience already knows the date, the scene creates dramatic irony rather than suspense about *what* will happen; the interest shifts to *how* and *why*. The brevity and repetition make the warning feel absolute. Giving the relaying line to Brutus and the fetching line to Cassius quietly frames the warning between the two men who will fulfil it. Placing it in Caesar's first minutes on stage makes dismissal of warnings the very first thing we learn about him.
:::

::: level LEVEL 4 — How does it connect to the whole play?
It opens the fate-versus-free-will argument that Cassius answers with "Men at some time are masters of their fates." It begins Caesar's pattern of ignoring warnings (Calpurnia's dream, the augurers — the priests who read omens — and Artemidorus's letter in Act 2). The Soothsayer returns in Act 2 Scene 4 and Act 3 Scene 1, where Caesar's remark that the Ides have come is answered with the reminder that they have not yet gone. Brutus, in Act 5, names the Ides of March as the beginning of the 'work' his death must finish.
:::

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
In a Class IX extract question on this passage, the sub-questions are likely to ask where the scene is set and what is happening, what the Soothsayer's words mean, why Caesar reacts as he does, what his reaction reveals about him, and what happens immediately afterwards (Brutus and Cassius are left alone). For Class X use, see the exam box below. In either case, always connect: 'Caesar's dismissal of the Soothsayer in Act 1 is the first of several warnings he ignores.'
:::

::: icse Where this actually earns marks
**EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE.** Act 1 belongs to the Class IX portion of the syllabus, so the Soothsayer passage itself is a school-examination extract, not a board-paper one. But the 2025 ICSE board paper asked candidates to explain what 'the work the ides of March begun' refers to — a Class X question that cannot be answered without this Act 1 scene. Expect the phrase 'the ides of March' to surface in Acts III and V extracts, and be ready to explain in one clean sentence: the Soothsayer's warning in Act 1 Scene 2, the date of the assassination in Act 3 Scene 1, and Brutus's use of the phrase before Philippi. No question is guaranteed; the knowledge is.
:::

::: weak A weak answer to 'Why does Caesar dismiss the Soothsayer?'
'Caesar dismisses the Soothsayer because he is arrogant and does not believe in fortune-tellers. He calls him a dreamer and goes away. This shows he is overconfident.'
:::

::: strong An excellent answer to the same question
'Caesar dismisses the Soothsayer with three short, dismissive sentences — "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." — after insisting on looking at the man's face. His response reveals his habit of judging by appearances and his need, in a crowded public square on a feast day, not to be seen taking fright at a stranger's words; he speaks of himself in the third person as "Caesar", the public image rather than the man. Because the audience knows that the Ides of March is the day of his death, the dismissal is heavy with dramatic irony: the man he calls a dreamer is the only one awake to the truth. It is also the first instance of a pattern — he will later brush aside Calpurnia's dream and the augurers — so the moment characterises Caesar and foreshadows his fall at once.'
:::

Notice what the strong answer does that the weak one does not: it quotes exactly, it names the device (dramatic irony) and explains *how* it works here, it reads a detail (the third person, the demand to see the face), and it connects forward. Four moves, one paragraph.

### Relationship to later events: where the Ides of March goes next

Everything below is a **GLANCE AHEAD** beyond the verified Act 1 text. Later-act wording is given only as paraphrase in single quotes; do not quote these as if they were exact.

**GLANCE AHEAD — Act 2 Scene 1.** Brutus, unable to sleep, has his servant check the calendar for the date; the conspirators gather in his orchard before dawn. The Ides are now hours away, and Brutus is the one counting.

**GLANCE AHEAD — Act 2 Scene 2.** Calpurnia — the woman whose pale cheek Brutus noticed in Act 1 — has dreamt of Caesar's death and begs him not to go to the Capitol. The priests' sacrifice yields a bad omen. Caesar wavers, then lets Decius Brutus reinterpret the dream as a lucky one, and goes. This is the Soothsayer scene replayed at length: warning, hesitation, dismissal. What Act 1 did in fifteen lines, Act 2 does in a whole scene, so that the audience can watch the pattern harden into fate.

**GLANCE AHEAD — Act 2 Scene 3 and Scene 4.** Artemidorus writes Caesar a letter naming the conspirators — a warning that, unlike the Soothsayer's, gives real information — and Caesar will not read it. Then the Soothsayer himself returns, met by Portia in the street; he tells her he fears what may happen to Caesar and goes to find a place from which to speak to him again. Shakespeare brings back the nameless man so that the Ides do not arrive without their prophet.

**GLANCE AHEAD — Act 3 Scene 1.** On the way to the Senate, Caesar sees the Soothsayer and, in effect, taunts him: the Ides of March have come. The Soothsayer answers that they have come, but not yet gone. Within a few minutes Caesar is dead. The exchange is the direct payoff of Act 1 Scene 2, and examiners writing on Act 3 extracts assume you know its origin.

**GLANCE AHEAD — Act 5 Scene 1.** Before Philippi, Brutus tells Cassius that this same day must end 'that work the ides of March begun'. The date has become the name of the entire enterprise — the assassination, the civil war, and his own approaching death. The Soothsayer's five words have travelled from a warning to Caesar into the epitaph (the words written over a grave) of the men who killed him.

| Cassius: "Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name" (Act 1 Scene 2) | Brutus reads the forged letters in his orchard in Act 2 Scene 1 and is pushed over the edge. | Deception at the root of the 'honourable' conspiracy; Cinna carries the papers in 1.3. |
| Casca: "Marullus and Flavius... are put to silence." (Act 1 Scene 2) | Caesar's intolerance of dissent; the mob's silencing of Cinna the poet in Act 3 Scene 3. | Casual delivery of a chilling fact; the first casualty of Caesar's power; the republican tribunate under attack. |
| Casca on the crowd: "as they use to do the players in the theatre" (Act 1 Scene 2) | The crowd is swayed by Brutus, then reversed by Antony, in Act 3 Scene 2; it tears a poet apart in 3.3. | The crowd as an audience that applauds whoever performs best; the decisive force of the play has no fixed loyalty. |
| The storm and prodigies (Act 1 Scene 3) | Calpurnia lists the same kind of portents in Act 2 Scene 2; Caesar's ghost appears in Act 4. | The supernatural as warning; characters 'construe' the signs to suit themselves. |
| Cicero: "men may construe things after their fashion" (Act 1 Scene 3) | Decius construes the dream; Brutus construes Caesar's ambition; the crowd construes Antony's speech. | The interpretive key to the whole play: meaning is made by the reader of the sign. |
| Cassius: "Men at some time are masters of their fates." (Act 1 Scene 2) | Cassius dies at Philippi on his birthday, having misread a battlefield report; he admits he has begun to credit omens. | The free-will pole of the play's central tension; ironically fulfilled and undercut at once. |
| Casca: "he sits high in all the people's hearts... like richest alchemy" (Act 1 Scene 3) | Brutus's reputation legitimises the murder in Act 3 and briefly wins the crowd. | The conspiracy needs Brutus's name more than his sword; explains why Cassius works so hard on him. |

## PART B — THE SUPPORTING CAST OF ACT 1

A supporting character is not a minor character. In a play as economical as this one, every named person who speaks is doing a job the protagonists cannot do for themselves. For each figure below you will find the same three things: the **evidence** (what the text actually gives you), the **function** (why Shakespeare needs this person here), and the **exam value** (what an examiner reasonably expects you to know, with honesty about the Class IX / Class X split).

### Antony: the loyal executor

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Antony speaks four times in Act 1, six lines in all, and is spoken *about* far more than he speaks. The stage direction brings him on "for the course" — stripped for the ritual race of the Lupercal. Caesar addresses him by the formal 'Antonius' and gives him a task; Antony's reply is the defining line:

> **Antony:** I shall remember. / When Caesar says ‘Do this,’ it is performed.

Later in the scene, Caesar calls him again — "Antonius." — "Caesar." — and confides his suspicion of Cassius. Antony answers, "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given." (**well given** = well disposed, of good character.) Caesar's reply tells us what Caesar sees in Antony: "He loves no plays, / As thou dost, Antony; he hears no music" — Antony is a lover of plays and music, a man of pleasure. Brutus has already contrasted himself with him: "I am not gamesome. I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony" (**gamesome** = fond of sport; **quick spirit** = liveliness). And in Casca's report the crown was offered by "Mark Antony" — Casca's plain answer to "Who offered him the crown?" is "Why, Antony." In Act 1 Scene 3 he is Caesar's messenger even offstage: Caesar "did bid Antonius / Send word" to Cicero about the Senate meeting.

**FUNCTION.** Antony in Act 1 is the executor — the man who turns Caesar's words into deeds. Notice that his defining line is grammatically impersonal: not 'I perform it' but "it is performed", as if the doing were automatic, without an agent. He is Caesar's body in public (the runner in the race), Caesar's hand in ceremony (the crown-offerer), Caesar's ear in private (the confidant told to "Come on my right hand"). Everyone else in the scene defines him by contrast: Brutus is grave where Antony is playful; Cassius is lean and reads much where Antony loves plays and sleeps at night — Caesar's ideal of the safe man, "Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights", is more or less a portrait of Antony.

There is one moment where the executor's judgement is tested, and he fails: "he's not dangerous." Antony misreads Cassius. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the text leaves open whether this is genuine naivety or the soothing diplomacy of a man who tells his master what calms him. Either way it matters: in Act 1, the shrewdest political mind in the play appears to be Caesar's, not Antony's.

**What Act 1 plants for Act 3.** Three seeds. First, *loyalty as identity*: the funeral oration is Antony doing, on a vast scale, what he promised in one line — performing Caesar's will after Caesar can no longer speak it. Second, *the theatre-lover*: Caesar's remark that Antony loves plays is, in retrospect, a professional description; the man who will hold up Caesar's torn mantle (cloak) and make a crowd weep understands performance as Brutus does not. The crown-offering in Casca's report is his rehearsal — a public show staged for a crowd, with Caesar as the actor. Third, *Brutus's misreading*: because Brutus sees in Antony only "quick spirit" and gamesomeness, he will (GLANCE AHEAD) refuse to have him killed in Act 2 and allow him to speak in Act 3. Act 1 shows you exactly why Brutus makes that mistake.

**EXAM VALUE.** In Class X board questions on Antony's oration (Act 3 Scene 2), sub-questions asking what Antony had done for Caesar 'earlier', or why Brutus underestimated him, are answered from Act 1: he offered the crown, he was Caesar's loyal executor, he was known as a lover of plays and sport. Be able to quote "When Caesar says ‘Do this,’ it is performed" and explain that Antony's obedience in Act 1 becomes his vengeance in Act 3.

::: insight Why Antony's few Act 1 lines are enough to set up Act 3
Six lines seem a slender foundation for the man who will turn the play upside down. But Shakespeare is not building Antony's *character* in Act 1; he is building everyone else's *misreading* of him, and that is what Act 3 needs.

Watch how the information arrives. We do not see Antony offer the crown — we hear it through Casca, who thinks the whole affair was "mere foolery". We do not hear Antony reason — we hear Caesar say that he loves plays and music, a remark meant as a compliment to Antony's harmlessness. We hear Brutus set himself apart from Antony's "quick spirit" as a serious man distances himself from a sportsman. Every lens through which we see Antony in Act 1 diminishes him, and every lens belongs to a man who will pay for the error. The one line he speaks in his own voice, "it is performed", sounds like servility. Only afterwards does it read as a promise.

This is the technique: plant a character in the *blind spot* of the other characters. The audience, who knows the story, registers the irony; the conspirators, who do not, walk past him. When Antony finally speaks at length in Act 3, the shock is theirs, not ours — and Act 1 is the reason we were never fooled.
:::

### Casca: cynic in the sunlight, coward in the storm, first recruit at midnight

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Casca has three faces in Act 1, and the exam wants you to know all three.

*In the procession* he is Caesar's crowd-controller, the officious (self-importantly busy) voice twice demanding quiet: "Peace, ho! Caesar speaks." and "Bid every noise be still. Peace, yet again!"

*After the games*, alone with Brutus and Cassius, he is the play's first narrator, and a sour one. Cassius has warned that Casca will tell the news "after his sour fashion". His account of the crown-offering is dripping with contempt for everyone involved: "It was mere foolery; I did not mark it." Caesar "put it by with the back of his hand, thus" — a gesture Casca mimics. The crown "'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets" (a **coronet** is a small, lesser crown) — he belittles even the object. Yet Casca is a sharp observer despite himself: "to my thinking, he would fain have had it"; Caesar "was very loath to lay his fingers off it" (**fain** = gladly; **loath** = reluctant). His contempt for the crowd is physical — "the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps and uttered such a deal of stinking breath" — and so is his joke: "I durst not laugh for fear of opening my lips and receiving the bad air." Asked about Cicero, he claims ignorance: "Ay, he spoke Greek", and "for mine own part, it was Greek to me." Even his acceptance of a dinner invitation is grudging: "Ay, if I be alive, and your mind hold, and your dinner worth the eating."

Once he is gone, Brutus and Cassius supply the key to him. Brutus: "What a blunt fellow is this grown to be! / He was quick mettle when he went to school." (**quick mettle** = lively spirit, keen temperament.) Cassius: "So is he now in execution / Of any bold or noble enterprise, / However he puts on this tardy form. / This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit" (**tardy form** = a sluggish manner; **sauce** = seasoning that sharpens). The sullenness, Cassius says, is a costume.

*In the storm* of Act 1 Scene 3 the costume falls away. Casca enters with his sword drawn — "I ha' not since put up my sword" — and breathless: "Are not you moved, when all the sway of earth / Shakes like a thing unfirm?" The prose cynic now speaks verse, and frightened verse. He believes the portents (signs of coming disaster) mean something: "It is the part of men to fear and tremble / When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us." And yet, when Cassius works on him, he pledges himself first of anyone we actually see recruited on stage: "You speak to Casca, and to such a man / That is no fleering telltale. Hold. My hand. / Be factious for redress of all these griefs, / And I will set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest." (**fleering** = sneering, mocking; **telltale** = informer; **factious** = active in forming a faction.) He also shows he understands why Brutus matters: "he sits high in all the people's hearts", and his reputation, "like richest alchemy", will turn the crime into virtue.

**FUNCTION.** Casca does two jobs no one else can. First, he is the *filter* through which we receive the crown scene. Shakespeare could have staged Antony offering the coronet; he chose instead to have it *reported*, by the most jaundiced (bitter, prejudiced) witness available. The result is that the audience never sees Caesar refuse the crown — it sees Casca's Caesar, who "would fain have had it", performing for a herd. Whether that is fair to Caesar is something the text deliberately leaves open. Second, Casca is the *weather-vane of Roman nerve*: the man who scorned the crowd's fear is himself terrified by the storm, and it is fear, as much as principle, that Cassius turns into commitment. Watching Casca recruited is watching how a conspiracy actually grows: not by argument alone but by shared dread and the offer of a hand.

**The prose–verse shift** is a genuine textual fact worth a sentence in any answer: Casca reports the games in prose (the register — the level and tone of language — of cynicism and gossip) and describes the storm in verse (the register of awe). The form changes because the man does.

**On 'Greek to me'.** Educated Romans of Cicero's class spoke Greek fluently; it was the language of philosophy and high culture. When Casca says Cicero's remark "was Greek to me", he may be confessing genuine ignorance, or — given Brutus's memory of his schooldays — playing the blunt soldier who will not admit to learning. The phrase has since become an English idiom for anything incomprehensible, and this line did more than any other to fix it in the language.

**HISTORICAL RECORD and GLANCE AHEAD.** The historical Publius Servilius Casca Longus struck the first blow at the assassination, and Shakespeare keeps this: in Act 3 Scene 1 it is Casca who strikes first. The first recruit becomes the first hand. In an Act 3 extract question, being able to say that Casca's readiness was already visible in his pledge to Cassius in Act 1 Scene 3 marks you out.

**EXAM VALUE.** Class IX extracts on Casca's report are common in school papers: expect sub-questions on why he calls the offering "foolery", what "Greek to me" means, what the report reveals about Caesar and about the crowd, and how Brutus and Cassius describe Casca afterwards. In Class X, Casca's first blow and his role in the storm scene are the usual points of contact.

### Cicero: the sceptic who is never asked

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Cicero is on stage throughout Act 1 Scene 2 and never speaks. We see him only through Brutus's eyes when the procession returns: "Cicero / Looks with such ferret and such fiery eyes / As we have seen him in the Capitol, / Being crossed in conference by some senators." (**ferret** eyes = small, red and angry, like the animal's; **crossed in conference** = contradicted in debate.) Casca reports that at the games "he spoke Greek", and that "those that understood him smiled at one another and shook their heads" — an educated, coded disapproval that the crowd, and Casca, could not follow.

In Act 1 Scene 3 he speaks at last, and every line is cool where Casca's is hot. He opens with plain questions — "Good even, Casca. Brought you Caesar home? / Why are you breathless? And why stare you so?" — and, when Casca pours out the prodigies, he answers with the most quietly important sentence in the scene:

> **Cicero:** Indeed, it is a strange-disposèd time. / But men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves.

(**construe** = interpret; **clean from** = entirely away from.) Then a practical question — "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" — and a sensible exit: "Good night then, Casca. This disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in." He leaves before Cassius enters. He is never in the same conversation as the conspiracy.

**FUNCTION.** Cicero is, for most readers, the play's voice of reason, and Shakespeare uses him to state, once, the rule by which the whole play operates: signs mean what their readers decide they mean. Casca reads the storm as divine anger; Cassius, minutes later, reads it as a portrait of Caesar and a licence to kill; (GLANCE AHEAD) Decius will read Calpurnia's dream as good fortune, Brutus will read Caesar's future as tyranny, the crowd will read Antony's tears as truth. Cicero names the mechanism and then walks out of the play's action, leaving everyone else to demonstrate it. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many critics see in his exit a judgement — that the one man capable of standing outside the fever of interpretation has no place in the story that follows. The text does not say this; the staging suggests it.

His Greek matters too. It marks him as the intellectual, the man whose criticism of Caesar takes the form of a witty remark in a learned language rather than a dagger. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the real Cicero (106–43 BC) was Rome's greatest orator, fluent in Greek, and was not part of the conspiracy; he was killed on the orders of the triumvirs (the three men — Antony, Octavius and Lepidus — who seized power after Caesar's death) in December 43 BC.

**GLANCE AHEAD — his exclusion.** In Act 2 Scene 1 the conspirators debate whether to invite Cicero; Brutus rejects him on the grounds that he will never follow anything other men begin. The sceptic of Act 1 Scene 3 is kept out for exactly the quality Act 1 showed us: he thinks for himself.

**EXAM VALUE.** Class IX extracts from the storm scene regularly ask what Cicero means by construing things "after their fashion" and how his attitude differs from Casca's. In Class X, the question of why Cicero was left out of the conspiracy touches Act 2, but the character evidence for the answer is here.

### Cinna: the messenger of the letters (not the poet)

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Cinna enters Act 1 Scene 3 late and in a hurry. Cassius identifies him in the dark by his walk — "'Tis Cinna; I do know him by his gait. / He is a friend." — and Cinna's first words show a nervous man checking who is present: "To find out you. Who's that? Metellus Cimber?" He has felt the night's terror too: "What a fearful night is this! / There's two or three of us have seen strange sights." Then he says the thing the entire conspiracy is thinking: "O Cassius, if you could / But win the noble Brutus to our party—". Cassius cuts him off and loads him with the forged letters — "take this paper, / And look you lay it in the Praetor's chair, / Where Brutus may but find it; and throw this / In at his window; set this up with wax / Upon old Brutus' statue." Cinna obeys: "Well, I will hie / And so bestow these papers as you bade me." (**hie** = hurry; **bestow** = place.)

**FUNCTION.** Cinna is the hand that makes Cassius's soliloquy real. At the end of Act 1 Scene 2 Cassius *planned* to throw writings "in at his windows" as if "from several citizens"; in Act 1 Scene 3 the plan becomes paper in a man's hands, and the three placements — the praetor's chair (Brutus's official seat), his window (his private house), the statue of his ancestor who drove out the kings — are aimed at Brutus's public role, his private conscience and his family honour in turn. Cinna's line about winning "the noble Brutus" also confirms, from a second mouth, that the conspiracy wants Brutus's *name*. He and Casca ("richest alchemy") say the same thing in the same scene; Cassius's whole campaign is thus shown to be the will of the group, not one man's obsession.

**Distinguish him from Cinna the poet.** This Cinna is **Lucius Cornelius Cinna**, a conspirator (**HISTORICAL RECORD:** praetor in 44 BC). (GLANCE AHEAD) In Act 3 Scene 3 an entirely different man, Cinna the poet, is seized by the mob after the funeral; he protests that he is not the conspirator, and the crowd kills him for his name anyway. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** this is one of the play's cruellest ironies, and it reaches straight back to Act 1 Scene 2, where Cassius argued that a name is only a sound — "‘Brutus’ and ‘Caesar’—what should be in that ‘Caesar’?" The mob answers him: a name is enough to kill for. Students who confuse the two Cinnas lose marks on an easy MCQ; students who *connect* them gain marks on a hard essay.

**EXAM VALUE.** Multiple-choice and short recall questions ('Who was given the letters to place for Brutus?') are the likeliest form. Any Act 3 Scene 3 extract on Cinna the poet expects you to say who the other Cinna was and what he did in Act 1.

### Flavius and Marullus: the tribunes who are 'put to silence'

The tribunes are examined at length in *Act 1 Scene 1 — The Street*; here we look only at what Act 1 does with them *after* their scene. In Act 1 Scene 1 they scatter the holiday crowd — "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!" — and set off to strip Caesar's statues: "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies." Their motive is republican fear: to pluck the "growing feathers" from Caesar's wing before he can "soar above the view of men".

Then they disappear, and their end arrives as an afterthought in Casca's mouth: "I could tell you more news too: Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence." (**scarves** = the decorations on the statues; **put to silence** = deprived of the power to speak in public — deposed from office, or worse.)

**FUNCTION.** Two things. First, the *manner* of the telling. Casca treats the destruction of two tribunes as gossip, on a level with "more foolery". The offhandedness is Shakespeare's point: in this Rome, the silencing of elected officials is a small item after the sports report. Second, the *institution*. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** tribunes of the plebs were sacrosanct — their persons were protected by religious law — and their office existed to shield the people from the powerful; the historical tribunes Marullus and Flavus were deposed by Caesar in 44 BC after removing a diadem (a royal headband, the Greek sign of kingship) from his statue and arresting men who hailed him as king. Whatever "put to silence" means precisely (the text does not say, and you should not pretend it does), the fact that Caesar can silence tribunes at all is the clearest evidence in Act 1 that the Republic's safeguards are failing. It is the only concrete exercise of Caesar's power inside Rome that we hear of in the whole act — and it is exactly the kind of act the conspirators claim to be preventing.

::: textnote The tribunes in the Act 1 Scene 2 stage direction
The Folger text, following the First Folio, brings Marullus and Flavius on at the start of Act 1 Scene 2 "after them" — silently trailing Caesar's procession, moments after they had told the crowd to disperse. The MIT (Globe) text omits them. Editors differ about whether they should appear; if they do, the staging is pointed: the two men who tried to stop the celebration walk in the celebrant's train, and by the end of the scene they have been silenced. Your school edition may or may not list them; do not build an answer on the stage direction alone.
:::

**EXAM VALUE.** Class IX extracts from Act 1 Scene 1 almost always ask what the tribunes fear and what they decide to do; being able to add, from Act 1 Scene 2, what happened to them — and that we learn it only through Casca's throwaway report — turns a plot answer into a character-and-theme answer. In Class X, the tribunes are the first evidence to cite whenever a question on Acts III–V asks whether the conspirators' fear of Caesar's tyranny was justified.

### The Cobbler and the Carpenter: the crowd with faces

They are fully treated in the Scene 1 chapter; here is the point that matters for Act 1 as a whole. The Cobbler and the Carpenter are the *only* ordinary Romans in Act 1 who speak. They speak prose, in their "best apparel", on a working day, and their reason is simple: "we make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph." The Cobbler is witty — "a mender of bad soles", "I can mend you", a "surgeon to old shoes" — and he holds his own against two magistrates for a dozen lines. Then Marullus turns the full force of oratory on the crowd — "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!", "Knew you not Pompey?" — and they fold: "They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness."

**FUNCTION.** Shakespeare gives the crowd a face and a voice *before* he turns it into a roar. Because we have met a cobbler who can pun and reason, we know that the crowd is not stupid. Because we have watched him fall silent under a well-made speech, we know it is movable. That double knowledge — intelligent, pliable — is what Act 1 Scene 2 and (GLANCE AHEAD) Act 3 Scene 2 depend on. Without the Cobbler, the "tag-rag people" of Casca's report would be a mere caricature; with him, they are us.

**EXAM VALUE.** For Class IX, the Cobbler's exchange with the tribunes is a natural Act 1 extract for school papers: expect to explain his puns ("soles", "mend you", "recover", "awl"), to say why the tribunes are angry, and to describe the mood of the crowd before and after Marullus speaks. For Class X, the Cobbler is the face you should give the crowd whenever an Act 3 Scene 2 or Act 3 Scene 3 question asks what the plebeians are like — intelligent, good-humoured, easily swayed — because the board extracts on the mob assume you already know this.

### Calpurnia: three words and a pale cheek

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Calpurnia speaks three words in Act 1: "Here, my lord." Caesar summons her twice by name, positions her in the runners' path, and explains why in front of the whole procession: "The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse." Later Brutus notes, in the returning train, that "Calpurnia's cheek is pale".

**What she stands for.** Four things, each of which can anchor a paragraph.

1. *The heir Caesar does not have.* Caesar announces his wife's childlessness publicly, as a matter of state ritual. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** a republican magistrate does not need an heir; a *king* does. Caesar's anxious concern with fertility is, to many readers, the first quiet sign that he already thinks dynastically — of founding a ruling family — before anyone has offered him a crown. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the marriage was indeed childless; Caesar's only legitimate child, Julia, had died in 54 BC, and his will would adopt his great-nephew Octavius. Audiences in 1599 may also have felt the resonance with their own ageing, unmarried, heirless queen, though you should say 'may have felt', not 'felt'.

2. *The private Caesar.* Calpurnia is the only glimpse Act 1 gives of Caesar at home. The glimpse is not tender: he commands, she answers, and her body is made the site of a public ritual.

3. *Obedient silence.* "Here, my lord." is total submission. (GLANCE AHEAD) In Act 2 Scene 2 she will find a voice — pleading, arguing, kneeling — and lose. Act 1 shows the silence that Act 2 breaks.

4. *The barometer.* Her pale cheek is the first sign the audience receives that something has gone wrong at the games, before Casca tells us what. Calpurnia registers danger in her body before anyone speaks of it; her dream in Act 2 is the same instinct raised to prophecy. She and the Soothsayer are the play's two readers of danger whom Caesar overrules.

**EXAM VALUE.** In Class X, Act 2 Scene 2 is not on the board paper either, but Calpurnia's dream is referred to in Acts III–V; for Class IX, extract questions on the opening of Act 1 Scene 2 may ask why Caesar wants Antony to touch her and what this reveals. The word "barren" and the phrase "sterile curse" should be explained exactly.

### The Crowd as a character

Treat the Roman crowd as a single character with an arc, because Shakespeare does.

**Act 1 Scene 1 — celebration, then shame.** The crowd is on holiday, in its best clothes, to "rejoice in his triumph". The tribunes remind it of Pompey and it slinks home; Flavius reads the retreat as guilt — "See whe'er their basest mettle be not moved."

**Act 1 Scene 2 — the crowd as sound.** Now the crowd never appears. It is a noise offstage — the stage directions say *Flourish and shout*, *Shout. Flourish* — and Brutus and Cassius interpret the noise: "What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." … "Another general shout!" The crowd has become a force, felt through walls. Then Casca gives it back its body in the ugliest terms in the play: "the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps"; "the tag-rag people"; "the common herd"; the "Three or four wenches" who "cried ‘Alas, good soul!’ and forgave him with all their hearts" — and of whom Casca says, "if Caesar had stabbed their mothers, they would have done no less." His sarcastic "mine honest neighbours" seals the contempt.

**The theatre metaphor.** Casca's most important sentence is this: "If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre, I am no true man." The crowd is an *audience*; Caesar is a *player*. This is why he "plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut" (a **doublet** is a close-fitting Elizabethan jacket — Shakespeare dresses his Romans in the clothes of his own London) — a theatrical gesture to a house — and why, recovering from his fit, "he desired their Worships to think it was his infirmity": a performer apologising to his public in mock-humble language (calling commoners 'Worships' as if they were magistrates). **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the Globe's own audience, standing in the yard in their own sweaty caps, was being described to itself. The play knows that political power in Rome — and perhaps in London — is a matter of who performs best for the crowd. Casca despises the crowd; the play does not, because it knows the crowd decides.

**Decisive without a face.** Everything that matters in Act 1 is a reaction to what the crowd wants: Brutus fears they will "Choose Caesar for their king"; Cassius needs Brutus because "he sits high in all the people's hearts". Caesar, in Casca's report, offers the crowd his throat the moment he perceives that "the common herd was glad he refused the crown" — he is reading the room. (GLANCE AHEAD) In Act 3 the same crowd will be talked into approving the murder and then talked into avenging it in the space of a single scene.

**EXAM VALUE.** The crowd is the Act 1 'character' most likely to matter in the Class X paper, because Act 3 Scene 2 — the funeral orations — is a mob scene, and sub-questions on it often ask what the crowd's behaviour reveals. A sentence of Act 1 evidence — the holiday crowd shamed by Marullus, the offstage shouts, Casca's "players in the theatre" — shows the examiner that you understand the crowd as a consistent character across the play, not a fresh invention of Act 3.

::: insight Why Shakespeare gives the crowd no individual names in 1.2 yet makes it decisive
In Act 1 Scene 1 we meet a Cobbler and a Carpenter — men with trades, jokes and a reason to be in the street. In Act 1 Scene 2 those men are gone. The crowd is "the press", "the throng", an offstage shout, "the rabblement", "the tag-rag people", "the common herd", "wenches". Not one commoner speaks.

The change is deliberate and it works in two directions at once. Towards the *patricians* (the aristocratic families who ran Rome), it shows us how they see the people: as a mass, a noise, a smell — Casca's "stinking breath" is the aristocrat's whole political philosophy in two words. Brutus, Cassius and Casca never once refer to a citizen as an individual, and their conspiracy to save 'Rome' will be made without consulting a single Roman of the kind who makes shoes. Towards the *audience*, it shows us the crowd's real nature: it is not a person but a weather-system, and the men on stage are constantly checking the sky. Every shout changes the conversation between Brutus and Cassius; Caesar's every gesture at the games is calibrated to it.

So the crowd is nameless *and* decisive for the same reason: it is the medium in which power exists. Give it a face and it becomes a character who can be argued with. Take the face away and it becomes the thing that everyone is arguing *about*. (GLANCE AHEAD) When individual voices return in Act 3 — 'First Plebeian', 'Second Plebeian' — they are still nameless, and the first person they name, they kill.
:::

::: revision Ten things to carry out of this chapter
1. The Ides of March = 15 March, a marker-day sacred to Jupiter; Caesar died on it in 44 BC.
2. The Soothsayer speaks the warning twice, in identical words; Brutus relays it; Cassius fetches the man.
3. "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." — three shrinking sentences; the public Caesar; judging by faces.
4. Dramatic irony: the audience knows the date; Caesar hears only a date.
5. Prophecy is what a character does; foreshadowing is what the author does. The Soothsayer is both.
6. Fate (the Soothsayer) versus free will (Cassius: "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars") is the play's central tension, and the conspirators' free choice fulfils the fated date.
7. Antony: four speeches, six lines; the executor whom everyone underestimates.
8. Casca: prose cynic in 1.2, verse coward in 1.3, first recruit, first blow in Act 3.
9. Cicero: "men may construe things after their fashion" — the key to how every sign in the play is read; excluded later for thinking for himself.
10. The crowd: named and witty in 1.1, nameless and decisive in 1.2; an audience that claps and hisses "the players in the theatre".
