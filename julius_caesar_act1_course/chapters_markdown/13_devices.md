# The Act 1 Literary-Device Bank

*This chapter is a working reference, not a list to memorise. For every literary device that genuinely appears in Act 1 of* Julius Caesar *it gives you the same seven things in the same order: the term, a plain definition, one verified Act 1 example with speaker and scene, what the words mean, what effect they create, why Shakespeare needed the device at that exact moment, and a model sentence showing how to name and analyse it in an ICSE answer. A finder table of about twenty-five quotations follows, then a worked lesson on writing about Cassius's Colossus image. Use the bank alongside the scene chapters: the device is only ever a tool for explaining what the play is doing.*

## How to use this bank

Students who have struggled with Shakespeare usually make one of two mistakes with devices. The first is to treat the name of a device as the answer: they write 'This is a metaphor' and stop, as though the label were the insight. The second is the opposite: they describe an effect vividly but never name the technique, so the examiner cannot tell whether the student actually knows what Shakespeare did. This chapter trains you to do both at once, and to do them in the right order.

Every entry below follows the same ladder, and the ladder is the Level 1 to Level 5 method used throughout this book. **Meaning** is Levels 1 and 2 (what the words say and what they mean). **Effect** and **Dramatic purpose** are Levels 3 and 4 (why Shakespeare wrote it this way, and how it connects to the whole play). **ICSE application** is Level 5. Never write a Level 5 sentence about a device until you can honestly do Levels 1 to 4 in your head, because an examiner can tell within one sentence whether the device name is attached to understanding or floating free.

Three rules of accuracy govern this chapter. First, every quotation is drawn from the verified text of Act 1 and is given with speaker and scene; you may trust the wording. Second, when an entry says what a device *means*, that is a reading of Shakespeare's text; when it says what the device *does*, that is **literary interpretation**, and where critics genuinely disagree the entry says so. Third, wherever the chapter talks about examinations, remember the honest position: Act 1 is the Class IX portion of the ICSE syllabus; the Class X board paper examines Acts III to V, but it constantly assumes you know Act 1, and the devices you learn here are the very ones Antony, Brutus and Cassius use again in the later acts.

## Part One: The Three Ironies

Irony is the most important device in Act 1 and the most often misused. In every kind of irony there is a gap between two things: between what is said and what is meant, between what is expected and what happens, or between what a character knows and what the audience knows. Name the gap and you have understood the irony. Fail to name it and you have only used a word.

### Dramatic irony

**Term:** Dramatic irony.

**Definition:** A situation in which the audience knows something that a character on stage does not, so that the character's words or actions carry a meaning the character cannot see.

**Act 1 example:** Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2, dismissing the Soothsayer after hearing the warning "Beware the ides of March" twice: "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass."

**Meaning:** Caesar hears a stranger in the crowd warn him about a specific date. He has the man brought before him, hears the warning repeated, and then dismisses him as a fantasist (a 'dreamer', someone whose head is full of visions) and orders the procession to move on.

**Effect:** Shakespeare's first audience knew, as every audience since has known, that Caesar was murdered on the Ides of March. The line therefore does two things at once: on stage it shows a confident ruler brushing aside superstition; in the auditorium it produces a chill, because we are watching a man walk past the one piece of information that could save him. The word "Pass" is particularly sharp: Caesar is commanding the crowd to move on, but the audience hears him moving on from his own survival.

**Dramatic purpose:** Dramatic irony is how Shakespeare makes a play about a famous death suspenseful when everyone already knows the ending. Instead of asking 'Will Caesar die?' the play asks 'How will he fail to see it coming?' Every confident statement Caesar makes in Act 1 is shadowed by this irony, most of all "for always I am Caesar" in the same scene, spoken in the same breath as the admission "this ear is deaf". It also shapes how we hear Brutus: when he tells Cassius "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well", we know, as Brutus does not, that the man beside him is already planning to forge letters in his name.

**ICSE application:** 'Caesar's dismissal of the Soothsayer as "a dreamer" is dramatic irony: the audience knows that the Ides of March will be the day of his death, so his confident command "Pass" exposes the gap between his self-image as a man who cannot be touched by fear and the vulnerability the play has already made visible to us.'

::: textnote Common misidentification — dramatic irony versus foreshadowing
Students often write 'dramatic irony' for any line that hints at the future. The two overlap but are not the same. **Foreshadowing** is a hint planted by the playwright that something will happen; it works whether or not anyone on stage understands it. **Dramatic irony** requires a character to be *ignorant* of something the audience *knows*. "Beware the ides of March" is foreshadowing when the Soothsayer says it; it becomes dramatic irony when Caesar responds "He is a dreamer." The irony lives in the response, not in the warning. If you want full credit, point to the character's blindness, not merely to the hint.
:::

### Verbal irony

**Term:** Verbal irony.

**Definition:** Saying one thing while meaning another, usually the opposite, so that the listener (or the audience) is expected to hear the real meaning underneath the surface words. Sarcasm is its bitter, mocking form.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3, as Casca marvels at the terrifying storm: "A very pleasing night to honest men."

**Meaning:** The sky is dropping fire, a lion has walked past the Capitol and a slave's hand has burned without being hurt. Cassius calls this a pleasant evening. He does not mean it is pleasant in the ordinary sense; he means that for a man planning what he is planning, a night of omens is exactly the right weather, and that an 'honest' man, by which he means a true Roman republican, should welcome it.

**Effect:** The irony reveals character faster than any direct statement could. Casca is frightened; Cassius is exhilarated. By inverting the obvious meaning of the night, Cassius signals that he reads the omens not as warnings but as encouragement, and the word "honest" quietly redefines honesty as willingness to act against Caesar. The audience also hears an irony Cassius does not intend: the man who calls himself honest will, within a few hours, have forged letters delivered to Brutus.

**Dramatic purpose:** Verbal irony is Cassius's natural idiom throughout Act 1, and Shakespeare uses it to mark him as the play's great manipulator of language. Earlier in Act 1 Scene 2 he refers to "immortal Caesar" while cataloguing Caesar's fever and near-drowning, and calls the stricken Caesar "this god" in the very line that says "this god did shake". Each time, the surface praise carries a hidden sneer. The device trains the audience to listen for double meanings, which is exactly the skill Brutus lacks.

**ICSE application:** 'Cassius's description of the storm as "A very pleasing night to honest men" is verbal irony: he welcomes what terrifies Casca, and by calling conspirators "honest men" he redefines the word to suit his purpose, revealing both his fearlessness and his habit of bending language to manipulate.'

### Situational irony

**Term:** Situational irony.

**Definition:** A contradiction between what is expected to happen (or what would be fitting) and what actually happens, built into the situation itself rather than into anyone's words.

**Act 1 example:** Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2, having just given the most accurate character sketch in the play: "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar. / Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf."

**Meaning:** Caesar has identified Cassius as a man who "thinks too much", who "looks / Quite through the deeds of men" and who is "never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". Having diagnosed the danger perfectly, he refuses to act on it, because fear is not something Caesar admits to; then he asks Antony to speak into his good ear because the other one is deaf.

**Effect:** The situation itself is ironic in two ways. The man who sees through everyone cannot hear properly. The man who correctly names his future killer dismisses the knowledge in the same breath. Shakespeare places the deaf ear immediately after "for always I am Caesar" so that the public image and the private body collide within three lines. (**Historical record:** the deafness has no basis in the ancient sources; it appears to be Shakespeare's invention, which makes the irony deliberate.)

**Dramatic purpose:** Act 1 is full of situations that turn against expectation. The tribunes who strip Caesar's decorations are themselves "put to silence". Brutus's honour, which ought to protect him from conspiracy, is precisely the lever Cassius uses: "honour is the subject of my story." Casca reports that the crowd cheered Caesar for refusing the crown, so that the ruler who wanted power was applauded for appearing not to want it. **Literary interpretation:** one strong reading is that these reversals prepare the audience for the play's largest situational irony, that killing Caesar to save the Republic destroys the Republic.

**ICSE application:** 'There is situational irony in Caesar's "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf", spoken moments after he claims "always I am Caesar": the ruler who boasts of seeing through men and of being beyond fear is revealed as physically impaired and unwilling to act on his own accurate judgement.'

::: textnote Common misidentification — irony versus coincidence or mere contrast
Not every unexpected event is situational irony, and not every opposition is irony at all. Situational irony needs a *reversal of expectation that comments on the situation*. Caesar being deaf is not ironic in itself; it becomes ironic because he has just presented himself as all-seeing. If you cannot say what expectation is reversed, call it 'contrast' instead (see the entry on contrast below), which is a legitimate and separately creditable device.
:::

## Part Two: Figurative Language

### Metaphor

**Term:** Metaphor.

**Definition:** A direct comparison that describes one thing as if it *were* another, without using 'like' or 'as'. The reader is asked to transfer the qualities of the second thing on to the first.

**Act 1 example:** Flavius, Act 1 Scene 1, closing the scene: "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch, / Who else would soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness."

**Meaning:** Caesar is a bird of prey, a falcon or hawk, whose 'feathers' are the decorations and popular support that let him rise. If the tribunes pluck them (by pulling the scarves off his statues and dispersing the crowd), he will fly only at an ordinary height ("pitch" is the falconry term for the height a hawk reaches) instead of soaring out of sight and holding everyone below in frightened slavery ("servile fearfulness").

**Effect:** The metaphor turns a small act of civic vandalism into a political theory. Caesar's greatness is not innate but assembled from borrowed plumage; take away the people's adulation and he is a man of ordinary size. The hunting-bird image also carries menace: a hawk high above "the view of men" is a predator, and the fear it produces is the fear of prey.

**Dramatic purpose:** This is the first sustained metaphor in the play and it establishes the method the conspirators will follow: cut Caesar down to size. Cassius's Colossus simile and his question "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great?" are the same idea in different images. The metaphor also quietly tells the audience that the tribunes' plan will fail: feathers grow back, and Casca's report in the next scene shows the people cheering Caesar anyway.

**ICSE application:** 'Flavius's metaphor of "growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing" presents Caesar as a hawk whose height depends on the plumage of popular support; the image argues that his power is removable, which establishes the political logic the conspirators will follow, while "servile fearfulness" reveals the tribunes' genuine republican dread.'

Other metaphors you should be able to recognise in Act 1 include Cassius's "I, your glass" (he is a mirror that will show Brutus his hidden worth), "this age's yoke" (Rome is an ox harnessed to Caesar), "Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed" (Brutus's character is metal that can be reshaped by a smith) and Brutus's own "This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit" (Casca's bluntness is seasoning that makes his intelligence easier to digest).

::: textnote Common misidentification — metaphor versus simile
The commonest error in the whole of Act 1 is to call Cassius's Colossus image a metaphor. It is not: "he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus" uses "Like", so it is a simile. Students make the error because the image is so vivid that it *feels* like a metaphor. The test is mechanical: look for 'like' or 'as'. If present, simile; if absent, metaphor. "I, your glass" has neither word, so it is a metaphor. Examiners notice the distinction.
:::

### Simile

**Term:** Simile.

**Definition:** An explicit comparison between two different things using 'like' or 'as' (or 'than'), so that the reader is told to see a resemblance while remaining aware that the two things are separate.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2, after the second shout from the games: "Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves."

**Meaning:** Caesar stands with his legs apart ("bestride") across the whole world, which has become "narrow" because he fills it, like the giant statue of Rhodes. Everyone else is tiny, walking between his legs and peering around for nothing better than a disgraceful burial place.

**Effect:** The simile makes size into a moral argument. Caesar's greatness is not admirable but monstrous and unnatural; the Romans' smallness is not natural but shameful ("petty", "dishonourable"). The word "peep" is deliberately undignified: grown senators reduced to children peering about. Because the image is so vivid and physical, it bypasses argument; Brutus is made to *see* his own humiliation rather than reason about it.

**Dramatic purpose:** The Colossus is the hinge of the whole temptation. Immediately afterwards Cassius turns from image to doctrine, "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings", so the simile does the emotional work that lets the abstract claim land. **Literary interpretation:** many critics note that the image is also self-revealing: it is Cassius, not Brutus, who feels dwarfed, and the envy that drives the conspiracy is nowhere clearer than in "we petty men".

**ICSE application:** 'In the simile "he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus", Cassius makes Caesar's power grotesque by comparing him to the giant statue of Rhodes, while the diminutive "petty men" who "peep about" for "dishonourable graves" shames Brutus into feeling his own smallness, which is precisely the feeling Cassius needs him to have.'

Further similes worth knowing: Cassius's sneer that the feverish Caesar cried out "As a sick girl"; Casca's slave whose hand burned "Like twenty torches joined"; Casca's earth that "Shakes like a thing unfirm"; Brutus's observation that Caesar's followers "look like a chidden train" (a scolded group of attendants); and Casca's closing image that Brutus's reputation, "like richest alchemy", will turn the conspirators' crime into "virtue". Notice how often the similes in Act 1 belong to Cassius and Casca, the two men most committed to making others see Caesar differently.

### Imagery

**Term:** Imagery.

**Definition:** Language that appeals to the senses or builds a picture in the mind, usually through a cluster of related metaphors and similes. A play's imagery is best studied in *families*: when the same kind of picture keeps returning, it is carrying meaning.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3, describing the storm as a portrait of Caesar: "a man / Most like this dreadful night, / That thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars / As doth the lion in the Capitol".

**Meaning:** Cassius says he could name a man who is exactly like this terrifying night: a man who thunders and flashes, who splits open graves and who roars like the lion Casca has just reported seeing near the Capitol. He means Caesar, and Casca immediately says so.

**Effect:** Storm, fire and animal imagery fuse into one picture, so that the whole disordered sky becomes a description of a single human being. The effect is to make Caesar seem not merely powerful but unnatural, a disturbance in the order of things, which is exactly how Cassius wants Casca to feel about him.

**Dramatic purpose:** Act 1 has six image families, and you should be able to name all of them with an example:

| Family | Verified Act 1 example | Speaker and scene | What the family carries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bodily | "that same eye whose bend doth awe the world / Did lose his lustre"; "Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look" | Cassius, 1.2; Caesar, 1.2 | Politics is read off the body: eyes, lips, tongue, hands, legs, throat. Caesar's body is weak; Cassius's is lean; Brutus's face is a mirror. |
| Animal | "he would not be a wolf / But that he sees the Romans are but sheep; / He were no lion, were not Romans hinds" | Cassius, 1.3 | Predator and prey: Caesar's power exists only because the Romans consent to be herd animals. Also "ferret", "the bird of night", "Caesar's wing", "the common herd". |
| Fire | "Those that with haste will make a mighty fire / Begin it with weak straws"; "my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus" | Cassius, 1.3 and 1.2 | Rebellion as kindling; sparks of Roman spirit; the burning hand and the "tempest dropping fire". Fire is energy that can purify or destroy. |
| Storm | "the cross blue lightning seemed to open / The breast of heaven" | Cassius, 1.3 | The heavens as a body torn open; disorder in nature mirroring disorder in the state. |
| Disease | "Pray to the gods to intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude"; "we have the falling sickness" | Marullus, 1.1; Cassius, 1.2 | Rome is sick: ingratitude is a plague, Caesar's epilepsy becomes everyone's political paralysis, the Cobbler is "a surgeon to old shoes". |
| Theatre | "as they use to do the players in the theatre" | Casca, 1.2 | Public life as performance: Caesar performs refusal, the crowd behaves like an audience, Brutus forgets "the shows of love", Casca "puts on this tardy form". |

**Literary interpretation:** these families converge in Act 3. The bodily imagery becomes Caesar's actual wounds; the disease imagery becomes Brutus's claim to be a healer; the theatre imagery becomes the funeral orations played to a fickle crowd; the fire imagery becomes the mob burning the conspirators' houses. A student who can trace one family from Act 1 into Act 3 is doing exactly what the Class X paper rewards.

**ICSE application:** 'Shakespeare's animal imagery in Act 1 Scene 3, where Caesar "would not be a wolf / But that he sees the Romans are but sheep", shifts blame from the tyrant to the citizens: the predator exists only because the prey consents, so the imagery turns Cassius's hatred of Caesar into an accusation against Roman passivity.'

::: insight Why Shakespeare gives the finest imagery to the least honest speaker
Count the memorable images of Act 1 and most belong to Cassius: the mirror, Aeneas carrying Anchises, the Colossus, the fault in our stars, the wolf and the sheep, the fire and the straws, the stony tower and the walls of beaten brass. Brutus, the man of honour, speaks plainly; Caesar speaks in short, sculpted maxims; the poetry goes to the manipulator. Shakespeare does this deliberately, and the soliloquy at the end of Act 1 Scene 2 tells us why. The moment Brutus leaves, Cassius drops every image and speaks in flat, practical verse: "I will this night / In several hands in at his windows throw ... Writings". The imagery, we realise, was equipment. Caesar's own analysis confirms the pattern: Cassius "loves no plays" and "hears no music", yet he is the most theatrical and musical speaker in the act. **Literary interpretation:** the play is teaching its audience to distrust beauty of language, a lesson it repeats on a larger scale in Act 3 when Antony's imagery moves the crowd to burn and kill. For the ICSE student the practical point is this: when you identify a brilliant image in Act 1, always ask who is speaking and what they want from the listener. Imagery in this play is almost never decoration; it is persuasion, and the finest examples are the most suspicious.
:::

### Symbolism

**Term:** Symbolism.

**Definition:** The use of a concrete object, action or event to stand for an abstract idea beyond itself. Unlike a metaphor, a symbol is usually a *thing in the story*, not a comparison in a sentence.

**Act 1 example:** Casca, Act 1 Scene 2, on what Antony offered Caesar: "yet 'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets".

**Meaning:** Antony held out a crown, or, Casca corrects himself, not a full crown but a small coronet (a lesser circlet). Caesar pushed it away three times, each time more reluctantly in Casca's view.

**Effect:** The crown is the single most charged object in the Roman world: it stands for the kingship the Republic was founded by abolishing. Casca's correction, "one of these coronets", matters because it suggests a test, a smaller version offered to see how the people react. The object carries the whole political question of the play: does Caesar want to be king?

**Dramatic purpose:** Shakespeare keeps the crown offstage and lets Casca describe it, so that the symbol reaches us already interpreted by a cynic; the audience never sees Caesar's face as he refuses. Other Act 1 symbols include the "scarves" and "trophies" on Caesar's statues (public adulation made visible, and removable), the storm (**literary interpretation:** read by Casca as divine anger, by Cassius as a portrait of Caesar, by Cicero as nothing certain, so the storm symbolises the problem of interpretation itself) and Cassius's dagger, "I know where I will wear this dagger then", which stands for the Roman freedom to die rather than submit.

**ICSE application:** 'The crown that Casca describes is the central symbol of Act 1: Caesar's thrice-repeated refusal, each time "gentler than other", turns an object into a question about his ambition, and the fact that we see it only through Casca's cynical report means the audience inherits his suspicion.'

### Personification

**Term:** Personification.

**Definition:** Giving human qualities, feelings or actions to something non-human: an object, an animal, a place, a force of nature or an abstract idea.

**Act 1 example:** Marullus, Act 1 Scene 1, recalling the crowds that once cheered Pompey: "That Tiber trembled underneath her banks / To hear the replication of your sounds / Made in her concave shores?"

**Meaning:** The people's shout was so loud that the river Tiber shook beneath its banks, hearing its own echo ("replication") thrown back from the curved ("concave") shores.

**Effect:** The river becomes a frightened woman trembling at the noise. This does two things: it magnifies the crowd's former devotion to Pompey into something that moved nature itself, and it gives Rome's river a feminine, almost maternal presence that the people are now betraying. The personification is also hyperbole (see below), and the two devices work together.

**Dramatic purpose:** Personified nature runs through the act. Cassius describes "The troubled Tiber chafing with her shores" (the river fretting angrily against its banks) just before telling how he saved Caesar from it; Casca speaks of "the scolding winds" and "Th' ambitious ocean"; Cassius calls the storm "the strange impatience of the heavens". **Literary interpretation:** when rivers, winds and skies have tempers, the natural world seems to be a participant in Roman politics, which prepares the audience to take the omens of Act 1 Scene 3 and Act 2 seriously even while Cicero reminds us that men "construe things after their fashion".

**ICSE application:** 'Marullus personifies the river in "Tiber trembled underneath her banks", so that the crowd's former love for Pompey seems to have shaken nature itself; the image shames the commoners by measuring their present fickleness against a devotion the whole city once felt.'

::: textnote Common misidentification — personification versus apostrophe
Students confuse these because both involve treating non-human things as people. The test is *direction of address*. **Personification** describes something non-human as if it had human qualities ("Tiber trembled"). **Apostrophe** speaks *to* something absent or non-human as if it could hear ("Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!"). An apostrophe usually involves personification, but personification is not apostrophe unless the speaker is addressing the thing directly. Look for 'thou', 'you' or 'O'.
:::

### Apostrophe

**Term:** Apostrophe.

**Definition:** A figure in which the speaker turns away from the people present to address directly someone absent, something non-human or an abstract idea, as if it could hear.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2, at the climax of his tirade: "Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!"

**Meaning:** Cassius stops speaking to Brutus and addresses the present age and the city of Rome itself, accusing the age of disgrace and the city of having run out of the noble families ("breed of noble bloods") that once produced men who would never tolerate a king.

**Effect:** The sudden turn from a private conversation to a public, almost ceremonial address raises the emotional temperature. It also flatters Brutus indirectly: if Rome has 'lost' its noble blood, Brutus, descendant of the Brutus who expelled the kings, is being invited to prove the age wrong. The exclamation marks and the pronoun "thou" make the lines sound like a lament spoken over a grave.

**Dramatic purpose:** Cassius uses apostrophe at the moments when argument gives way to passion, real or performed. In Act 1 Scene 3 he cries "But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?" immediately after calling Rome "trash" and "offal", pretending that his own sorrow has carried him into dangerous speech. **Literary interpretation:** this second apostrophe is almost certainly calculated, a way of testing whether Casca will betray him, and it shows how a device that normally signals overwhelming feeling can be used as a tactic. He also addresses the gods, "Therein, you gods, you make the weak most strong", turning a political vow into something like a prayer.

**ICSE application:** 'Cassius's apostrophe "Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" turns a private persuasion into a public lament, and by mourning the loss of noble Romans in front of a descendant of the Brutus who expelled the Tarquins, he challenges Brutus to prove the accusation false.'

## Part Three: Structural and Rhetorical Devices

### Antithesis

**Term:** Antithesis.

**Definition:** The placing of opposite or strongly contrasting ideas side by side in a balanced grammatical structure, so that each half sharpens the other.

**Act 1 example:** Brutus, Act 1 Scene 2, stating his principle before Cassius has even made his case: "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently".

**Meaning:** Put honour on one side and death on the other, and Brutus will regard both equally ("indifferently" here means without preferring one), because, as he goes on, he loves "The name of honour more than I fear death".

**Effect:** The balance of the line enacts the balance of Brutus's mind: two eyes, two values, perfectly weighed. The antithesis makes him sound like a Roman statue, calm and symmetrical, and it tells Cassius everything he needs to know. Honour, not freedom or envy, is the word that will move this man.

**Dramatic purpose:** Antithesis is the grammar of a divided Rome. Cassius structures his whole appeal on oppositions: "I was born free as Caesar" against "A wretched creature"; the gods who "make the weak most strong"; "our fathers' minds are dead, / And we are governed with our mothers' spirits". Caesar's "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear" balances public warning against private fear. **Literary interpretation:** Brutus's own antithesis, "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well", is the hinge of his character: the two halves of that line are the two halves of the play's tragedy.

**ICSE application:** 'Brutus's antithesis "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other" shows a mind that weighs values with perfect, almost rigid balance; the structure reveals the idealism that Cassius will exploit, since a man who prizes honour above life can be persuaded to kill in honour's name.'

::: textnote Common misidentification — antithesis versus paradox versus oxymoron
These three sit close together. **Antithesis** sets opposites side by side but each remains true in its own half ("honour in one eye and death i' th' other"). **Paradox** is a statement that seems self-contradictory yet contains a truth ("Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius"). **Oxymoron** compresses two contradictory words into one phrase: Cassius's "honourable-dangerous consequence" in Act 1 Scene 3 is the one clear oxymoron in the act, yoking a word of virtue to a word of threat. Students often write 'paradox' for all three. Ask: are the opposites balanced (antithesis), is the whole statement seemingly impossible (paradox), or are two clashing words glued together (oxymoron)?
:::

### Repetition

**Term:** Repetition.

**Definition:** The deliberate reuse of a word, phrase or structure for emphasis, rhythm or emotional pressure. Anaphora (below) is one specialised type; this entry covers repetition in general.

**Act 1 example:** The Soothsayer, Act 1 Scene 2, when Caesar has him brought face to face and commands him to speak again: "Beware the ides of March."

**Meaning:** The Soothsayer says exactly the same five words he said from the crowd. He adds nothing, explains nothing, softens nothing.

**Effect:** Repetition without variation gives the warning the quality of an oracle rather than an opinion. A man who repeats himself word for word under the gaze of the most powerful person in Rome is either mad or certain; Caesar chooses to believe the first. For the audience, hearing the date twice fixes it in memory and makes the title of the play's catastrophe ring like a bell.

**Dramatic purpose:** Shakespeare uses repetition in Act 1 to mark obsession and ritual. Casca's doubled "Why, for that too" flattens the three shouts into one bored shrug, undercutting their importance. Casca's "never till tonight, never till now" in Act 1 Scene 3 and Cassius's "Therein, you gods, you make the weak most strong; / Therein, you gods, you tyrants do defeat" build incantatory force. Cassius's repeated sounding of the names "Brutus" and "Caesar" treats the names as objects to be written, sounded and weighed. **Literary interpretation:** each repetition tells you what a speaker cannot leave alone.

**ICSE application:** 'The Soothsayer's unaltered repetition of "Beware the ides of March" gives the warning the fixed, impersonal authority of prophecy; by refusing to elaborate even when brought before Caesar, he makes Caesar's dismissal of him as "a dreamer" seem all the more wilfully blind.'

### Anaphora

**Term:** Anaphora.

**Definition:** The repetition of the same word or phrase at the *beginning* of successive clauses, lines or sentences, building rhythm and accumulating force.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3, on the freedom to die: "Nor stony tower, nor walls of beaten brass, / Nor airless dungeon, nor strong links of iron, / Can be retentive to the strength of spirit".

**Meaning:** No stone tower, no wall of hammered brass, no dungeon without air, no iron chain can hold back ("be retentive to") a determined spirit, because a life that is tired of these worldly prisons always has the power to end itself ("dismiss itself").

**Effect:** Each "Nor" adds another wall and another lock, so that the sentence itself becomes a prison with four barriers, and then the main verb breaks through them. The device makes Cassius's doctrine, that a Roman can always escape tyranny by suicide, feel like a triumphant demolition rather than a grim admission. **Historical record:** Cassius did in fact die by suicide after Philippi, so the anaphora foreshadows his end.

**Dramatic purpose:** Marullus's "And do you now put on your best attire? / And do you now cull out a holiday? / And do you now strew flowers in his way" uses anaphora to hammer the commoners with their own ingratitude; each "And do you now" is another blow. Cassius's "Why all these fires, why all these gliding ghosts, / Why birds and beasts from quality and kind" piles up prodigies until the storm seems to demand a single explanation, which he then supplies: Caesar. Anaphora in this play belongs to speakers who are trying to overwhelm a listener.

**ICSE application:** 'Cassius's anaphora "Nor stony tower, nor walls of beaten brass, / Nor airless dungeon, nor strong links of iron" stacks four images of imprisonment before the spirit breaks free of them all, so that the rhythm itself performs the liberation he is preaching and makes suicide sound like victory.'

::: textnote Common misidentification — anaphora versus plain repetition
Anaphora is repetition *at the start of successive units*. "Therein, you gods ... Therein, you gods" is anaphora; "Beware the ides of March" said twice at different moments is repetition but not anaphora; Cassius's "Write them together ... Sound them ... Weigh them" is parallelism (repeated structure) rather than anaphora, because the opening words differ. Naming the more precise device shows the examiner you have actually looked at the lines.
:::

### Rhetorical question

**Term:** Rhetorical question.

**Definition:** A question asked for effect, to which no answer is expected because the answer is assumed to be obvious, or because the speaker intends to supply it.

**Act 1 example:** Marullus, Act 1 Scene 1, when the Cobbler says they have come to see Caesar's triumph: "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? / What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?"

**Meaning:** Why are you celebrating? What foreign land has he conquered? What conquered peoples who must pay tribute ("tributaries") are walking in chains behind his chariot? The implied answer to each is 'none', because Caesar's victory was over Pompey's sons, fellow Romans.

**Effect:** Three questions in a row, each more specific, back the commoners into silence. The device lets Marullus make an argument (this triumph is illegitimate) without stating it, so that the crowd seems to convict itself by having no reply. **Historical record:** a triumph for victory over Romans was indeed regarded as improper, which gives Marullus's questions real force.

**Dramatic purpose:** Rhetorical questions are how Act 1's persuaders control a conversation. Cassius asks Brutus "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great?" and asks himself "For who so firm that cannot be seduced?"; Cassius asks Casca "And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then?" and answers with the wolf and the sheep. Brutus, by contrast, asks real questions ("What means this shouting?"), which marks him as the listener rather than the manipulator.

**ICSE application:** 'Marullus's string of rhetorical questions, "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home?", forces the commoners to confront the fact that Caesar's triumph celebrates victory over Romans; by asking rather than asserting, he makes their silence the admission of guilt.'

### Paradox

**Term:** Paradox.

**Definition:** A statement that appears self-contradictory or absurd but, on reflection, expresses a truth.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2, correcting Brutus's remark that Caesar "hath the falling sickness": "No, Caesar hath it not; but you and I / And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness."

**Meaning:** Caesar has literally fainted in the market-place, so Brutus is literally right. Cassius denies it and claims that the healthy men standing around are the ones who are sick, because they have 'fallen' politically, collapsing before Caesar's power.

**Effect:** The paradox forces a second reading of a plain fact. Epilepsy becomes a metaphor for servility, and the man who really fell is reinterpreted as the man who stands over everyone. The move is typical of Cassius: take an event, invert it, and hand it back charged with his own meaning.

**Dramatic purpose:** Act 1's paradoxes cluster around freedom and bondage. "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" makes one man both prisoner and liberator; "Therein, you gods, you make the weak most strong" makes weakness a source of power through the freedom to die. Cassius's "Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man" is a paradox built on a pun (editors note that 'Rome' was then pronounced much like 'room'), so that a city with room for only one man is both vast and suffocating. **Literary interpretation:** paradox suits a play whose central act is a murder committed in the name of love and honour.

**ICSE application:** 'Cassius's paradox that Caesar "hath it not; but you and I / And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness" turns Caesar's literal collapse into an image of Rome's political collapse, revealing Cassius's gift for converting every fact about Caesar into an argument against him.'

### Hyperbole

**Term:** Hyperbole.

**Definition:** Deliberate exaggeration, not meant to be taken literally, used for emphasis or emotional effect.

**Act 1 example:** Marullus, Act 1 Scene 1, remembering how the crowd waited for Pompey: "Many a time and oft / Have you climbed up to walls and battlements, / To towers and windows, yea, to chimney tops, / Your infants in your arms".

**Meaning:** Again and again you climbed on to walls, towers, windows and even chimneys, carrying your babies, and sat all day to watch Pompey pass.

**Effect:** The list climbs as the people climbed, ending at the absurd height of chimney tops, and the babies in their arms add a touch of reckless devotion. The exaggeration makes the crowd's past love for Pompey enormous so that their present love for Caesar seems a grotesque betrayal. The following lines, in which the shout makes "Tiber trembled", extend the hyperbole into personification.

**Dramatic purpose:** Hyperbole is the natural mode of public oratory and of fear. Casca's prodigies, a hand burning "Like twenty torches joined" and "a hundred ghastly women", may be exaggeration or honest report; Shakespeare leaves it open, and Cicero's scepticism invites us to wonder. Cassius exaggerates systematically: a man "grown so great" that "her wide walks encompassed but one man". **Literary interpretation:** Act 1 asks the audience to notice when size is being inflated for a purpose, which is why the Colossus, the ultimate hyperbole of scale, is also the ultimate manipulation.

**ICSE application:** 'Marullus's hyperbole, in which the commoners once climbed "to chimney tops" with "infants" in their arms to see Pompey, magnifies their former loyalty so that their present celebration of Caesar appears as monstrous ingratitude rather than innocent holiday-making.'

### Allusion

**Term:** Allusion.

**Definition:** A brief reference to a person, event, story or text outside the work, which the audience is expected to recognise and which imports its associations into the present moment.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2, on rescuing Caesar from the Tiber: "I, as Aeneas, our great ancestor, / Did from the flames of Troy upon his shoulder / The old Anchises bear, so from the waves of Tiber / Did I the tired Caesar."

**Meaning:** Just as Aeneas, the Trojan hero whom Romans regarded as their ancestor, carried his aged father Anchises out of burning Troy on his shoulders, so Cassius carried the exhausted Caesar out of the river.

**Effect:** The allusion casts Cassius as the pious hero of Rome's founding legend and Caesar as the feeble old man who had to be carried. It dignifies Cassius and infantilises Caesar in one stroke, and it does so by appealing to a story Brutus has known since childhood. The comparison is also faintly ridiculous if examined (Caesar was not Cassius's father, and the Tiber is not Troy), which is why Cassius moves quickly on to "And this man / Is now become a god".

**Dramatic purpose:** Act 1 has three allusions every student must know. The **Aeneas and Anchises** story comes from Virgil's *Aeneid*. The **Colossus** is the giant bronze statue of Helios at Rhodes, one of the Seven Wonders; Shakespeare's picture of it straddling the harbour is a medieval legend, not ancient fact, but the image depends on it. **"There was a Brutus once"** refers to Lucius Junius Brutus, who led the expulsion of King Tarquin in 509 BC and from whom Marcus Brutus claimed descent; Cassius says this ancestor would have tolerated "Th' eternal devil" in Rome as readily as a king. Each allusion recruits Roman history and legend as a witness against Caesar. Editors also gloss "since the great flood" as the classical flood of Deucalion.

**ICSE application:** 'Cassius's allusion to "a Brutus once" who would not have "brooked ... a king" invokes Lucius Junius Brutus, founder of the Republic, so that Brutus's family name itself becomes an argument: to tolerate Caesar is to betray the ancestor whose statue stands in Rome.'

::: textnote Common misidentification — "Th' eternal devil" and anachronism
Students sometimes list "Th' eternal devil" as a classical allusion. It is not: the devil is a Christian concept with no place in republican Rome, and the phrase is one of Shakespeare's anachronisms (like the "doublet" Caesar wears in Casca's report and the striking clock in Act 2). Call it anachronism, not allusion, and note that Elizabethan audiences would not have noticed or minded.
:::

### Foreshadowing

**Term:** Foreshadowing.

**Definition:** A hint or warning, planted earlier in a work, of something that will happen later. It may be a prophecy, an image, a line of dialogue or an event.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 3, when Casca reports that the Senate means to crown Caesar: "I know where I will wear this dagger then; / Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius."

**Meaning:** If Caesar is made king, Cassius knows where his dagger will go. The surface meaning is suicide (he will free himself from slavery by killing himself), and the following lines about towers and dungeons confirm that reading; but the audience hears the other possibility, that the dagger is for Caesar.

**Effect:** The line is deliberately double. It lets Cassius sound like a Stoic hero while planting the image of a dagger that will, in Act 3, be used on Caesar. The ambiguity also tests Casca, who takes up the theme of suicide ("So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity") before Cassius turns the conversation to Caesar as tyrant.

**Dramatic purpose:** Act 1 is thick with foreshadowing because its job is to load the play's gun. "Beware the ides of March" names the date. Cicero's casual "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" fixes the place. Cassius's description of the night as "Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible" names the mood of the deed, and his couplet "let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure" ends Act 1 Scene 2 with a threat and a prophecy of civil war. Marullus's prayer that the gods "intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude" is, in retrospect, a forecast of the bloodshed of Acts 3 to 5. Even the lion "Against the Capitol" places a predator where Caesar will die.

**ICSE application:** 'Cassius's "I know where I will wear this dagger then" foreshadows the assassination through deliberate ambiguity: he speaks of suicide, yet the audience, knowing the Ides of March, hears the dagger pointed at Caesar, so the line reveals both his readiness to kill and his skill at saying dangerous things safely.'

### Contrast

**Term:** Contrast.

**Definition:** The deliberate placing of two characters, scenes, speeches or images side by side so that their differences illuminate each other. Unlike antithesis, contrast need not be compressed into one balanced sentence; it can span a scene or the whole act.

**Act 1 example:** Caesar, Act 1 Scene 2: "Let me have men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights. / Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous."

**Meaning:** Caesar wants companions who are plump, well-groomed and sleep soundly, in other words contented men. Cassius is thin and looks hungry; he thinks too much; men like that are a threat.

**Effect:** Fat against lean, sleep against thought, contentment against hunger: Caesar builds his whole theory of loyalty on a physical contrast, and the audience, who has just watched Cassius work on Brutus for two hundred lines, knows he is right. The contrast also characterises Caesar: a ruler who judges men by their waistlines is both shrewd and dangerously superficial.

**Dramatic purpose:** Act 1 is constructed from contrasts. Prose commoners against verse tribunes. Brutus, who is "not gamesome", against Antony's "quick spirit". Caesar's public "always I am Caesar" against the private deaf ear and the reported fainting fit. Casca's panic in the storm against Cicero's cool "men may construe things after their fashion". Cassius's fire against Brutus's stillness. **Literary interpretation:** the play's method is comparative; it asks the audience to weigh men against one another, which is exactly what Cassius asks Brutus to do with the names "Brutus" and "Caesar".

**ICSE application:** 'Caesar's contrast between "fat, / Sleek-headed men" and the "lean and hungry" Cassius reveals a ruler who reads character through the body; the irony is that his reading is accurate, yet pride prevents him from acting on it.'

### Pun

**Term:** Pun.

**Definition:** A play on words that exploits two meanings of the same word, or two words that sound alike, so that a sentence means two things at once, usually for comic effect.

**Act 1 example:** The Cobbler, Act 1 Scene 1, asked his trade: "A trade, sir, that I hope I may use with a safe / conscience, which is indeed, sir, a mender of bad / soles."

**Meaning:** He mends worn-out shoe soles; he also, by sound, claims to mend bad souls, which would make him a kind of priest, and the joke is aimed at the tribunes who are lecturing him about conscience.

**Effect:** The pun lets a working man score off his social superiors without quite saying anything punishable. Flavius and Marullus hear the insolence but cannot pin it down, which is why Flavius loses his temper ("Thou naughty knave"). Comedy also relaxes the audience before the serious verse of Marullus's speech, so that the tribune's anger lands harder.

**Dramatic purpose:** The Cobbler's speech is a chain of puns, and you should know them all: "be not out with me" (angry with me / with worn-out shoes) and "if you be out, sir, I can mend you"; "all that I live by is with the awl" (awl, the cobbler's pointed tool, and 'all'); "withal" (with all / with awl); "a surgeon to old shoes" who can "recover them" (restore them / put a new cover on them). **Literary interpretation:** the puns matter politically because they show that the common people are not stupid; they are quick, sceptical and hard to govern by shouting, which is exactly the problem Caesar, Brutus and Antony will face in Act 3.

**ICSE application:** 'The Cobbler's pun on "a mender of bad / soles" lets him mock the tribunes' concern with conscience while appearing to describe his trade; Shakespeare uses the wordplay to establish a crowd that is witty and irreverent rather than simply ignorant, which complicates the tribunes' contempt for them.'

::: textnote Common misidentification — pun versus metaphor
"A surgeon to old shoes" is often labelled a metaphor, and it is one: the Cobbler compares himself to a surgeon. But its force comes from the pun on "recover", so in an answer name both: a metaphor sustained by a pun. Similarly, "mettle" in "quick mettle" and "honourable mettle" carries a pun on 'metal' (spirit and the substance that can be forged); some editions print 'metal'. When two devices operate at once, saying so earns more credit than choosing one.
:::

### Wordplay

**Term:** Wordplay.

**Definition:** The broader category to which puns belong: any deliberate manipulation of words for their sound, shape, double sense or associations, including name-play, extended conceits and sly redefinition.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2, on the two names: "Write them together, yours is as fair a name; / Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; / Weigh them, it is as heavy; conjure with 'em, / 'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar.'"

**Meaning:** Put the names side by side and Brutus's looks as handsome; speak them and Brutus's sounds as well in the mouth; weigh them and it is as heavy; use them in a magic spell and the name Brutus will summon a spirit as quickly as the name Caesar.

**Effect:** Cassius treats names as physical objects that can be written, sounded, weighed and used in magic, which is itself a kind of wordplay: he empties the name 'Caesar' of its aura by handling it like a coin. The passage is more than a pun because it works on the whole idea of what a name is. It is also flattery disguised as logic.

**Dramatic purpose:** Wordplay in Act 1 is a sign of a mind that controls language. Cassius's "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" plays on one man as both captive and rescuer; Brutus's "chew upon this", followed by Cassius's remark that Casca's rudeness gives men "stomach to digest his words", turns thinking into eating. **Literary interpretation:** the Cobbler's puns and Cassius's name-play are the same skill at two social levels, and the play seems to say that whoever handles words best handles Rome.

**ICSE application:** 'Cassius's wordplay on the names, "Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; / Weigh them, it is as heavy", reduces "Caesar" to a mere word that can be handled like any other, so that the sacred aura of the name is dismantled before Brutus's eyes.'

### Euphemism

**Term:** Euphemism.

**Definition:** A mild, vague or indirect expression substituted for one that is blunt, harsh or frightening.

**Act 1 example:** Casca, Act 1 Scene 2, reporting the fate of the tribunes: "Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves / off Caesar's images, are put to silence."

**Meaning:** The two tribunes have been silenced. The phrase does not say how: dismissed from office, exiled, imprisoned or killed. **Historical record:** Caesar had the historical tribunes deposed from the tribunate. Shakespeare's phrase is deliberately vaguer than history.

**Effect:** The softness of the phrase is more chilling than a plain statement would be. Casca drops it in passing, as gossip, and the audience is left to imagine what 'silence' means under Caesar. The euphemism also tells us about Rome's political atmosphere: people have learned to speak carefully.

**Dramatic purpose:** Act 1's conspirators speak almost entirely in euphemism about what they intend. Cassius calls the assassination "an enterprise / Of honourable-dangerous consequence" and "the work we have in hand"; Brutus speaks of "such high things"; Casca asks Cassius to "Be factious for redress of all these griefs"; Cassius's "life ... Never lacks power to dismiss itself" softens suicide into a polite dismissal. Caesar, after his fit, asked the crowd to blame "his infirmity". **Literary interpretation:** the play never lets the conspirators say 'kill Caesar' in Act 1, and the gap between their language and their intention is one of its deepest subjects.

**ICSE application:** 'Casca's euphemism that the tribunes "are put to silence" conveys the menace of Caesar's Rome precisely by its vagueness: the audience is left to imagine the punishment, and the casual tone shows how quickly the suppression of dissent has become ordinary news.'

### Alliteration

**Term:** Alliteration.

**Definition:** The repetition of the same consonant sound at the beginning of nearby words.

**Act 1 example:** Marullus, Act 1 Scene 1: "That Tiber trembled underneath her banks".

**Meaning:** The river shook beneath its banks at the crowd's shout.

**Effect:** The repeated hard 't' in "Tiber trembled" gives the line a physical shudder, so that the sound enacts the trembling. This is the only kind of alliteration worth mentioning in an answer: alliteration that *does* something audible.

**Dramatic purpose:** Shakespeare rarely alliterates for its own sake in this play, and you should resist listing every pair of matching consonants. The two further cases worth knowing are Cassius's "walls of beaten brass" and "the breed of noble bloods", where the heavy 'b' sounds give weight to images of solidity and nobility.

**ICSE application:** 'The alliteration in "Tiber trembled" makes the line itself shudder, reinforcing Marullus's personification of the river and the sheer force of the crowd's former devotion to Pompey.' Use this device only when you can describe the sound's effect; a bare 'this is alliteration' earns nothing.

## Part Four: Dramatic Devices

### Prose and verse

**Term:** Prose and verse distinction.

**Definition:** Shakespeare writes some speeches in blank verse (unrhymed lines of roughly ten syllables with a regular beat) and others in prose (ordinary sentences with no metrical pattern). The choice is a device: it signals who is speaking, in what mood and at what social level.

**Act 1 example:** In Act 1 Scene 1 the Carpenter and Cobbler speak prose ("Truly, sir, all that I live by is with the / awl") while Flavius and Marullus answer in verse ("Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home?"). In Act 1 Scene 2 the patrician Casca delivers his whole report of the crown-offering in prose ("he put it by with the back of his hand, / thus, and then the people fell a-shouting").

**Meaning:** The commoners' prose is quick, punning and conversational; the tribunes' verse is formal, rhythmic and public. Casca's prose is sour and deflating; it refuses the dignity of verse to an event that Caesar wanted to be dignified.

**Effect:** The shift from prose to verse in Scene 1 is audible even to an audience that could not define either term: the tribunes sound like authority, the commoners like the street. In Scene 2 the effect is reversed. Casca, a nobleman surrounded by verse-speakers, drops into prose to describe Caesar's performance, and the prose itself becomes a judgement: the "rabblement", the "sweaty nightcaps", the "stinking breath" belong to a world without poetry.

**Dramatic purpose:** Shakespeare uses the medium to control how seriously we take an event. Had Casca reported the crown-offering in verse, it would have sounded heroic; in prose it sounds like a farce staged for a crowd that hisses and claps "as they use to do the players in the theatre". The same Casca speaks tense, frightened verse in Act 1 Scene 3 ("Are not you moved, when all the sway of earth / Shakes like a thing unfirm?"), which shows that the prose of Scene 2 was a chosen manner, the "tardy form" Cassius says he "puts on", rather than a limitation.

**ICSE application:** 'Shakespeare gives Casca's account of the crown-offering in prose rather than verse, so that Caesar's carefully staged refusal is reported in the flat, cynical register of gossip; the choice of medium strips the event of grandeur and prepares Brutus to see Caesar as a performer rather than a hero.'

::: insight How prose and verse encode class and mood
The simple rule, that commoners speak prose and nobles speak verse, is true of Act 1 Scene 1 and is worth stating in an answer, because it shows you know that the medium is meaningful. But the rule is a starting point, not a law, and the interesting cases are the exceptions. Casca is a patrician who speaks prose in Scene 2 and verse in Scene 3. **Literary interpretation:** one strong reading is that prose is his armour, the blunt, mocking manner Brutus calls "rudeness", and that the storm frightens him out of it; another is that Shakespeare simply needs the crown-offering reported in a register that denies it dignity. Either way, the shift tells you that prose can encode *mood* (deflation, cynicism, intimacy, comedy) as well as *class*. The pattern continues in the later acts in a way the Class X student must know: in Act 3 Scene 2 Brutus addresses the crowd in prose, reasoning with them as equals, while Antony answers in verse that sweeps them away. The commoners who began the play in prose end up moved by poetry, and the man of honour who chose prose loses Rome. When you write about prose and verse, therefore, never stop at 'this shows he is a commoner'; ask what the medium is doing to the moment.
:::

### Soliloquy

**Term:** Soliloquy.

**Definition:** A speech delivered by a character alone on stage, revealing private thoughts directly to the audience. By convention a soliloquy is truthful: the character has no one to deceive.

**Act 1 example:** Cassius, Act 1 Scene 2, after Brutus leaves: "Well, Brutus, thou art noble. Yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed."

**Meaning:** Brutus is noble, but his noble nature, like metal, can be worked away from its natural shape. Cassius goes on to say that Caesar "doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus", that he would never let himself be manipulated as he is manipulating Brutus ("If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me"), and that he will throw forged letters in at Brutus's windows that night.

**Effect:** This is the only soliloquy in Act 1, and it changes the meaning of everything before it. For two hundred lines Cassius has spoken of honour, friendship and Rome; alone, he speaks of technique. The word "wrought" turns Brutus into raw material. The audience, which may have been half-persuaded by the Colossus and the Tiber, is suddenly placed outside the persuasion, looking at the machinery.

**Dramatic purpose:** Shakespeare ends the scene with the soliloquy so that the audience carries its knowledge into the storm scene and into Act 2, where Brutus will read the letters and believe them. The soliloquy also establishes a question the play never fully answers: is Cassius a patriot who uses dishonest means, or an envious man who uses patriotic language? The lines "Caesar doth bear me hard" (Caesar bears a grudge against me) and "we will shake him, or worse days endure" can support either reading, and a good answer says so.

**ICSE application:** 'Cassius's soliloquy reveals the gap between his public and private selves: the man who has just appealed to Brutus's honour now plans to have that "honourable mettle ... wrought" through forged letters, so the device exposes the manipulation and ensures the audience cannot share Brutus's trust.'

::: textnote Common misidentification — soliloquy versus long speech versus aside
Cassius's Tiber story and his Colossus speech are *long speeches addressed to Brutus*; they are not soliloquies, however personal they sound. A **soliloquy** requires the speaker to be alone (or to believe himself alone). An **aside** is a brief remark meant for the audience, or for one other character, which the rest of the stage does not hear. The Folger text of Act 1 marks no asides at all. Brutus and Cassius talking quietly while Caesar's train passes is a split-stage conversation, not an aside, because each speaker is heard by the person he addresses.
:::

### Aside and reported action

**Term:** Reported action (the messenger device), with a note on the aside.

**Definition:** Reported action is the technique of having an event that happens offstage described by a character who witnessed it, instead of staging it. The describer's personality colours the report.

**Act 1 example:** Casca, Act 1 Scene 2: "I saw Mark / Antony offer him a crown (yet 'twas not a crown / neither; 'twas one of these coronets), and, as I told / you, he put it by once; but for all that, to my / thinking, he would fain have had it."

**Meaning:** Casca saw Antony offer Caesar a crown, or rather a coronet; Caesar pushed it away, but in Casca's opinion he would gladly ("fain") have accepted it.

**Effect:** The audience never sees the crown-offering. We hear only the shouts, and then Casca's version, interrupted by Brutus and Cassius, hedged with "to my / thinking", and soaked in contempt for the crowd. The event reaches us already interpreted, and the interpretation is Casca's. We cannot check whether Caesar really wanted the crown, and that uncertainty is the point.

**Dramatic purpose:** Staging the offer would have forced Shakespeare to decide what Caesar's face showed. Reporting it keeps Caesar's ambition a question rather than a fact, which the whole play depends on. It also lets the audience experience the shouts as Brutus experiences them, from outside, fearing that "the people / Choose Caesar for their king". The same device operates when Casca reports the omens in Act 1 Scene 3: the burning hand, the lion and the owl are described, not shown, so that the audience receives them through a frightened man's eyes while Cicero reminds us that witnesses "construe things after their fashion". **Historical record:** Plutarch says the diadem was offered and refused twice; Shakespeare's Casca says three times, and the cynical reading that Caesar wanted it is Shakespeare's addition.

**ICSE application:** 'Shakespeare presents the crown-offering only through Casca's report, so that the audience, like Brutus, receives a filtered account coloured by phrases such as "to my / thinking, he would fain have had it"; the device keeps Caesar's true ambition uncertain and demonstrates how political reality in this play is always somebody's version.'

::: icse What device questions actually look like
**Exam-oriented advice.** In the Class X board paper the drama extracts come from Acts III to V, and the sub-questions (in the 2025 paper, five parts marked 3, 3, 3, 3 and 4) mostly ask you to explain a phrase, say why a speaker says something, describe what is revealed about character, or give a supported personal response. A sub-question rarely says 'name the figure of speech' and stops there; more often the device is the *means* by which you explain a phrase well. So the skill this chapter builds is not labelling but explaining-through-labelling: 'Antony's repeated "honourable man" is verbal irony because ...' is the shape of a full-mark sentence. Act 1 itself is examined in Class IX, usually through reference-to-the-context questions where device recognition is more directly tested; the table above is your bank for those. Nothing here is a prediction of what any paper will ask.

## HOW TO WRITE ABOUT A DEVICE IN AN ICSE ANSWER

Every strong sentence about a device has four parts, in this order: **quote** the words (briefly and exactly), **name** the device, **explain** what the words mean, and **analyse** what the device does for the speaker, the character or the play. Students usually manage the first two and skip the last two. The last two are where the marks are.

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
Suppose the question asks, about Cassius's speech after the second shout, 'Explain the comparison Cassius makes here. What does it reveal about his attitude to Caesar and to the Romans?' This is a Level 5 task, but you can only do it if Levels 1 to 4 are already secure: you know what a Colossus is (Level 1), you can paraphrase the lines (Level 2), you can say why Shakespeare chose an image of size at this moment (Level 3), and you can connect the image to the play's larger argument about greatness and consent (Level 4). Watch the difference below.
:::

::: weak A weak answer on the Colossus image
Cassius uses a metaphor and compares Caesar to a Colossus. A Colossus is a very big statue. This shows that Caesar is very powerful and everyone else is small. Cassius is jealous of Caesar and wants Brutus to join him. The imagery is very effective and makes the audience understand Cassius's feelings.
:::

What is wrong with it? The device is misnamed (the line uses "Like", so it is a simile). Nothing is quoted. 'Very big', 'very powerful', 'very effective' are not analysis. The answer names Cassius's jealousy without showing where in the words it can be seen, and it never asks what the image does to Brutus, who is the person being spoken to. It would be awarded for basic understanding and little else.

::: strong A strong answer on the Colossus image
Cassius's simile, "he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus", compares Caesar to the giant statue of Rhodes, which legend said stood astride the harbour so that ships passed between its legs. The comparison makes Caesar's power monstrous rather than admirable: the world has become "narrow" because one man fills it. The real force of the image, however, lies in what it does to everyone else. Romans are "petty men" who "Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves"; the verb "peep" turns senators into children, and "dishonourable graves" tells Brutus that to live under Caesar is to die in disgrace. By making smallness shameful, Cassius turns a description into an accusation, and the plural "we" and "ourselves" include Brutus in the humiliation. The image therefore reveals Cassius's envy, since it is he who feels dwarfed, but it also reveals his skill: he does not argue that Caesar is dangerous, he makes Brutus feel small, and then supplies the doctrine that follows, "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings." The simile prepares the ground for that claim by making the Romans' inferiority seem a matter of their own consent.
:::

Notice what the strong answer does that the weak one does not. It quotes exactly and briefly. It names the device correctly and glosses the allusion in one clause. It explains meaning before effect. It picks out individual words ("narrow", "peep", "dishonourable", "we") and says what each does. It keeps asking who is being spoken to and what the speaker wants. And it ends by connecting the image to the lines that follow, which shows that the device has been understood as part of a design rather than as an isolated ornament. The strong answer is longer, but every sentence earns something; length without analysis would not.

::: revision The device bank in one minute
Irony needs a gap: dramatic (audience knows, character does not: "He is a dreamer"), verbal (says one thing, means another: "A very pleasing night to honest men"), situational (expectation reversed: "always I am Caesar" beside "this ear is deaf"). Metaphor says *is* ("I, your glass"); simile says *like* ("Like a Colossus"). Imagery runs in six families: body, animal, fire, storm, disease, theatre. Personification describes ("Tiber trembled"); apostrophe addresses ("Rome, thou hast lost"). Antithesis balances; paradox contradicts; oxymoron compresses ("honourable-dangerous"). Anaphora repeats openings ("Nor ... nor ... nor"). Allusion borrows a story (Aeneas, Colossus, "a Brutus once"). Euphemism softens ("put to silence"). Prose for commoners and for Casca's cynicism; verse for authority and feeling. One soliloquy, Cassius's, and no marked asides. Always: quote, name, explain, analyse.
:::
