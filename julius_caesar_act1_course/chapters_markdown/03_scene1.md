# Act 1 Scene 1 — The Street: Commoners, Tribunes and the Fickle Crowd

*What this chapter does: it takes the shortest scene in Act 1 and teaches it as the professional Shakespearean reads it — as a complete piece of political theatre, not a warm-up. You will first learn what literally happens, then what it means, then why Shakespeare built it this way, then how it wires itself into Acts 2 to 5, and finally how the scene earns marks in an ICSE answer. Every difficult word is explained the first time it appears. Every important passage is taught line by line with the same fixed labels, so you can scan and revise. By the end you should be able to explain the Cobbler's jokes, Marullus's speech and Flavius's falcon image without notes.*

## Before You Begin: How to Read This Scene Without Fear

You have probably been told that Act 1 Scene 1 is 'just an introduction'. It is not. It is a small, perfectly engineered machine, and once you see how it works, the rest of the play becomes far easier, because almost every large idea in *Julius Caesar* is switched on here in miniature: the power of the crowd, the fear of one man rising above the rest, the clash between the classes, the gap between what people say and what they mean, and the sense that Rome is quietly breaking.

Three practical facts will remove most of your difficulty.

**First, who is on stage.** Only four people speak: two tribunes, Flavius and Marullus, and two workmen, a Carpenter and a Cobbler. A crowd of other commoners stands with the workmen but says nothing. Caesar never appears; he is only talked about. Neither Brutus nor Cassius appears. So you are not being asked to juggle the famous characters yet — only to watch two officials confront a holiday crowd.

**Second, the shape of the language.** The commoners speak **prose** (ordinary sentences, printed like a paragraph). The tribunes speak **verse** (lines of roughly ten syllables, printed with a capital letter at the start of each line). Shakespeare uses this split deliberately: the workmen sound quick, earthy and witty; the tribunes sound formal, elevated and angry. When the Cobbler jokes, he does it in prose. When Marullus delivers his great speech about Pompey, he does it in verse. If you notice the switch, you have already begun to read like a critic.

**Third, the vocabulary.** Most of the difficulty in this scene is a small set of old or specialised words. Learn the table below once, and the scene opens up.

## The Five Levels: A First Pass Through the Scene

Before we take the scene apart, walk through it five times at increasing depth. This is the method the whole book uses, and it is the method you should use in the exam hall: never write about meaning until you are sure of the facts, and never write about Shakespeare's craft until you are sure of the meaning.

::: level LEVEL 1 — What happened?
It is a public holiday in Rome, and the streets are full of working men in their best clothes. Two tribunes — elected officials whose traditional job is to protect the ordinary people — arrive and are furious. Flavius orders the crowd home, demanding to know why tradesmen are wandering about on a working day without the tools or aprons that mark their trade. Flavius demands a Carpenter's trade and gets a plain answer; Marullus asks where the man's apron and rule are, then turns to a Cobbler, who refuses to answer plainly and instead spins a chain of jokes about mending soles, mending the tribunes themselves, living by the awl and being a 'surgeon' to old shoes. Pressed for the real reason he is out, the Cobbler finally admits it: the people have taken the day off to see Caesar and celebrate his triumph. Marullus explodes. He asks what conquest Caesar has actually won and what foreign captives follow his chariot — the answer being none. He reminds the crowd how they once waited all day on walls and rooftops to cheer Pompey, so loudly that the river Tiber shook; and now they dress up and scatter flowers for the man who triumphs over Pompey's own blood. He calls them ungrateful and tells them to go home and pray that the gods hold back the plague such ingratitude deserves. Flavius sends them off to weep into the Tiber. The commoners leave in silence. Flavius then tells Marullus to split up and strip any decorations from Caesar's statues. Marullus hesitates because it is the feast of Lupercal; Flavius overrules him. The scene ends with Flavius's image of Caesar as a young hawk whose growing feathers must be plucked before he soars beyond the sight of men and keeps everyone in slavish fear.
:::

::: level LEVEL 2 — What does it mean?
Underneath the quarrel about aprons and shoes, the scene is about loyalty and who deserves it. The tribunes are not really angry about a missed day of work; they are frightened that the people of Rome have transferred their love from Pompey — the dead republican hero — to Caesar, the man who destroyed him. To the tribunes, Caesar's triumph is not a national victory but a civil-war victory over fellow Romans, which makes celebrating it a kind of betrayal. The Cobbler's wordplay means something too: a working man can be sharper than the officials who sneer at him, and the class that the tribunes call 'idle' and 'vulgar' is neither stupid nor silent. The crowd's silent exit means that public feeling in Rome is soft and movable — it can be shouted into shame in a few minutes. And Flavius's final image means that at least some Romans already see Caesar as a danger who must be cut down to ordinary size before he becomes untouchable.
:::

::: level LEVEL 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way?
Shakespeare had a choice. He could have opened with Caesar in glory, or with Brutus brooding, or with the conspirators plotting. Instead he opened at street level, with nobodies. This does several things at once. It establishes Rome as a divided city before we meet any of the great men, so that when Caesar enters in Scene 2 we already know he is loved by the crowd and feared by the officials. It introduces the crowd as a character in its own right — a character that will decide the fate of Rome in Act 3. It lets comedy do serious work: the Cobbler's puns relax the audience and make the tribunes look pompous, so that when Marullus's anger becomes genuinely eloquent we feel the shift from farce to fear. The prose-verse contrast marks class instantly for the ear. And by keeping Caesar offstage, Shakespeare makes us build our first picture of him entirely from other people's reactions — which is exactly how reputation works in politics. The scene teaches the audience how to watch the play: listen to what the crowd is doing, and ask who is steering it.
:::

::: level LEVEL 4 — How does it connect to the whole play?
Almost every thread pulled here is tied off later. The crowd that switches from Pompey to Caesar will switch from Caesar to Brutus and from Brutus to Antony in Act 3 Scene 2, and will then tear an innocent poet to pieces in Act 3 Scene 3 for having the wrong name. The tribunes' stripping of Caesar's statues has a direct consequence in Scene 2, where Casca reports that Marullus and Flavius have been "put to silence" for pulling scarves off Caesar's images — our first sight of Caesar's power to crush opposition, and a fact that Cassius and Brutus can use. Flavius's fear that Caesar will "soar above the view of men" is exactly the fear Cassius exploits in Scene 2 and Brutus wrestles with in Act 2 Scene 1. The word "Pompey" will echo all the way to the conspirators meeting at Pompey's Porch in Scene 3 and Caesar falling at the foot of Pompey's statue in Act 3 Scene 1. The theme of ingratitude, first flung at the crowd, will return with terrible force when Caesar is killed by men he trusted. In short, Scene 1 is the play's seed-bed.
:::

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
Be honest with yourself about the exam first. Act 1 is the Class IX portion of the syllabus; the Class X board paper sets its drama extracts from Acts III, IV and V. So you will almost certainly not receive an extract from this scene in the board examination. But you will need this scene constantly, in three ways. First, any question about the crowd in Act 3 — why Antony succeeds, why the mob turns, why Cinna the poet dies — is answered far better by a candidate who can say that Shakespeare established the crowd's fickleness in the very first scene, and who can name the Pompey-to-Caesar shift as the first swing in a pattern. Second, questions about why the conspirators fear Caesar, or whether their fear is justified, can draw on the tribunes' republican anxiety and their punishment for touching Caesar's statues. Third, Class IX internal examinations and 'reference to the context' tests do set this scene directly, and sub-questions such as 'Explain the wordplay of the Cobbler' or 'What does Marullus accuse the commoners of?' are exactly the kind of thing this chapter prepares you for. The rule: quote briefly, explain precisely, connect to the larger pattern.

## The Scene as a Complete Dramatic Unit: Twenty Angles

A professional reader does not read a scene once; he turns it in the light and looks at it from many sides. The twenty short sections below do exactly that. Do not skim them as a checklist — each one is a lens, and the exam rewards candidates who can switch lenses.

### 1 Setting

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** gives no scene heading at all in the First Folio; the familiar 'Rome. A street.' was added by later editors, and it is the natural inference from Flavius's order to the workmen to leave the "streets" and Marullus's command to "Run to your houses". So we are outdoors, in public, in the open city — not in a palace, a Senate house or a private home. This matters. Public space in Rome belongs to no one and to everyone; it is where power is displayed (a triumph is a parade through the streets) and where power is contested (the tribunes try to clear those same streets). The time is a festival day: the Cobbler says "we make holiday", and Marullus later reminds Flavius "it is the feast of Lupercal". Shakespeare has quietly fused two occasions — Caesar's triumph and the Lupercalia — into one crowded afternoon, so that the setting itself is saturated with celebration. Every costume detail supports this: the men wear their "best apparel" and "best attire" rather than working clothes. The setting is, in effect, a party that two angry officials are trying to shut down.

### 2 Characters

Four people speak. **Flavius** and **Marullus** are tribunes of the people (see angle 14 for the office). Flavius opens the scene and closes it; he gives the orders, including the order to strip the statues, and he has the final, image-laden speech. Marullus shares the interrogating (the apron question, "Answer me directly") and delivers the great Pompey speech; he is the orator of the pair, but also the one who hesitates. **The Carpenter** speaks once, plainly and respectfully: "Why, sir, a carpenter." He is the ordinary, obedient workman. **The Cobbler** is the star of the first half: evasive, witty, punning, never quite insolent enough to be arrested, and finally the one who states the real reason for the holiday. Around them stand **the other Commoners**, who never speak but whose silent exit is one of the scene's most important moments. Off stage, but present in every line, stands **Caesar**, and behind him the ghost of **Pompey**. Notice that none of the commoners has a name. They are defined entirely by trade. That anonymity is the point: they are 'the people', a collective, and the play will treat them as one body with one changeable mood.

### 3 Political situation

**HISTORICAL RECORD:** Rome in the play's opening is a republic in name that has been ruled for years by military strongmen. Caesar has defeated Pompey at Pharsalus, Pompey has been murdered in Egypt, and Caesar has finished off Pompey's sons in Spain; he holds the dictatorship, an emergency office that was supposed to last six months and that he has held again and again. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** compresses all this into a single sentence of the Cobbler's — the people "rejoice in his triumph" — and a single furious question of Marullus's about a triumph "over Pompey's blood". The tribunes represent the old republican constitution: officers elected to protect the plebeians against the powerful. But in this scene the plebeians do not want protecting; they want a holiday and a hero. That reversal is the political situation in a nutshell. The people and their official guardians have come apart. Caesar's popularity now flows directly from crowd to leader, bypassing the institutions that were meant to stand between them, and the tribunes can feel the ground going. The stripping of the statues is a small, symbolic attempt to reassert institutional control over a personality cult — the worship of one leader as if he were more than a man — that is already outgrowing it.

### 4 What happens

The action has four movements. **Movement one — confrontation:** the tribunes order the crowd home and demand to know their trades; the Carpenter answers, the Cobbler stalls with wordplay. **Movement two — revelation:** pressed, the Cobbler admits the crowd is out "to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph". **Movement three — rebuke:** Marullus's speech about Pompey, followed by Flavius's order to go and weep into the Tiber; the commoners leave without a word. **Movement four — plan:** the tribunes agree to split up, drive the crowd from the streets and strip Caesar's statues of their decorations, over Marullus's religious scruple about the Lupercal. Shakespeare structures the scene so that the comic energy peaks early (the puns), the emotional energy peaks in the middle (the Pompey speech), and the political energy peaks at the end (the plan and the falcon image). Each movement is shorter and more serious than the one before. That tightening is what gives such a small scene its sense of urgency.

### 5 Why it happens

On the surface, the tribunes are enforcing a rule about working days. But Shakespeare makes their real motive unmistakable within a few lines. They are not offended by idleness; they are offended by whom the idleness honours. Flavius's first words attack the holiday itself — "Is this a holiday?" — before he knows what it is for, and Marullus's fury only becomes eloquent once Caesar's name is spoken. So the deeper cause of the scene is the tribunes' fear that a triumph over Romans is being celebrated as if it were a triumph over foreigners, and that the people's applause is manufacturing exactly the kind of one-man power the Republic was founded to prevent. There is a further, subtler cause: the tribunes are losing a competition for the crowd's loyalty, and they know it. Their contempt is partly wounded pride. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many critics also see the tribunes acting from a memory of Pompey that is as much political as personal — Pompey stood, however imperfectly, for the Senate's side in the civil war, and to cheer his conqueror is to cheer the defeat of the Senate's cause.

### 6 What each character wants

**Flavius** wants the streets emptied and Caesar's public honours removed; more deeply, he wants Caesar brought down to "an ordinary pitch" before it is too late. **Marullus** wants the same, but he also wants to be understood — his speech is an attempt to make the crowd feel shame, not simply to obey — and he wants to avoid offending the gods on a holy day. **The Carpenter** wants to avoid trouble; he answers at once. **The Cobbler** wants to enjoy his holiday, to keep his dignity in front of men who despise him, to score a few points off authority without paying for them, and — he tells us so with a grin — to drum up business, since a crowd walking the streets will "wear out their shoes". **The silent Commoners** want to see Caesar. That is all they want, and Shakespeare lets that simplicity stand as a fact about crowds: they want spectacle and a figure to cheer. Understanding these wants matters because the scene's conflict is not good against bad; it is several legitimate desires colliding in a public square.

### 7 What changes during the scene

At the start, the street is full of celebrating tradesmen; at the end it is nearly empty and the tribunes are on their way to deface the dictator's statues. That is the visible change. The invisible changes are more interesting. The crowd shifts from festive confidence to guilty silence; the Cobbler, so talkative early on, is not given a single word after Marullus's speech. The tribunes shift from scolding officials to active resisters — they move from words to an act of political defiance. And the audience's mood shifts too: we begin laughing at puns and end with an image of a hawk that must have its feathers plucked. Yet notice what does *not* change. The commoners are not convinced; they are cowed. Nothing in the text says they now hate Caesar. In Scene 2 the crowd is cheering him again within the hour. Shakespeare shows us a mood being suppressed, not a mind being changed, and the difference between those two things is one of the play's great subjects.

### 8 What the audience learns

We learn, in order: that it is a holiday; that tradesmen are celebrating; that the celebration is for Caesar's triumph; that this triumph is over Pompey's kin rather than foreign enemies; that the same crowd once adored Pompey; that officials exist who regard Caesar's rise as dangerous; that Caesar's statues have been decorated like idols; that today is also the Lupercal; and that at least one official believes Caesar would "soar above the view of men" if not checked. We learn all this without a single word from Caesar. We also learn something about tone: this is a play in which ordinary people will be allowed to be funny and clever, and in which the great political speeches will be aimed at exactly such people. Finally, and most importantly, we learn how to read the rest of the play: whenever a crowd shouts, ask who has moved it, and how fast.

### 9 What Shakespeare deliberately withholds

Notice the silences. Caesar is never seen. The commoners never tell us whether Marullus has persuaded them — they simply "vanish". The triumph is never named or described; there is no mention of Spain, of Munda, of the actual battle. The names of the Carpenter and the Cobbler are withheld, so that they remain types. Brutus and Cassius, the men who will actually act on the tribunes' fear, are not even mentioned. We are not told whether the tribunes' fear is justified; the scene gives us their view and no rebuttal. And Shakespeare withholds the consequence of stripping the statues until Casca reports it in Scene 2. Each of these gaps is a deliberate design choice. By withholding Caesar, Shakespeare makes reputation the first thing we experience. By withholding the crowd's reply, he keeps the question 'what does the crowd really think?' open — a question the play will answer, brutally, in Act 3. By withholding a counter-argument, he lets the republican fear stand as the audience's first frame for Caesar, so that when Caesar does appear we are already watching him for signs of the tyrant. Great dramatists control what you do not know as carefully as what you do.

### 10 Important language

Three registers of language meet in this scene. **The language of command** belongs to the tribunes: "Hence!", "get you home!", "Speak, what trade art thou?", "Answer me directly", "Be gone!", "Run to your houses". Imperatives pile up, and they tell us these men are used to being obeyed — and are alarmed that they may not be. **The language of evasion and wit** belongs to the Cobbler: he answers questions with definitions ("a mender of bad / soles"), with offers ("I can mend you"), with boasts ("a / surgeon to old shoes"), with anything except a straight reply. His speech is dense with double meanings, and its rhythm is quick, colloquial prose. **The language of rhetoric** belongs to Marullus's speech and Flavius's closing lines: rhetorical questions in series, the direct address "O you hard hearts", the ceremonial epithet (descriptive title) "great Pompey", the grand hyperbole (deliberate exaggeration) of a river trembling, and finally the controlled, metaphorical verse of the hawk. The scene therefore teaches you, within a few minutes, to hear three ways of using words: to order, to deflect, and to persuade. All three will return. The last of them — persuasion aimed at a crowd — is the engine of Act 3.

### 11 Important images

An image is a picture made in words, and this scene has five that you should be able to describe and interpret. **The missing tools:** the "leather apron" and "thy rule" that the workmen should be wearing. Their absence is a picture of order abandoned — a man without the sign of his trade is, to the tribunes, a man out of his place. **The stone crowd:** "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless / things!" The people are pictured as lumps of inert matter, incapable of feeling; then the insult is sharpened — they are *worse* than stones, because stones at least cannot be ungrateful. **The trembling river:** the Tiber shaking beneath its banks at the echo of the crowd's shout for Pompey. This turns a river into a frightened body and a cheer into an earthquake — a picture of how enormous public love once was, and therefore how enormous the present betrayal is. **The river of tears:** Flavius's order to the poor to weep into the Tiber until "the lowest stream / Do kiss the most exalted shores of all" — a grotesque hyperbole in which the people's guilt should be large enough to flood Rome. **The plucked hawk:** Caesar as a young bird of prey whose "growing feathers" must be pulled so that he flies "an ordinary pitch". Notice how the images move from the low and physical (aprons, stones) to the elevated and airborne (a hawk above the sight of men). That upward movement is the scene's own picture of ambition.

### 12 Literary devices

Only devices genuinely present are listed; do not invent others in an exam. **Pun (wordplay):** the Cobbler's soles/souls, out/out, mend/mend, awl/all, withal/with awl, recover/re-cover — see the dedicated section later. **Prose–verse contrast:** commoners in prose, tribunes in verse, marking class and tone. **Rhetorical question:** Marullus's "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home?" and the triple "And do you now…" — questions that expect no answer because the answer is shaming. **Anaphora (repetition at the start of successive lines):** "And do you now put on your best attire? / And do you now cull out a holiday? / And do you now strew flowers in his way". **Apostrophe (direct emotional address):** "O you hard hearts, you cruel men of Rome". **Metaphor:** the crowd as "blocks" and "stones"; the Cobbler as "surgeon to old shoes"; Caesar as a hawk. **Personification:** the Tiber "trembled" beneath "her banks"; the statues are to be "Disrobe[d]" as though they wore clothes. **Hyperbole:** the trembling river, the flood of tears, the "chimney tops". **Synecdoche (a part standing for the whole):** "Pompey's blood" — blood standing for his kin and his cause. **Antithesis:** "the lowest stream" against "the most exalted shores"; "an ordinary pitch" against soaring "above the view of men". **Extended metaphor:** the falconry image sustained over four lines. **Dramatic irony** and **foreshadowing** are treated in angles 15 and 16.

### 13 Themes

**Public opinion and its fickleness.** The crowd's love has moved from Pompey to Caesar; the scene's whole argument turns on that movement. **Power and the fear of one man.** Flavius's hawk speech is the first statement of the republican terror that a single Roman may rise above the rest. **Class and hierarchy.** Officials and workmen collide; the officials assume superiority, the workman quietly proves he is cleverer. **Loyalty, betrayal and ingratitude.** Marullus frames the crowd's behaviour as a moral failure — a betrayal of Pompey's memory. **Appearance versus reality.** Best clothes instead of aprons; statues dressed in decorations; a triumph that looks like conquest but is really civil war. **Language as power.** The scene is a contest of words — the Cobbler's deflections, Marullus's rhetoric — and words do move the crowd, at least for a moment. **Order versus holiday.** The tribunes want a labouring day; the people want a festival; Rome itself seems to be deciding whether it is a working republic or a spectacle for one man. Chapters 11 and 12 develop each of these across the whole Act; here you need only see that every one of them is already alive in the first minutes.

### 14 Historical context

::: history Tribunes, triumphs and a troubling victory
**HISTORICAL RECORD.** *Tribunes of the plebs* were created after the plebeians' first great walk-out from Rome in 494 BC. By the late Republic there were ten of them, elected annually; their persons were sacrosanct (protected by religious law), and they could veto legislation. Their whole purpose was to protect the common people against the power of the great. That is why it is so striking that Shakespeare's tribunes spend the scene abusing the people they exist to defend. *A triumph* was Rome's highest honour, granted by the Senate to a victorious general: a procession from the Campus Martius through the city to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline hill, with captives in chains, wagons of spoils, the general in a four-horse chariot wearing purple and gold and a laurel wreath, and his soldiers following. Traditionally, a triumph celebrated victory over foreign enemies; a triumph over fellow Romans was regarded as improper. Caesar's last triumph, in October 45 BC, celebrated his victory at Munda in Spain over the sons of Pompey — and Plutarch records that Romans resented it precisely because it celebrated the killing of Romans. That resentment is what Marullus voices. Historically, the tribunes Gaius Epidius Marullus and Lucius Caesetius Flavus removed a diadem (a royal headband) from one of Caesar's statues in January 44 BC and were deposed from office for it. Shakespeare moves that episode onto the day of the triumph and the Lupercal (15 February), compressing months into an afternoon. The fuller narrative is in *Chapter 02 — Roman History Before Act 1*.
:::

For the Elizabethan audience of 1599 there was a second layer. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many editors note that the demand that workmen carry the "sign / Of your profession" on a working day sounds more like the regulated London of Shakespeare's own time — with its guilds, apprentices and rules about who might wear what — than anything recorded of ancient Rome. Shakespeare frequently dresses Rome in London clothes, and the Cobbler is as much an Elizabethan tradesman as a Roman one. (The Globe's own holiday audience of tradesmen and apprentices is considered in the insight box on public opinion below.)

### 15 Foreshadowing

Foreshadowing is a hint of what is to come. This scene is full of it, and the professional habit is to name the hint *and* the fulfilment. The crowd's shift from Pompey to Caesar foreshadows its shift from Brutus to Antony in Act 3 Scene 2. Marullus's prayer that the gods "intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude" foreshadows the civil war that will indeed fall on Rome after the assassination — a plague of the tribunes' own side's making. The stripping of Caesar's statues foreshadows the tribunes' punishment reported in Scene 2 and, on a larger scale, the conspirators' attempt to strip Caesar of power itself. Flavius's image of plucking Caesar's "growing feathers" foreshadows the conspiracy's logic: act now, before he is beyond reach — the very argument Brutus will make to himself in Act 2 Scene 1 when he decides that a dangerous creature must be dealt with while it is still in the shell. And the tribunes' contempt for "the vulgar" foreshadows the conspirators' fatal underestimation of the people, who will be the instrument of their ruin.

### 16 Dramatic irony

Dramatic irony occurs when the audience knows something the characters do not. Shakespeare's audience knew the story — the title alone told them Caesar would die. So every line here carries a double charge. The crowd celebrates Caesar; we know his triumph is his last. The tribunes set out to clip Caesar's wings; we know the wings will be clipped by other men, with daggers, and that the tribunes themselves will be silenced first. Marullus accuses the crowd of ingratitude; we know that Caesar will be killed by men who have received his pardons and favours, and that Antony will stand over the body and accuse *them* of exactly this vice. Flavius fears Caesar will "keep us all in servile fearfulness"; we know that after Caesar's death Rome will fall into a fear far worse than anything he imposed. There is a smaller, sharper irony inside the scene itself: the tribunes reduce the crowd to silence — "tongue-tied" — and in the next scene it is they who are "put to silence". The silencers are silenced. Shakespeare rarely lets an act of contempt go unanswered.

### 17 Character development

Neither tribune appears again, so their 'development' happens entirely within the scene, and it is real. **Flavius** moves from a bully shouting at workmen to a political thinker capable of the hawk metaphor; the scene lets us discover that his anger is principled. **Marullus** moves from blunt interrogation to soaring rhetoric — and then to hesitation, when he asks "May we do so?" on a holy day. In two lines Shakespeare gives him a conscience and shows that the pair are not identical: one dares, one doubts. **The Cobbler** begins as a comic evader and ends silent; his arc is the crowd's arc in miniature, from confidence to guilt. **The Carpenter** does not develop; he is the baseline of ordinary obedience against which the Cobbler's wit stands out. And **Caesar**, absent, develops in our minds from a name on a holiday to a hawk that must be plucked — the first stage of the play's long argument about who he really is.

### 18 Scene connections

Scene 1 flows directly into Scene 2: the same day, the same festival, the same crowd — but now the great men enter and the mood is public ceremony rather than street quarrel. The Lupercal that Marullus mentions becomes the race Antony runs. Caesar's "images", stripped here, return when Casca reports the tribunes' punishment. The shouting crowd that Marullus describes from memory becomes the crowd we *hear* shouting offstage in Scene 2 as Caesar refuses the crown. The tribunes' fear of Caesar's flight becomes Cassius's rhetoric of the Colossus (the giant statue a man might walk beneath) and the "narrow world". Scene 3's storm, with its talk of a man grown "prodigious" and "fearful", is the same fear in supernatural dress. Beyond Act 1, this scene's true partner is Act 3 Scene 2, the Forum scene: the same crowd, the same street rhetoric, the same swing of loyalty, but with a corpse on stage and history at stake.

### 19 Future consequences

Trace the chain of cause and effect that begins here:

```chain

↓

↓
Caesar is shown to punish dissent — evidence for the conspirators' fear (Scenes 2–3)
↓

↓
```

The tribunes disappear from the play, but their two acts — the confrontation with the crowd and the defacing of the statues — set both of these chains running. The first exposes the crowd's softness, which the conspirators ignore and Antony exploits. The second exposes Caesar's intolerance of opposition, which the conspirators use as justification. You could argue that Scene 1 quietly supplies the two facts on which the whole tragedy depends.

Here is the difference between an ordinary and an excellent answer to a typical Class IX-style sub-question: *'Why does Marullus call the commoners "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things"?'* [3 marks]

::: weak A weak answer
Marullus is angry with the commoners because they are celebrating Caesar's triumph and not working. He insults them by calling them blocks and stones because they are stupid.
:::

::: strong An excellent answer
Marullus is enraged that the commoners are celebrating Caesar's triumph, which he regards as a victory "over Pompey's blood" — over fellow Romans — rather than a true conquest. He calls them "blocks" and "stones" because, like inert matter, they seem incapable of feeling or memory: they have forgotten how they once cheered "great Pompey" until the Tiber trembled. But they are "worse than senseless things" because stones cannot be ungrateful, whereas the crowd has feelings and yet has betrayed its old hero. The insult thus introduces the theme of the crowd's fickleness, which Shakespeare will develop when the same crowd turns from Brutus to Antony in Act 3.
:::

Notice what the strong answer does: it quotes exactly, explains the logic of the insult rather than merely restating it, names a theme, and connects forward. That is the pattern this book will drill into you.

## WHY SHAKESPEARE BEGINS WITH COMMONERS

Think of the openings Shakespeare rejected. A trumpet, a procession, Caesar in the chariot: that would have made the play Caesar's from the first second. Brutus alone in his orchard: that would have made it a study of one conscience. Cassius whispering to Casca: that would have made it a thriller. Instead the first face we see is a workman's, and the first voice we hear is an official's shouting at him. The choice is so unusual that we should treat it as the play's first argument.

::: insight Why Shakespeare begins with ordinary citizens
One strong reading of *Julius Caesar* — and the reading this chapter follows — is that it is not, finally, the tragedy of one man but the tragedy of a city that cannot decide what it wants. Critics still argue over who the tragic hero is (Caesar? Brutus?), but Shakespeare signals the city's importance from the first line by making the city — in the form of its tradesmen — the first thing on stage. School guides usually say the commoners 'provide comic relief' or 'set the scene'. That undersells the design. The commoners are here because in this play the people are not background: they are the deciding vote. In Act 3 it is the crowd, not the Senate, that determines whether the assassination succeeds politically, and it is the crowd that Antony must win. By giving the people the stage first, Shakespeare tells us where the real power will lie. He also fixes the play's method of judging leaders: we will assess Caesar, Brutus and Antony largely by how they handle exactly this kind of crowd — and by what the crowd does to them. Notice, too, that the workmen are given the play's first jokes and its first act of verbal resistance. Shakespeare refuses to let the people be a faceless mass; he gives them a mind, in the Cobbler, before he shows them behaving like a herd. That order matters. It means that when they later behave like a herd, we cannot excuse it as stupidity. We have seen that they are capable of thought, and chose not to use it.
:::

### The political significance of the crowd

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** In a republic, legitimacy — the recognised right to rule — comes from the people; in a dictatorship, it comes from the person. This scene stages the exact moment when Rome's crowd is transferring its allegiance from the first model to the second. The tribunes, who hold their office *from* the people, discover that the people no longer look to them; they look to Caesar. Shakespeare shows political power as something that flows, like a river — the Tiber is not in the scene by accident — and that can be redirected. The crowd's political significance is therefore twofold. As a body, it is the source of Caesar's strength: Cassius will complain in Scene 2 that Caesar has become a god because men bend to him, and here we see the bending in progress. As a body without memory, it is also the source of everyone's weakness: whatever the crowd gives, it can take away. The tribunes attempt to reclaim the crowd by shame; Caesar (we learn in Scene 2) holds it by spectacle; Brutus will try reason and Antony will try tears, gold and a corpse. Scene 1 is the first round of a contest for the same prize, and the prize is a body of people who, in the end, belong to no one.

### Flavius

Flavius has the first word and the last, which tells you he is the scene's frame. His opening is pure command — "Hence! Home, you idle creatures" — and his instinct throughout is to act: he interrogates, he dismisses, he assigns routes ("Go you down that way towards the Capitol. / This way will I"), and he issues the order that will destroy him: "Disrobe the images". When Marullus hesitates on religious grounds, Flavius brushes it aside in three words — "It is no matter" — and that brusqueness is a character note. He is a man of practical courage and limited caution. Yet Shakespeare gives *him*, not the eloquent Marullus, the scene's most intellectually serious speech, the falconry image. That is a deliberate surprise: the blunt enforcer turns out to have thought most clearly about what Caesar is becoming. Flavius is, in miniature, a preview of the conspirators — decisive, principled, contemptuous of the crowd and blind to consequences. Historically his counterpart was Lucius Caesetius Flavus, deposed from the tribunate for the diadem affair; Shakespeare keeps the deposition (as "put to silence") but never lets us see it, so that Flavius simply vanishes, a small warning of how Caesar's Rome deals with dissent.

### Marullus

Marullus is the interrogator and the orator. He asks the first sharp question — "Where is thy leather apron and thy rule?" — and he is the one the Cobbler dances around, which makes him slightly ridiculous for a few minutes. Then the Cobbler names Caesar, and Marullus becomes a different man. His speech about Pompey is the scene's emotional centre: it moves from savage insult ("You blocks, you stones") to grieving memory (the crowds on the rooftops) to accusation (the flowers strewn for Pompey's conqueror) to religious warning (the plague that ingratitude deserves). This is genuine rhetoric — structured, escalating, aimed at the feelings — and it is the first demonstration in the play of what a well-made speech can do to a crowd. But Shakespeare also gives Marullus a moment of doubt that Flavius lacks: "May we do so? / You know it is the feast of Lupercal." He will insult the people without a qualm, but he fears offending the gods. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** one strong reading is that Marullus represents the conscience of the old Republic — pious, traditional, articulate — while Flavius represents its nerve. Neither, alone, is enough, and together they achieve nothing but their own removal. Historically his counterpart was Gaius Epidius Marullus; the Folio spells the name 'Murellus', and some editions keep that form.

### The Carpenter

The Carpenter speaks a single line, and it is worth asking why Shakespeare bothered with him at all. He is the control in the experiment. Asked his trade, he answers at once and with respect: "Why, sir, a carpenter." He does not joke, does not evade, does not argue. Against this plain obedience the Cobbler's evasions stand out as choices, not as the only way a workman could respond. The Carpenter also establishes the tribunes' expectation — that commoners will answer when questioned — so that the Cobbler's refusal to be pinned down registers as a small act of rebellion. In a scene about how the people can be handled, the Carpenter is the people as the tribunes wish they were: known by their trade, quick to answer, easy to send home. The Cobbler is the people as they actually are.

### The Cobbler

The Cobbler is the first fully alive person in the play. Everything about him is double. His trade is double: a 'cobbler' in Elizabethan English meant both a shoe-mender and a clumsy bungler, which is why Marullus, hearing the word, demands again "But what trade art thou?" — he has heard 'I am a botcher', not 'I am a shoemaker'. His answers are double: each one is true on the surface and mocking underneath. His motives are double: he is out to see Caesar *and* to drum up trade. Even his morality is double-edged — he practises a trade he can use "with a safe / conscience", a phrase that quietly asks whether the tribunes can say the same of theirs. What he never does is lose his temper or overstep. He keeps calling the tribunes 'sir'; he begs them not to be angry; he claims he means nothing but shoes. That is the survival wit of the powerless, and Shakespeare admires it. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the Cobbler shows us that the common people of this play are not fools. They are shrewd, verbal and self-interested — and that makes their later behaviour as a mob more disturbing, not less. When the crowd in Act 3 tears a man apart for his name, we cannot say they lacked the intelligence to know better. The Cobbler proved they had it.

::: insight Why Caesar is not placed at the centre of our understanding at once
A lesser dramatist would have introduced Caesar so that we could judge him for ourselves. Shakespeare makes us meet his reputation before his person — and, crucially, meet it through hostile witnesses. Our first frame for Caesar is the tribunes': a man triumphing over Romans, a man whose statues are dressed like a god's, a hawk to be plucked. Only in Scene 2 do we see him, and by then we are already scanning him for the tyrant. This is a deliberate manipulation of the audience, and it mirrors what the conspirators do to themselves. They too build their Caesar out of fears, reports and images rather than out of the man in front of them. When Caesar does appear he is imperious but also deaf in one ear, superstitious about barrenness, and (Casca tells us) subject to fainting fits — a very human figure who does not quite match the monster of Scene 1. The gap between the reputation and the man is the play's central problem, and Shakespeare plants it by delaying the man. There is a further effect. Because Caesar is a name before he is a body, the play is from the start about 'Caesar' as an idea — the name Cassius will weigh against 'Brutus', the name that will outlive the corpse. Keeping the person offstage lets the name grow first.
:::

### Wordplay and puns: getting every joke

A pun is a joke that depends on one sound carrying two meanings. Elizabethan audiences loved them and expected to hear them fast; you have the luxury of slowing down. Here is every pun in the Cobbler's sequence, explained so that you can both get the joke and see its point — which is that the commoner outwits his social superiors while appearing to be perfectly polite.

**"a mender of bad / soles".** Marullus demands the Cobbler's trade. The Cobbler says he mends bad *soles* — the bottoms of shoes. But the word sounds exactly like *souls*. So he has also claimed to be a mender of bad *souls* — a priest, a moral reformer. The joke is that a humble shoemaker has just described himself in the language of religion, and has also hinted that the tribunes' souls might need mending. It is Flavius who takes it as insolence — his is the next line, "What trade, thou knave? Thou naughty knave, what trade?" — and Marullus has to repeat the question before the Cobbler will go on.

**"be not out with me. / Yet if you be out, sir, I can mend you."** *Out with me* means 'angry with me'. But *out* also means 'having worn-out shoes' — out at the toes, out at the heels. So the sentence says: 'Do not be angry with me; but if your shoes are worn out, I can repair them.' The tribune hears 'I can fix *you*', a claim to correct his betters, and asks what he means. The Cobbler innocently replies: "Why, sir, cobble you" — 'patch your shoes'. Once again the working man has said something cheeky and retreated behind the literal meaning.

**"all that I live by is with the awl."** An *awl* is the pointed tool a cobbler uses to pierce leather. The sentence means 'I earn my whole living with the awl.' But *awl* sounds like *all*, so it also says 'everything I live by is with all' — a nonsense that keeps the tribunes guessing, and a boast that his one small tool is his 'all'. This is the pun a struggling student most often misses, because 'awl' is an unfamiliar word; learn it and the line becomes funny.

**"withal".** The Cobbler says he meddles with no tradesman's matters nor women's matters, "but withal I am indeed, sir, a / surgeon to old shoes". *Withal* means 'nevertheless' or 'all the same'. Said aloud, however, it is *with awl* — so he is also saying 'but with my awl I am a surgeon to old shoes'. The pun works both ways at once, and it is the third time in a few lines that he has smuggled the tool of his trade into the conversation. (The bracketing about 'women's matters' is a cheeky aside — he protests, with a wink, that he keeps out of other men's business and other men's wives.)

**"surgeon to old shoes: when they are in great danger, / I recover them."** Here the Cobbler elevates cobbling into medicine. He is a *surgeon*; the shoes are patients in *great danger*; and he *recovers* them — which means both 'restores them to health', as a doctor does, and 're-covers' them, puts a new covering of leather on them, as a cobbler does. The mock-grandeur is the point: he is parodying the way important men (tribunes, for instance) inflate their own work.

**"As proper men as ever trod upon / neat's leather have gone upon my handiwork."** *Proper* means 'handsome, fine'; *neat's leather* is cowhide; *to tread upon neat's leather* is simply to wear shoes. So: 'the finest men who ever wore shoes have walked in shoes I made.' It is a tradesman's boast — and, glancing at the tribunes' feet, a hint that even they may be standing on his work. The great depend on the small.

**"to wear out their shoes, to / get myself into more work."** Asked why he leads the crowd about the streets, the Cobbler first gives the self-interested answer: the more they walk, the sooner their shoes wear out, the more business he gets. This is not strictly a pun but a comic inversion: the tribunes expect a political motive and receive a commercial one. It also quietly deflates the whole notion of the crowd's 'loyalty' — some of them, at least, are here for their own reasons.

Put together, the sequence shows a man who is never rude and never straightforward, who answers every question truthfully and none of them helpfully, and who wins every exchange while apparently losing his social position. That is why the tribunes' contempt for "the vulgar" rings false to the audience before it is even spoken: we have just watched a member of the vulgar out-think them.

::: insight Why public opinion is introduced immediately
Most tragedies begin with the private lives of the great and only later show the public consequences. *Julius Caesar* reverses the order: the first thing we see is public opinion in action — a crowd choosing whom to cheer. Shakespeare does this because public opinion is not a backdrop to this play's politics; it is the mechanism. Caesar's power rests on the crowd's love, which Casca will describe in Scene 2 as clapping and hissing him like players in a theatre. The conspirators' plan will succeed or fail according to whether the crowd accepts their explanation. Antony's revenge will be accomplished entirely by turning the crowd. By introducing the crowd first, and showing it already halfway through one change of heart, Shakespeare makes us watch every later speech as an attempt to move this same body. There is an uncomfortable edge to this in a theatre. The audience at the Globe in 1599 was itself a paying crowd of tradesmen and apprentices on a holiday afternoon, being 'worked on' by speeches. The scene may be inviting them — and us — to notice how easily we are moved.
:::

::: insight The irony that the protectors of the people despise the people
The office of tribune existed for one reason: to protect the plebeians from the powerful. Yet Shakespeare's tribunes address the plebeians as "idle creatures", "thou knave", "naughty knave", "saucy / fellow", "blocks", "stones", "worse than senseless / things", "hard hearts", "cruel men" and, finally, "the vulgar". Their sympathy for Pompey is not sympathy for the people; it is sympathy for the Republic's institutions, of which they are officers. This irony is not a slip; on one strong reading it is the play's diagnosis of why the Republic falls. The republicans — the tribunes here, Brutus and Cassius later — claim to act for Rome but hold Romans in contempt, and so they never trouble to win them. Caesar, by contrast, is loved precisely because he gives the crowd what it wants: a triumph, a holiday, decorated statues, a spectacle in the marketplace. Brutus will repeat the tribunes' error on a grand scale in Act 3 Scene 2, assuming that a crowd will follow reason and then walking away, leaving Antony to speak. The tribunes' contempt is therefore the first appearance of the flaw that kills the conspiracy. It is also a real moral question the play leaves open: can you defend liberty on behalf of people you do not respect?
:::

### Class

The scene is a collision of ranks, and Shakespeare marks rank by every means available. By *form*: verse for the tribunes, prose for the workmen. By *address*: the tribunes use 'thou' and 'thy' to the workmen — the familiar form used to inferiors, children and servants (Marullus's one polite "You, sir" lasts a single line before he too drops to "thou") — while the Cobbler answers with the respectful 'sir' and 'you'. By *insult*: "thou knave", "naughty knave", "saucy fellow", "the vulgar" all assume the right of the higher to name the lower. By *costume*: the tribunes' first complaint is literally that the workmen are dressed above their station, in "best apparel" rather than the apron and rule that would label them. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the scene exposes a paradox at the heart of Roman (and Elizabethan) order. The upper ranks need the lower to be visibly, legibly (readably, at a glance) lower — hence the demand for the "sign / Of your profession". A crowd in holiday clothes is a crowd that has, for a day, stopped being legible, and that is what frightens the officials before Caesar's name is even mentioned. The Cobbler's wit is a further breach: intelligence is not supposed to run upward from the apron to the toga. Class in this scene is therefore not just background; it is the first source of tension, and it is inseparable from the political fear that follows, because Caesar's power is built on exactly this crowd of the 'wrong' class.

### Public opinion

Public opinion in this scene is visible, audible and physical. It has a costume (best attire), a gesture (strewing flowers), a sound (the universal shout that once shook the Tiber) and a location (walls, battlements, towers, windows, chimney tops). Shakespeare never treats it as an abstraction; he shows it as bodies in a street. He also shows its two faces within a few minutes: the confident holiday crowd of the opening and the guilty, "tongue-tied" crowd of the exit. That change is produced by nothing more than one angry speech. The lesson the play draws — and asks us to carry forward to Act 3 — is that public opinion in Rome is a resource anyone with sufficient eloquence can tap, and that no one owns it for long. The tribunes tap it here for a moment; Caesar taps it in Scene 2 with the theatre of refusing a crown; Brutus and Antony will tap it in turn. It is also worth noticing what public opinion is *for* in this scene: it is about honour. The crowd honours Caesar with a holiday; Marullus says the honour belongs to Pompey's memory. Public opinion is thus, from the first, a contest over who deserves Rome's love, and the play never lets that contest rest.

### Political loyalty

Loyalty in Scene 1 comes in three kinds, and the exam rewards a candidate who can tell them apart. The **crowd's loyalty** is to a person and a spectacle: whoever is triumphing today. It is warm, real and short-lived. The **tribunes' loyalty** is to an institution — the Republic — and to a dead man who symbolised it. It is principled and durable, but cold towards the actual people it claims to serve. The **Cobbler's loyalty**, if he has one, is to his own livelihood and his own dignity; he will cheer Caesar and sell shoes to Caesar's crowd. Shakespeare places these three side by side without telling us which is right. But he does show which is most powerful: the crowd's. Marullus can shame it for a moment; he cannot redirect it. And by Scene 2 it is shouting for Caesar again. The scene's quiet conclusion is that in Rome political loyalty has come loose from institutions and attached itself to personalities — which is exactly the condition in which a republic turns into a monarchy.

### The fickleness of the Roman crowd

'Fickle' means changeable, unreliable in affection. The word is the single most useful keyword this scene gives you, and you must be able to prove it from the text rather than merely assert it. The proof is Marullus's own contrast: the same people who once sat "The livelong day, with patient expectation, / To see great Pompey pass the streets of Rome" now "strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood". Same crowd, opposite hero, no visible embarrassment. Then Shakespeare gives a second proof inside the scene: this loudly celebrating crowd is silenced and sent home by one speech, "tongue-tied in their guiltiness". A body that can be shamed out of a festival in a few minutes is a body that can be shouted into anything. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** Shakespeare is not simply sneering at the poor. He is diagnosing a political condition: a people that has lost its institutions has nothing to be loyal *to* except whoever is most present and most impressive. Fickleness is the symptom; the disease is the collapse of the Republic's structures. Casca's report in Scene 2 (the crowd clapping and hissing Caesar like actors in a theatre) and the Forum scene in Act 3 are the later stages of the same illness. A first-class answer names the symptom, the disease and the later stages.

### Pompey

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus (106–48 BC) — 'Pompey the Great' — was the most celebrated Roman general of the generation before Caesar's. He celebrated three triumphs (in 79, 71 and 61 BC), an almost unheard-of honour, and he was immensely popular in Rome. He was for a time Caesar's political partner, in the alliance historians call the First Triumvirate, and Caesar's son-in-law, having married Caesar's daughter Julia, who died in 54 BC. When the alliance broke down, Pompey led the Senate's side in the civil war; Caesar defeated him at Pharsalus in August 48 BC, and Pompey fled to Egypt, where he was murdered on arrival. His sons carried on the fight until Caesar crushed them at Munda in Spain in March 45 BC. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** uses none of these details. Pompey exists in Scene 1 purely as a memory of love — "great Pompey" whose chariot the people once climbed chimney tops to see. That is deliberate. Shakespeare wants Pompey to stand for what the crowd has forgotten, not for a political programme. But the name will not stay buried. The conspirators will gather at "Pompey's Porch" in Scene 3, and Caesar will fall in Act 3 at the base of Pompey's statue. The dead rival is present at every stage of the play, and this scene is where Shakespeare first plants him.

### Caesar's triumph — and why a triumph over "Pompey's blood" is troubling

Marullus's questions define a proper triumph by what this one lacks. "What conquest brings he home?" — a triumph should mark a conquest, new territory for Rome. "What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?" — a triumph should display foreign kings and princes walking in chains behind the general's chariot, the living proof of Rome's victory over outsiders. The implied answer to both questions is: none. Caesar's victory was over Romans. So what walks behind his chariot, in Marullus's furious phrase, is "Pompey's blood": the defeated sons of Pompey, the blood of Roman citizens spilt by Roman swords, and the bloodline of a man the crowd once worshipped. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Plutarch reports that Romans did indeed feel that Caesar should not have triumphed for a victory that destroyed the family of a great Roman, and that it grieved them to see him celebrate the ruin of fellow citizens. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** Shakespeare makes this the scene's moral core because it turns a party into a question. Is the crowd cheering Rome's glory, or Rome's self-destruction? The word 'blood' also begins the play's long bloodline of imagery — Caesar's blood on the conspirators' hands and swords, the blood Antony will show the crowd — so that the first blood mentioned in the play is the blood of a civil war Caesar has already won, and the last is the blood of the civil war his death will start.

### The statues, the "ceremonies" and the "trophies"

Flavius orders: "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies", and adds: "Let no images / Be hung with Caesar's trophies." Three words need unpacking. *Images* are statues of Caesar standing in public places. *Ceremonies* here means ceremonial decorations — the scarves, garlands and ornaments hung on the statues in his honour (Casca, in Scene 2, calls them "scarves"). *Trophies* are tokens of victory, the spoils and emblems of Caesar's triumph. The verb *disrobe* — to strip of clothing — treats the statues as dressed figures, which is exactly what alarms the tribunes: a decorated statue is a step towards a worshipped one. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the real tribunes removed a royal diadem from a statue of Caesar, which is why the episode had such a charge — a diadem was the emblem of a king, and Rome had hated the very word 'king' since it expelled its last one in 509 BC. Shakespeare softens the diadem into "ceremonies" and "trophies", but the meaning survives: the tribunes are trying to stop a man being turned into an idol. There is a further irony that many critics notice. In stripping the statues, the tribunes are themselves attacking *images* of Caesar, not Caesar — just as, in the Forum, the crowd will attack a poet who merely shares a conspirator's name. Rome is a city that fights over signs.

### Removing the decorations

The tribunes' plan is small, physical and symbolic, and it is important to see all three qualities. It is *small*: two men with their hands, pulling cloth off stone. It is *physical*: not a speech in the Senate but an act in the street, in the same public space the crowd occupied. And it is *symbolic*: they cannot strip Caesar of his victory or his dictatorship, so they strip the emblems of them, hoping that to undress the image is to shrink the man. Flavius says so in his falcon speech: these are "growing feathers" to be "plucked". The audience, however, can see what the tribunes cannot — that the man is not the image, and that pulling scarves off statues will not slow a dictator but only provoke him. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** confirms this in Scene 2, when Casca reports that Marullus and Flavius "are put to silence" for exactly this act. The phrase is ambiguous — it could mean removed from office or something worse — and the text leaves it open; historically the tribunes were deposed. Either way, the first act of resistance in the play fails within a scene, and it fails because it mistakes the symbol for the substance. That is a warning the conspirators will not heed.

### The tribunes' attitude: contempt, fear and duty

Three attitudes coexist in the tribunes, and the scene's realism comes from the mixture. **Contempt for the crowd** is the loudest — the insults are relentless, and even the softer "good countrymen" is followed by an order to go and weep. **Fear of Caesar** is the deepest; it surfaces in the hawk image, in the anxiety about a man who would "soar above the view of men", and in the phrase that names what they fear for themselves — "servile fearfulness", the condition of slaves. **Republican duty** is what they would say they are acting from: as tribunes they are the constitutional guardians of the people's liberty, and defacing the emblems of one-man rule is, in their eyes, exactly their job. The trouble is that the three do not fit together. Duty to the people is hard to square with contempt for them. Fear of tyranny is hard to distinguish from resentment at losing influence. Shakespeare does not resolve the contradiction; he displays it, and then lets it reappear, enlarged, in Brutus and Cassius.

### Republican concerns

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Rome's republican constitution rested on a few principles: no single man should hold supreme power; offices should be shared, elected and time-limited; the Senate should deliberate; the people, through their tribunes, should be protected. The word *libertas* — liberty — summed up the ideal, and the word *rex* — king — summed up its opposite. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** compresses all of this into Flavius's closing quatrain (four-line group). A man who soars "above the view of men" has escaped the shared, level world of the Republic; a Rome kept in "servile fearfulness" has lost its liberty. What makes the passage republican rather than merely hostile is the phrase "us all": Flavius fears not for himself but for the collective. This is the concern Cassius will voice with far more bitterness in Scene 2 — that one man should "bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus" while others walk beneath — and that Brutus will make the basis of his decision. Scene 1 gives the republican argument in its purest, least personal form, before jealousy (Cassius) and self-doubt (Brutus) have complicated it.

### The dramatic purpose of the opening

Gather the threads. The opening (1) establishes setting and occasion economically; (2) introduces the crowd as a character and shows its fickleness in action; (3) supplies the political situation and its danger through hostile witnesses; (4) creates the first image of Caesar as a man to be feared, before we meet him; (5) provides comedy that both relaxes the audience and sharpens the class conflict; (6) demonstrates the power of rhetoric over a crowd, the play's central mechanism; (7) plants the name of Pompey, which will haunt the plot; (8) sets in motion the first act of resistance and its punishment; and (9) tunes the audience's ear to the prose–verse distinction it will need throughout. Above all, it suggests — before we have met a single great man — that the play's subject may be not one man's fall but a city's choice. If you can say all this about a scene in which the protagonist never appears, you have understood why Shakespeare is Shakespeare.

### From Pompey to Caesar: what the shift tells us about public loyalty

The scene's argument rests on a before-and-after that Marullus draws for us. Set it out as a table and the pattern becomes a prediction.

| The crowd's behaviour | For Pompey (in Marullus's memory) | For Caesar (today, in the scene) | What this predicts for Act 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where they went | Climbed "walls and battlements, / To towers and windows", even "chimney tops" | Fill the streets in a "holiday" crowd | They will pack the Forum to hear the conspirators explain the murder |
| How long they waited | Sat "The livelong day, with patient expectation" | Take a whole day off work to "see Caesar" | They will listen patiently to Brutus — and then, just as patiently, to Antony |
| What they did with their bodies | Brought "infants in your arms" to watch | Wear "best attire" and "strew flowers in his way" | They will carry Caesar's body, then carry fire to the conspirators' houses |
| What noise they made | "an universal shout" that made "Tiber trembled underneath her banks" | Shouts heard offstage in Scene 2 as Caesar refuses the crown | They will shout for Brutus to be Caesar, then howl for revenge with Antony |
| How they responded to a speech | — | Fall silent, "tongue-tied", at Marullus's rebuke | Brutus will silence them with reason; Antony will unleash them with grief |

Read across each row and the lesson is the same: the crowd's behaviour is constant; only its object changes. It does not love Pompey less than it loved him or Caesar more than it will love Brutus. It loves *whoever is in the chariot*. That is what Marullus cannot forgive and what the conspirators will fatally forget. When you write about the Forum scene in Act 3, this table is your evidence that Shakespeare set up the crowd's behaviour from the first scene, and that its swing to Antony is not a surprise but a fulfilment.

::: quote The two halves of Marullus's contrast
> **Marullus:** Many a time and oft / Have you climbed up to walls and battlements, / To towers and windows, yea, to chimney tops, / Your infants in your arms, and there have sat / The livelong day, with patient expectation, / To see great Pompey pass the streets of Rome.

> **Marullus:** And do you now put on your best attire? / And do you now cull out a holiday? / And do you now strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood?

Learn the first as the 'then' and the second as the 'now'; the whole theme of fickleness lives in the gap between them.

## LINE-BY-LINE TEACHING SYSTEM

This is the part of the chapter to return to before any test on the scene. Ten passages are taught, chosen because they matter to plot, character or theme, are frequently quoted, or feed later events. Each passage is taught under the same sixteen labels so that you can scan for exactly what you need. Trivial lines are deliberately left out; do not waste revision time on them.

### Passage 1 — Flavius clears the street

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Flavius:** Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home! / Is this a holiday? What, know you not, / Being mechanical, you ought not walk / Upon a labouring day without the sign / Of your profession?

**Simple English:** Get away from here and go home, you lazy creatures! Is today a holiday? Don't you know that as tradesmen you are not allowed to walk about on a working day without the tools or dress that show what your trade is?

**Difficult words:** *Hence* — away from here. *Idle* — lazy, unoccupied. *Creatures* — living things; used of people, it is contemptuous, as if they were animals. *Mechanical* — belonging to the class of manual workers (here used as a noun: 'you, being tradesmen'). *Labouring day* — a working day. *Sign of your profession* — the apron, tools or badge that identify a trade.

**Literal meaning:** The tribune orders the crowd home and claims they are breaking a rule by being out on a working day without the visible marks of their trades.

**Subtext:** Flavius already knows perfectly well that the crowd regards this as a holiday; the question "Is this a holiday?" is not a request for information but a challenge. He is refusing to recognise the holiday, and therefore refusing to recognise what it celebrates.

**Hidden meaning:** The rule about the "sign / Of your profession" is a demand that the lower classes remain visibly labelled. A workman in his best clothes has, for a day, escaped his label, and that small liberty is the first thing authority wants to remove.

**Character psychology:** Flavius is a man who begins with orders, not questions. His contempt ("idle creatures") comes before his argument. He is confident of his authority and irritated that it is being ignored.

**Political meaning:** The Republic's officials try to control public space and the public's time. A crowd that decides for itself when to celebrate is a crowd that is slipping out of institutional control — and towards Caesar.

**Historical context:** No Roman law requiring workmen to carry their tools is known; editors generally treat the detail as Elizabethan colouring, reflecting a London of guilds and regulated dress. Shakespeare gives Rome the social texture his audience knew.

**Literary devices:** Imperative mood (a string of commands); rhetorical question ("Is this a holiday?"); contemptuous epithet ("idle creatures"); the whole speech in verse, marking rank against the prose that will answer it.

**Effect:** The play opens in the middle of a quarrel, with raised voices, so the audience is instantly alert. The tone is tense and a little absurd — a magistrate scolding shoemakers — which prepares the comedy to come.

**Dramatic significance:** These are the play's first words. Shakespeare chooses to begin not with grandeur but with conflict between classes over a public celebration, announcing that this will be a play about the crowd and its controllers.

**Foreshadowing:** Officials failing to control the crowd by shouting at it foreshadows the far greater failure of the conspirators to control the crowd in Act 3.

**Scene connection:** Flavius will end the scene as he begins it, driving "the vulgar from the streets" — but by then his motive has been made explicit.

**ICSE keywords:** 'imperative', 'class', 'hierarchy', 'contempt', 'authority', 'public space'.

**Exam application:** If asked how Shakespeare establishes conflict at the start of the play, you could write: 'The very first line is a command — "Hence! Home, you idle creatures" — spoken by an official to workmen, so that the play opens on a collision between authority and the common people that will define its politics.'

### Passage 2 — Marullus and the missing apron

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Marullus:** Where is thy leather apron and thy rule? / What dost thou with thy best apparel on?

**Simple English:** Where is your leather apron and your ruler? What are you doing dressed in your best clothes?

**Difficult words:** *Leather apron* — the protective apron worn by craftsmen such as carpenters. *Rule* — a measuring ruler, the carpenter's tool. *Apparel* — clothing. *Thy* and *thou* — the singular 'your' and 'you', used to social inferiors.

**Literal meaning:** Marullus points out that the Carpenter is not wearing the apron and carrying the ruler that would mark his trade, and is instead dressed for a festival.

**Subtext:** 'You are out of your place.' The complaint is not about hygiene or safety; it is about a workman being visually indistinguishable from his betters.

**Hidden meaning:** Clothes are signs, and the scene is obsessed with signs: aprons, tools, best attire, and later the "ceremonies" on Caesar's statues. Marullus wants the workman re-labelled, just as he will want Caesar's statues un-labelled. Both are attempts to control what things *look like*.

**Character psychology:** Marullus is precise and observant — he notices exactly which items are missing — and he expects deference. He speaks to the Carpenter as a master to a servant.

**Political meaning:** Social order and political order are one system. A crowd in holiday dress honouring Caesar is a double breach: of class rules and of republican loyalty.

**Historical context:** The leather apron and rule are the tools of an Elizabethan tradesman as much as a Roman one. The 'thou' of superiority to inferiors was standard Elizabethan usage.

**Literary devices:** Rhetorical questions; concrete visual imagery (apron, rule, apparel) that lets the audience see the class contrast; the formal 'thou' as a marker of rank.

**Effect:** The audience sees, through the tribune's eyes, a man dressed above his station, and begins to sense that the tribunes care more about order than about people.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the moment the Carpenter is dismissed and the Cobbler takes over; Marullus's second question, "You, sir, what trade are you?", opens the pun sequence.

**Foreshadowing:** The theme of appearance versus reality — best clothes hiding a workman, decorations turning a statue into an idol — will grow into the play's concern with false shows (Cassius's forged letters, Caesar's public modesty over the crown).

**Scene connection:** The "best apparel" here becomes the "best attire" of Marullus's later speech; the same detail is first a class offence and then a moral one.

**ICSE keywords:** 'appearance versus reality', 'social order', 'costume as sign', 'class contempt'.

**Exam application:** In a question about how the tribunes treat the commoners, you might write: 'Marullus's demand "Where is thy leather apron and thy rule?" shows that he sees the workman purely as his trade, and resents any sign that the common people can step outside their allotted place.'

### Passage 3 — The Cobbler's pun sequence

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cobbler:** A trade, sir, that I hope I may use with a safe / conscience, which is indeed, sir, a mender of bad / soles.

> **Cobbler:** Nay, I beseech you, sir, be not out with me. / Yet if you be out, sir, I can mend you.

> **Cobbler:** Truly, sir, all that I live by is with the / awl. I meddle with no tradesman's matters nor / women's matters, but withal I am indeed, sir, a / surgeon to old shoes: when they are in great danger, / I recover them. As proper men as ever trod upon / neat's leather have gone upon my handiwork.

**Simple English:** My trade, sir, is one I can practise with a clear conscience: I mend bad soles. Please don't be angry with me — but if your shoes are worn out, I can fix them. Honestly, sir, I make my whole living with my awl; I don't interfere in other tradesmen's business or in women's business, but all the same I am a doctor to old shoes, and when they are in danger I restore them. Some very fine men have walked about in shoes I made.

**Difficult words:** *Safe conscience* — a clear conscience. *Soles* — shoe bottoms (heard as 'souls'). *Out with me* — angry with me; *out* also means 'worn out at the toes'. *Mend* — repair; also reform morally. *Awl* — a pointed tool for piercing leather (heard as 'all'). *Meddle* — interfere. *Withal* — nevertheless (heard as 'with awl'). *Surgeon* — doctor. *Recover* — restore to health, and re-cover with leather. *Proper* — handsome. *Neat's leather* — cowhide. *Handiwork* — the work of my hands.

**Literal meaning:** The Cobbler describes his trade in a chain of double meanings, never quite giving the plain word the tribunes want, and ends with a boast about the quality of his customers.

**Subtext:** Each pun says two things: a humble one to the tribunes and a cheeky one to the audience. The 'mender of bad souls' who can 'mend' angry officials is quietly claiming moral superiority over them.

**Hidden meaning:** The phrase about a "safe / conscience" implies that there are trades one cannot practise so safely — and the men in front of him are politicians. His boast that fine men have walked on his handiwork hints that the great literally stand on the labour of the small.

**Character psychology:** The Cobbler is quick, confident, self-protective and proud of his craft. He enjoys the game and knows exactly where the line is: he never drops the "sir", never refuses outright, never gives the tribunes a charge to arrest him on.

**Political meaning:** The powerless resist power through language. This is the only weapon the crowd has, and the Cobbler wields it with skill — which is what makes the crowd's later silence so significant.

**Historical context:** Puns were a beloved Elizabethan entertainment; the cobbler-as-witty-underdog is a stock figure of London comedy that Shakespeare transplants to Rome. A 'cobbler' could also mean a bungler, which is why Marullus at first refuses to accept the word as a trade.

**Literary devices:** Pun (soles/souls, out/out, mend/mend, awl/all, withal/with awl, recover/re-cover); metaphor ("surgeon to old shoes"); mock-heroic exaggeration (grand language for a small matter: "in great danger"); prose, marking the speaker's class and comic register.

**Effect:** Laughter — and a shift of the audience's sympathy towards the workman and away from the sneering officials. The comedy also slows the scene so that the revelation about Caesar lands harder.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the play's first display of verbal intelligence, and it belongs to a commoner. Shakespeare establishes that the people are not fools before he shows them behaving like a herd.

**Foreshadowing:** The clever, self-interested Cobbler foreshadows the plebeians of Act 3, who are equally quick — they interrupt, judge and respond — but whose quickness is turned to violence.

**Scene connection:** The Cobbler's talkativeness makes his silence after Marullus's speech ("tongue-tied") a measurable change; the same crowd will be heard shouting offstage in Scene 2.

**ICSE keywords:** 'pun', 'wordplay', 'comic relief' (use with care — the comedy has a purpose), 'prose', 'social inversion', 'wit'.

**Exam application:** Asked to explain the humour of the Cobbler's replies, you could write: 'When the Cobbler calls himself "a mender of bad soles" he plays on the sound of 'souls', so that a shoemaker appears to claim the priest's role of mending souls; the pun lets a commoner mock the tribunes while seeming perfectly respectful.'

### Passage 4 — The real reason for the holiday

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cobbler:** But indeed, sir, we / make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his / triumph.

**Simple English:** But truly, sir, we are taking the day off to see Caesar and to celebrate his triumph.

**Difficult words:** *Make holiday* — take a day off, keep a festival. *Triumph* — the formal victory procession granted to a Roman general.

**Literal meaning:** After all his jokes, the Cobbler gives the straight answer: the crowd is out to watch Caesar's triumphal procession.

**Subtext:** The word "indeed" marks the change of register (the level or style of speech) — the joking stops. The Cobbler seems to expect the tribunes to find this reason perfectly acceptable, which tells us how normal Caesar-worship has become on the street.

**Hidden meaning:** The pronoun *we* is the important word. The Cobbler speaks for the whole crowd; the individual wit has become the voice of a collective loyalty. And "rejoice" is exactly the word Marullus will seize on: rejoicing implies a good outcome, and the tribunes deny there is one.

**Character psychology:** The Cobbler is genuinely, uncomplicatedly pleased about the holiday. There is no political calculation here; he likes a spectacle and a day off.

**Political meaning:** The people's affection for Caesar is casual and unreflective — which is both what makes it strong (it asks no questions) and what makes it fragile (it can be shamed).

**Historical context:** The triumph in question corresponds to Caesar's Spanish triumph of October 45 BC after Munda, though Shakespeare never names it and fuses it with the Lupercal of February 44 BC.

**Literary devices:** Plain statement after a run of puns — the contrast itself is the device; the pronoun *we* signals collective identity.

**Effect:** The audience, which has been laughing, is suddenly given the political fact, and the mood changes at once. The line acts as a hinge between comedy and anger.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the first time Caesar is named in the play, and he is named as the object of the crowd's rejoicing — the object, in other words, of public opinion. Everything before was preparation; everything after is response.

**Foreshadowing:** A crowd that makes holiday to see Caesar will make a far darker holiday of his funeral in Act 3, and will 'rejoice' with fire.

**Scene connection:** Marullus's "Wherefore rejoice?" picks up the Cobbler's "rejoice" word for word; the speech that follows is an answer to this line.

**ICSE keywords:** 'public opinion', 'popularity', 'spectacle', 'collective voice', 'triumph'.

**Exam application:** To show that Caesar's popularity is established before he appears, write: 'Caesar is first named not by a senator but by a cobbler, who says the people "make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph" — the crowd's love for him is presented as a simple fact of Roman life before we ever see the man.'

### Passage 5 — Marullus remembers Pompey

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Marullus:** Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? / What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels? / You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things! / O you hard hearts, you cruel men of Rome, / Knew you not Pompey?

> **Marullus:** Have you not made an universal shout, / That Tiber trembled underneath her banks / To hear the replication of your sounds / Made in her concave shores?

**Simple English:** Why rejoice? What conquest has he won? What conquered rulers walk in chains behind his chariot to make his triumph glorious? You lumps of wood, you stones, you things with less feeling than objects that have no feeling at all! You hard-hearted, cruel Romans — did you not know Pompey? … Didn't you all shout together so loudly that the river Tiber shook beneath its banks at the echo of your voices bouncing off its hollow shores?

**Difficult words:** *Wherefore* — why. *Conquest* — victory over an enemy, gaining territory. *Tributaries* — defeated rulers or peoples obliged to pay tribute. *Grace* — adorn, do honour to. *Captive bonds* — the chains of prisoners. *Senseless* — without senses or feeling. *Universal* — made by everyone. *Replication* — echo, reverberation. *Concave* — hollowed out, curving inward.

**Literal meaning:** Marullus argues that Caesar's triumph is empty because it brings no foreign conquest or captives; he insults the crowd for its lack of feeling and reminds them how they once cheered Pompey so loudly that the river seemed to shake.

**Subtext:** 'You are cheering a man who killed Romans, and you have forgotten the Roman you used to love.' The insults are meant to hurt because the memory that follows is meant to shame.

**Hidden meaning:** The order of ideas matters. Marullus first tries logic (what conquest? what captives?), then abuse (blocks, stones), then memory (Pompey). The speech is a lesson in how to move a crowd: from reason to feeling. Also, the trembling Tiber is a picture of the crowd's power — a shout that shakes a river is a force any politician would want to own.

**Character psychology:** Marullus is grief-stricken as much as angry. The detail of the "infants in your arms" and the long wait on the rooftops is remembered lovingly; his contempt for the crowd is the contempt of a man who once saw them do something he admired.

**Political meaning:** A triumph over Romans is not a triumph; celebrating it is a civic sin. The crowd's shift from Pompey to Caesar is presented as the moment Rome's loyalty came unmoored from its own past.

**Historical context:** Pompey's three triumphs and popularity, and Plutarch's report that Romans resented the Spanish triumph, are set out in the history box and the Pompey section above. What this passage adds is staging: a triumph passed through the city to the Capitol, so the crowd on walls and rooftops is historically plausible, and the Tiber, Rome's river, was literally within earshot of the route.

**Literary devices:** Rhetorical questions in series; apostrophe ("O you hard hearts"); metaphor ("blocks", "stones"); climax — the insults intensify to "worse than senseless things"; hyperbole and personification (the Tiber "trembled", with feminine "her banks"); epithet ("great Pompey"); alliteration in "Tiber trembled".

**Effect:** The scene's temperature soars. The audience feels the force of real oratory for the first time and, whatever it thinks of the tribunes, is made to see the crowd as changeable.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the first great speech of the play and the first attempt to move the crowd by rhetoric. It sets a pattern that Brutus and Antony will follow in the Forum.

**Foreshadowing:** The "universal shout" for Pompey foreshadows the shouts for Caesar heard in Scene 2 and the shouts for Brutus and then Antony in Act 3 — the same sound, redirected each time.

**Scene connection:** Marullus's "Wherefore rejoice?" answers the Cobbler's "rejoice"; his vision of Pompey's chariot is the mirror image of the triumph the crowd has come to see.

**ICSE keywords:** 'rhetorical question', 'apostrophe', 'hyperbole', 'personification', 'fickleness', 'ingratitude', 'oratory', 'civil war'.

**Exam application:** When explaining why the tribunes are angry, write: 'Marullus asks "What conquest brings he home?" because a true triumph should display foreign captives; Caesar's, he implies, displays only "Pompey's blood", so the crowd is celebrating the death of fellow Romans and forgetting the Pompey for whom they once shouted until "Tiber trembled underneath her banks".'

### Passage 6 — The three accusing questions

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Marullus:** And do you now put on your best attire? / And do you now cull out a holiday? / And do you now strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood?

**Simple English:** And now you dress up in your finest clothes? And now you pick out a day to make a holiday? And now you scatter flowers in the path of the man whose triumph is built on the blood of Pompey's family?

**Difficult words:** *Attire* — clothing. *Cull out* — pick out, select (as one picks the best from a heap). *Strew* — scatter. *In his way* — in his path, on the road he travels. *Triumph* — the victory procession. *Pompey's blood* — Pompey's kin (his sons, defeated in Spain) and, by extension, the Roman blood shed in the civil war.

**Literal meaning:** Marullus turns from the memory of the crowd's love for Pompey to their behaviour today, listing three festive acts — dressing up, taking a holiday, scattering flowers — and naming the man they honour as Pompey's destroyer.

**Subtext:** Every *now* is an accusation. The word insists on the distance between then and now, between the rooftops crowded for Pompey and the flowers strewn for his conqueror. The speech does not ask the crowd to think; it asks them to remember, and to be ashamed of what remembering reveals.

**Hidden meaning:** The three acts are the three ways a crowd makes a man great: by its appearance (dressing for him), by its time (giving him a day) and by its gestures (honouring his path). Marullus is describing the manufacture of greatness from below. The hidden point is that Caesar's triumph is not something done *to* the crowd but something the crowd is *doing* — his glory is their gift. If they can give it, they can withdraw it, which is what the whole play goes on to show.

**Character psychology:** Marullus's anger has found its shape. The first part of his speech was a torrent of insults; this part is disciplined — three parallel questions, each a notch more serious, closing on the one word that cannot be answered. He is a man who, once he has the crowd's attention, knows exactly how to use it.

**Political meaning:** A triumph is only a triumph if Rome agrees it is. By refusing to agree — by calling it a procession "over Pompey's blood" — Marullus denies Caesar's legitimacy at the level where legitimacy is made: the street. This is a republican official telling the people that their applause is a political act with consequences, not an innocent holiday.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** flowers and garlands were indeed strewn and worn at Roman festivals and triumphs, and Caesar's Spanish triumph of October 45 BC celebrated his victory at Munda over Pompey's sons, Gnaeus and Sextus; Gnaeus was killed, Sextus escaped. Plutarch records that Romans grieved to see Caesar triumph over the ruin of a great Roman house. Marullus's phrase compresses that grievance into two words.

**Literary devices:** Anaphora — the repeated opening "And do you now" — building to a climax; a series of rhetorical questions; synecdoche in "Pompey's blood" (blood for kin and for the cause); antithesis between the remembered devotion to Pompey and the present devotion to Caesar; the strong verb *strew* sets a vivid, visual image against the abstract charge of ingratitude.

**Effect:** The audience hears the speech tighten like a rope. The repetition makes the crowd's actions seem compulsive and thoughtless, and the final question lands with the weight of a verdict. In the theatre this is the moment the laughter of the pun sequence is fully extinguished.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the rhetorical climax of the scene and the first time in the play that a speaker uses structured repetition to overpower a crowd. Antony will do the same in Act 3 Scene 2 with far greater skill, repeating that Brutus is an honourable man until the word turns against him. Shakespeare shows us the technique in its plain form before he shows it in its masterly one.

**Foreshadowing:** The crowd that strews flowers for Caesar's chariot will, in Act 3, make a procession of a different kind — carrying his body, then carrying fire. The flowers of the triumph and the flames of the funeral are two ends of the same loyalty.

**Scene connection:** The "best attire" repeats the "best apparel" that Marullus complained of at the start, but the offence has changed: at first it was a class offence (workmen dressed above their station), now it is a moral one (Romans dressed to honour the killer of Romans). The same detail has been reinterpreted inside a single scene — a small lesson in how Shakespeare makes images grow.

**ICSE keywords:** 'anaphora', 'rhetorical question', 'climax', 'synecdoche', 'fickleness', 'ingratitude', 'legitimacy', 'civil war'.

**Exam application:** To prove the crowd's fickleness from the text, write: 'Marullus's repeated "And do you now…" hammers home the contrast between the crowd that once waited all day for "great Pompey" and the crowd that now scatters flowers before the man "That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood"; Shakespeare uses the anaphora to make their change of loyalty sound both mindless and shameful.'

### Passage 7 — The prayer against the plague

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Marullus:** Be gone! / Run to your houses, fall upon your knees, / Pray to the gods to intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude.

**Simple English:** Go away! Run home, fall on your knees, and pray to the gods to hold back the plague that is bound to fall upon this ingratitude of yours.

**Difficult words:** *Be gone* — go away at once. *Fall upon your knees* — kneel in prayer. *Intermit* — to suspend, hold off, interrupt for a time. *Plague* — pestilence, a deadly epidemic; also, more generally, any divine punishment. *Needs must* — must necessarily, cannot help but. *Light on* — fall upon, land on. *Ingratitude* — the failure to be grateful; here, forgetting Pompey.

**Literal meaning:** Marullus dismisses the crowd and tells them to pray that the gods postpone the punishment their ingratitude is certain to bring.

**Subtext:** Marullus does not say 'pray that there be no plague'; he says pray that the gods *intermit* it — delay it. In his mind the punishment is already deserved and already on its way. The crowd is being told that it has committed not a social error but a sin.

**Hidden meaning:** Ingratitude is the sin the speech names, and it is the sin the whole play will turn on. Caesar will be killed by men who owe him their lives and offices; Antony will accuse the conspirators of exactly this; the crowd will be ungrateful to Brutus within minutes of applauding him. Marullus thinks he is describing the crowd's fault. He is describing Rome's.

**Character psychology:** Here Marullus reveals that his politics are religious. He believes the gods take sides in Roman affairs and punish disloyalty with pestilence. This is the same man who, a few lines later, will hesitate to strip Caesar's statues because it is a holy day. He is pious to his core, and that piety makes his contempt for the crowd feel like a priest's rather than a politician's.

**Political meaning:** By calling the crowd's loyalty to Caesar a sin against the gods, Marullus raises the stakes from politics to religion. This is a powerful rhetorical move — it is hard to argue with heaven — but it also exposes the weakness of the republican position: it has no reward to offer the crowd, only guilt and threats. Caesar offers a holiday. The scene lets us feel which argument will win.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Romans did regard plague, famine and prodigies as signs of divine displeasure, to be averted by prayer and ritual. Shakespeare's own London had lived through severe outbreaks of plague in his lifetime — the theatres were closed by plague for long periods in the early 1590s — so an Elizabethan audience would have heard the word with a shudder that we can only partly recover. Whether they connected it to the play's politics is a matter of interpretation, not record.

**Literary devices:** Imperatives in rapid sequence ("Be gone!", then *Run*, *fall*, *Pray*); hyperbole — a plague as the fit punishment for a holiday; personification of the plague as something that will "light on" the crowd like a bird settling; the religious register (*gods*, *knees*, *Pray*) lifts the speech from insult to prophecy. The rhythm is worth noticing: the half-line "Be gone!" breaks the verse, as if Marullus's anger has outrun his sentence.

**Effect:** The speech ends not with a question but with a curse-like prayer, and the crowd, which has been silent throughout, does not answer. The audience is left with a sense of doom hanging over a festive day — the first of many moments in the play where celebration and foreboding are made to share a stage.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the last thing said to the commoners before they leave, and the play's first explicit linking of Rome's political life to divine punishment. That link will be made again by the storm in Scene 3, by the omens of Act 2, and by the civil war that is the play's actual 'plague'.

**Foreshadowing:** The plague Marullus prays against does fall on Rome — but not on the crowd for cheering Caesar. It falls as civil war after Caesar's murder, brought about by men on Marullus's own side. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many readers take this as one of the play's sharpest ironies: the republicans curse Rome with a punishment that their own actions deliver.

**Scene connection:** Flavius immediately echoes and softens the command — "Go, go, good countrymen" — but adds his own grotesque penance, that the poor should weep into the Tiber until the river rises to its highest banks. Marullus asks for prayer; Flavius asks for tears. Together they give the crowd nothing to do but feel guilty, which is why the crowd simply vanishes.

**ICSE keywords:** 'ingratitude', 'divine punishment', 'hyperbole', 'imperative', 'religious imagery', 'irony', 'foreboding'.

**Exam application:** If asked what Marullus accuses the commoners of, write: 'He accuses them of "ingratitude" so grave that only prayer can "intermit the plague" the gods must send for it; by turning a political complaint into a religious one, Marullus makes the crowd's new love for Caesar sound like a sin against Rome and heaven alike.'

### Passage 8 — The silent exit and the metal of the crowd

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Flavius:** Go, go, good countrymen, and for this fault / Assemble all the poor men of your sort, / Draw them to Tiber banks, and weep your tears / Into the channel, till the lowest stream / Do kiss the most exalted shores of all.

> **Flavius:** See whe'er their basest mettle be not moved. / They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness.

**Simple English:** Go, go, my good countrymen, and to make up for this fault, gather all the poor men of your class, lead them to the banks of the Tiber, and weep your tears into the river until the water at its lowest point rises to touch the highest banks of all. … Look — see whether even their low, worthless natures have not been moved. They slip away in silence, struck dumb by their guilt.

**Difficult words:** *Countrymen* — fellow Romans. *Fault* — offence, wrongdoing. *Your sort* — your class, your kind. *Channel* — the river bed. *Exalted* — raised high. *Whe'er* — a contraction of 'whether'. *Basest* — lowest, most worthless; also 'base' as in base metal, cheap metal such as lead as opposed to gold. *Mettle* — spirit, temperament, natural quality (with a pun on *metal*). *Moved* — stirred, affected emotionally. *Tongue-tied* — unable to speak. *Guiltiness* — sense of guilt.

**Literal meaning:** Flavius dismisses the crowd with an order to perform an exaggerated act of penance; after they leave he remarks to Marullus that even such low people have been affected, and that they have gone off silently, ashamed.

**Subtext:** "good countrymen" sounds kinder than anything Marullus said, but what follows is not kindness. The order to weep until a river floods is impossible, and its impossibility is the point: Flavius is telling the poor that no amount of grief can make up for what they have done. The 'kind' tribune is as contemptuous as the angry one; he is simply quieter about it.

**Hidden meaning:** "basest mettle" is a small masterpiece of condescension. *Mettle* means spirit or nature; *base metal* is cheap metal. Flavius says, in effect, 'look — even lead can be stirred.' But a second meaning runs under the first. In the next scene Brutus will remark that Casca "was quick mettle when he went to school", and the play will repeatedly test what men are made of. Flavius thinks the crowd is base metal and the republicans are gold. Act 3 will put both to the fire.

**Character psychology:** Flavius is a reader of crowds, or thinks he is. He watches the commoners leave and *interprets* their silence as guilt. Notice how quickly he moves from observation to conclusion, and how satisfied the conclusion makes him. He is a man who sees what he expects to see — a trait that, in Brutus, will be fatal.

**Political meaning:** The tribunes have won a round, and the way they have won it is instructive: not by argument but by shame, delivered from above. The crowd has been *silenced*, not *persuaded*. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the text does not tell us why the commoners leave. Flavius says guilt; a director could as easily stage boredom, fear or sullen resentment. Shakespeare leaves the crowd's inner state unreadable, which is exactly how the great men of the play will experience it — as a surface they interpret, never a mind they know.

**Historical context:** The Tiber was Rome's river and the scene of real public mourning and public spectacle; its flooding was a known danger. Flavius's image of tears raising the river belongs to the same family of hyperbole as Marullus's river trembling at a shout — both tribunes picture the crowd's emotion as a force of nature acting on the city's water.

**Literary devices:** Hyperbole (tears that flood a river); antithesis ("the lowest stream" against "the most exalted shores"); personification (the stream will *kiss* the shores); pun ("basest mettle" — spirit and base metal); irony in the gentle "good countrymen" that introduces a humiliating command; alliteration in "tongue-tied". The silent exit of the commoners is itself a dramatic device — a stage picture of a crowd unmade.

**Effect:** The audience sees the holiday crowd drain from the stage without a word, and the sudden emptiness is startling after the noise of the puns and the speech. We are left with two officials congratulating themselves in a cleared street — a picture of a small, hollow victory.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the first demonstration in the play that the crowd can be moved by words, and the first demonstration that moving it is not the same as winning it. Both facts govern Act 3. Also, "tongue-tied" introduces the motif of silencing: in the next scene Casca will report that the tribunes themselves "are put to silence". Those who silence are silenced.

**Foreshadowing:** The emotional, movable crowd that leaves "tongue-tied" here will be moved again by Brutus's reason and then by Antony's tears — and after Antony it will not be silent at all. The hyperbole of a river of tears also foreshadows Antony's use of weeping as a political instrument in the Forum.

**Scene connection:** This is the hinge of the scene. Before it, the tribunes dealt with the crowd; after it, they deal with Caesar's images. With the people gone, their republicanism turns from scolding to action, and the dialogue drops from public rhetoric to a private plan.

**ICSE keywords:** 'hyperbole', 'pun', 'antithesis', 'condescension', 'silencing', 'mob psychology', 'public opinion', 'contempt'.

**Exam application:** Asked how the commoners react to the tribunes' rebuke, write: 'They do not reply at all; Flavius observes that they "vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness", and his pun on their "basest mettle" shows that even in victory the tribunes despise the people they have just shamed — a contempt the conspirators will share, to their cost.'

### Passage 9 — Disrobing the images and the feast of Lupercal

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Flavius:** Go you down that way towards the Capitol. / This way will I. Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies.

> **Marullus:** May we do so? / You know it is the feast of Lupercal.

> **Flavius:** It is no matter. Let no images / Be hung with Caesar's trophies.

**Simple English:** You go down that way towards the Capitol; I will go this way. Strip the statues if you find them hung with decorations. — Are we allowed to do that? You know it is the festival of Lupercal. — It does not matter. Let no statue be hung with Caesar's tokens of victory.

**Difficult words:** *The Capitol* — the Capitoline hill, the religious and political heart of Rome, with the Temple of Jupiter. *Disrobe* — strip of clothing; here, strip of decorations. *Images* — statues. *Decked* — adorned, decorated. *Ceremonies* — ceremonial ornaments (the scarves and garlands Casca mentions in Scene 2). *The feast of Lupercal* — the Lupercalia, the festival of 15 February. *It is no matter* — it does not matter. *Trophies* — tokens or emblems of victory.

**Literal meaning:** Flavius divides the city between them and orders the removal of any decorations found on Caesar's statues. Marullus asks whether this is permitted on a holy day. Flavius dismisses the objection.

**Subtext:** Marullus's question is not cowardice; it is conscience. He is asking whether a political act is lawful on a religious festival. Flavius's three-word answer — "It is no matter" — sweeps religion aside for politics. The exchange quietly separates the two tribunes: one still feels the old pieties, the other has decided that the threat of Caesar outranks them.

**Hidden meaning:** The verb *disrobe* treats the statues as bodies wearing clothes. Earlier the tribunes wanted the workmen *re*-dressed in the signs of their trade; now they want Caesar's images *un*-dressed of the signs of his glory. Both are attempts to control appearance, and both fail — the crowd goes on cheering, and the statues will be dressed again. Rome, the scene suggests, is a city governed by what things look like.

**Character psychology:** Flavius is decisive to the point of recklessness. Marullus is the more careful, the more religious, and — perhaps — the more frightened. Yet Shakespeare does not let us settle who is wiser. Marullus's scruple would have saved them both; Flavius's boldness is the only act of resistance anyone in Rome manages before the Ides. The scene lets both qualities stand.

**Political meaning:** Stripping a dictator's statues is a public, visible challenge to his personality cult, carried out by the officers whose job is to defend the people's liberty. It is the Republic's constitution, in the persons of two tribunes, trying to reassert itself over a man who has outgrown it. That Caesar answers by silencing them (reported in Scene 2) tells the audience exactly how much of the Republic is left.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the Lupercalia, held on 15 February, was an ancient purification and fertility festival in which priests called Luperci ran through the city striking bystanders, especially women, with strips of goat-hide; Antony ran in it as a Lupercus in 44 BC, which is the race Caesar refers to in Scene 2. The real tribunes' removal of a diadem in January 44 BC, and Shakespeare's relocation of it to this day, is explained in the history box above. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** Marullus's religious hesitation is not recorded in the sources Shakespeare used; it appears to be his own dramatic touch, added to distinguish the two men.

**Literary devices:** Personification of the statues (they can be "Disrobe[d]" and "decked"); a stichomythia-like exchange (stichomythia is rapid, line-for-line dialogue) — short question, short dismissal — marking the shift from oratory to plotting; the brisk, practical imperatives ("Go you down", "Disrobe", "Let no images") show verse at its plainest; the balance of "that way" and "This way" stages the division of the city on the spot.

**Effect:** After the great public speech, this hushed, hurried planning feels conspiratorial, and the audience senses danger for the first time — not from Caesar yet, but for these two men. Marullus's question plants a doubt that Flavius's answer does not remove.

**Dramatic significance:** This exchange is the play's first act of resistance to Caesar and its first conspiracy, two men agreeing in a street to deface a dictator's images. It is also the first mention of the Lupercal, which binds this scene to the next: the festival the tribunes nearly respect is the one Caesar is about to use as a stage.

**Foreshadowing:** The consequence arrives in Scene 2, when Casca reports that "Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence." Beyond that, the plan to strip an *image* of Caesar foreshadows the conspirators' plan to remove the *man*, and both acts share a flaw: they attack what Caesar looks like and leave his power — the crowd's love — untouched. The mob's killing of Cinna the poet for his name in Act 3 Scene 3 is the darkest version of the same confusion between sign and substance.

**Scene connection:** The Capitol, named here as a direction, will be the destination of Caesar's final walk in Act 3 Scene 1 — Shakespeare relocates the assassination there from its historical site in Pompey's theatre complex. The "ceremonies" return as the ceremony Caesar insists on in Scene 2 — "Set on and leave no ceremony out" — so that within minutes of the tribunes trying to strip ceremony from Caesar's statues, Caesar himself demands it in full.

**ICSE keywords:** 'personality cult', 'symbol', 'resistance', 'religious scruple', 'personification', 'appearance versus reality', 'consequence'.

**Exam application:** When explaining why the tribunes are punished in Scene 2, write: 'Flavius ordered Marullus to "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies", a direct attack on the public honours that made Caesar seem more than a citizen; Casca's report that they "are put to silence" proves that Caesar would not tolerate even this symbolic defiance.'

### Passage 10 — The falcon that must not soar

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Flavius:** I'll about / And drive away the vulgar from the streets; / So do you too, where you perceive them thick. / These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch, / Who else would soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness.

**Simple English:** I will go about the city and clear the common people from the streets; you do the same wherever you see them crowding. If we pluck these new feathers from Caesar's wing, he will fly only at an ordinary height — whereas otherwise he would soar so high that men could not see him, and would keep all of us in slavish fear.

**Difficult words:** *I'll about* — I will go about, make my rounds. *The vulgar* — the common people (from Latin *vulgus*, the crowd; not yet 'rude' in the modern sense, but already contemptuous). *Perceive them thick* — see them gathered densely. *Growing feathers* — new feathers, the plumage a young bird grows as it matures; here, Caesar's newly acquired honours and popular support. *Plucked* — pulled out. *Pitch* — in falconry, the height to which a hawk or falcon climbs before it swoops on its prey. *Ordinary* — normal, usual. *Soar* — fly high. *Above the view of men* — out of men's sight, beyond their power to watch or check. *Servile* — slave-like. *Fearfulness* — the condition of being afraid.

**Literal meaning:** Flavius plans to clear the streets and explains the purpose of stripping the statues: by removing Caesar's public honours now, the tribunes will keep him at a normal level, instead of letting him rise beyond control and reduce all Romans to frightened slaves.

**Subtext:** This is the real reason for everything in the scene. The anger about aprons, the sneering at the Cobbler, the shaming over Pompey — all of it was the surface of a fear Flavius only now states plainly: Caesar is becoming something other than a citizen, and there is very little time left to stop him.

**Hidden meaning:** The metaphor carries a precise political theory. A falcon's *pitch* is the height from which it strikes; a bird that climbs beyond sight is a bird that can strike anywhere, unseen. 'Above the view of men' means more than 'very high' — it means *beyond accountability*, where the Republic's machinery of watching and checking cannot reach. And "feathers" are the right image for honours and applause: they are not the bird, but they are what lets the bird fly. The crowd's love is Caesar's plumage. The tribunes have understood that; what they have not understood is that you cannot pluck a bird by scolding its admirers.

**Character psychology:** The blunt enforcer of the opening lines turns out to be the scene's most lucid thinker. Flavius speaks here without rage, in a sustained image, and the phrase "us all" shows that his fear is for the community, not for himself. He is also, we notice, a man who believes small actions can alter great outcomes — a hopefulness that the next scene punishes.

**Political meaning:** This is the republican case in its purest form, before any personal grievance has coloured it: one man must not rise above the level of other men, because a Rome with one man above it is a Rome of slaves. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the Roman Republic was founded, in its own tradition, on the expulsion of a king in 509 BC, and Romans retained a deep hatred of the word *rex*. Flavius never uses the word 'king', but "servile fearfulness" describes exactly what Romans believed kingship meant.

**Historical context:** Falconry was a sport of the Elizabethan gentry, and its vocabulary — pitch, stoop, haggard, mew — was common currency in Shakespeare's language; his audience would have grasped the image instantly. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the image is Shakespeare's, not Plutarch's; the sources give the tribunes' action but not this explanation of it. Shakespeare supplies the political reasoning so that the audience enters Scene 2 already equipped with the republican fear.

**Literary devices:** Extended metaphor (Caesar as a bird of prey, sustained over four lines: feathers, wing, fly, pitch, soar); antithesis ("an ordinary pitch" against soaring "above the view of men"); alliteration in *fly* / *feathers* and in "servile fearfulness"; the inclusive pronoun "us all" widening the stakes from two tribunes to all Rome; the scene's final word, "fearfulness", left ringing — an ending on the emotion that drives the whole play.

**Effect:** The scene closes not with a joke or a shout but with a thoughtful, frightening picture. The audience, having laughed at puns and winced at a tirade, is now made to think. We leave the empty street carrying an image of Caesar as a hawk, and that image colours everything we see of him in the next scene.

**Dramatic significance:** These are the scene's last words and the play's first explicit statement of its central political anxiety: that Caesar will rise beyond the reach of ordinary men. Everything the conspirators later say — Cassius's complaint that Caesar does "bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus" while "we petty men / Walk under his huge legs", Brutus's reasoning in his orchard that Caesar must be stopped before he grows — is a variation on Flavius's falcon. The scene ends by handing the audience the lens through which to watch Caesar's entrance.

**Foreshadowing:** The attempt to pluck Caesar's feathers fails at once; the tribunes are silenced and Caesar is offered a crown in the same afternoon. But the logic — act before he soars — survives them and becomes the conspiracy. In that sense the falcon speech foreshadows the Ides of March: the conspirators will pluck not feathers but life, and will discover that Caesar's spirit, in Brutus's own later admission, still walks abroad. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the deepest irony is that the plucking does not keep Rome at an 'ordinary pitch'; it produces Antony, Octavian and an empire.

**Scene connection:** The "vulgar" that Flavius drives from the streets are the crowd we will hear shouting for Caesar in Scene 2; his clearing of the streets fails before it begins. The upward imagery — soaring, above the view of men — is answered in Scene 2 by Cassius's Colossus, which also makes Caesar vast and the rest of Rome small. And the tribunes' plan links directly to Casca's report of their silencing, the first proof that Caesar's wings are already too strong to pluck.

**ICSE keywords:** 'extended metaphor', 'falconry imagery', 'republicanism', 'tyranny', 'ambition', 'liberty versus servitude', 'political fear', 'foreshadowing'.

**Exam application:** When asked why the conspirators fear Caesar, or whether their fear was shared, write: 'The fear is first voiced not by Cassius but by the tribune Flavius, who hopes that "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch"; the falcon image shows that even before the conspiracy, republican officials saw Caesar as a bird of prey rising "above the view of men", able to keep Rome "in servile fearfulness".'

### How the ten passages fit together

Read in order, the ten passages trace one movement. The scene begins with authority shouting at the people (Passages 1–2), is interrupted by the people answering back with wit (Passage 3), turns on a single plain sentence naming Caesar (Passage 4), rises to rhetoric and curse (Passages 5–7), empties the stage in silence (Passage 8), and ends with two men agreeing on a small act of resistance and the large fear behind it (Passages 9–10). If you can tell that story in your own words — command, wit, revelation, rhetoric, silence, resistance, fear — you can reconstruct the whole scene from memory, and you can see why Shakespeare called it the beginning of a tragedy rather than a comedy.

## End-of-Scene Revision Sheet

::: revision ONE-MINUTE REVISION — Act 1 Scene 1
**Five must-remember events.** (1) The tribunes Flavius and Marullus find tradesmen in holiday clothes on the street and order them home. (2) The Cobbler evades their questions with a chain of puns about soles, mending, the awl and being a surgeon to old shoes. (3) The Cobbler admits the crowd is out to see Caesar and rejoice in his triumph. (4) Marullus denounces the crowd for forgetting Pompey and cheering the man who triumphs over "Pompey's blood"; the commoners leave in silence. (5) Flavius and Marullus split up to strip Caesar's statues of their decorations, despite Marullus's worry that it is the feast of Lupercal.

**Five keywords.** Fickleness; ingratitude; republicanism; public opinion; hierarchy (class).

**Three quotations.** "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!" (Marullus to the crowd). "And do you now strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood?" (Marullus — the proof of fickleness). "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch" (Flavius — the republican fear).

**Three character insights.** The Cobbler is witty, self-interested and never quite insolent — proof that the common people are not fools. Marullus is the orator and the pious one; he moves the crowd but hesitates on a holy day. Flavius is the man of action and the clearest political thinker; his decisiveness gets both tribunes silenced.

**Three themes.** The fickleness of public opinion (Pompey → Caesar). The fear of one man rising above the Republic. Class conflict between officials and commoners, marked by verse against prose.

**Two literary devices.** Pun (soles/souls, awl/all, withal/with awl, recover/re-cover). Extended metaphor (Caesar as a falcon whose feathers must be plucked) — with anaphora ("And do you now…") and rhetorical question as the next two to name.

**Two historical points.** Caesar's Spanish triumph (October 45 BC) celebrated victory over Pompey's sons at Munda and was resented because it was a victory over Romans. The historical tribunes removed a diadem from Caesar's statue and were deposed; Shakespeare moves the episode to the day of the triumph and the Lupercal.

**Three connections.** Casca in Scene 2: the tribunes "are put to silence" for pulling scarves off Caesar's images. Cassius in Scene 2: the Colossus speech is Flavius's falcon fear restated with envy. Act 3 Scene 2: the crowd that swung from Pompey to Caesar swings from Brutus to Antony.

**One central idea.** The crowd's love makes a man great and can be moved by anyone with words — so whoever controls the crowd controls Rome, and the republicans who despise the crowd have already lost it.
