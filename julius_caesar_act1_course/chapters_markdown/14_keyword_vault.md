# The ICSE Keyword Vault for Act 1

*What this chapter does: It gives you the words. Not a glossary of hard Shakespearean vocabulary (that is the business of the chapter 'Difficult Shakespearean English: The Act 1 Vocabulary System'), but two armouries of terms that earn marks. Type A holds Shakespeare's own words and phrases from Act 1, each one explained as a small piece of argument you can quote. Type B holds the analytical vocabulary of politics, rhetoric, drama and Roman history that lets you name precisely what the play is doing. Every entry follows one fixed format, so you can revise it like a deck of cards, and the chapter ends with a conversion table that turns the weak words students reach for into the strong words examiners reward.*

## How to Use the Vault

Students who struggle with Shakespeare usually believe their problem is understanding. Very often their real problem is expression. They have seen what Cassius is doing to Brutus; they simply cannot say it in fewer than forty words, so they write 'Cassius tricks Brutus into joining him by saying nice things about him', and the examiner, who was waiting for the word 'insinuates' or 'flatters' or 'manipulates', gives the point half its value. The idea was there. The word was not.

This chapter fixes that. It is built in two parts.

**Type A** entries are Shakespeare's own words. When you quote "lean and hungry" or "servile fearfulness" you are not decorating your answer; you are borrowing the play's own analysis, because the characters of this play are themselves analysts of one another, and their chosen words carry judgements. To quote the word is to quote the judgement.

**Type B** entries are the analytical terms: 'republicanism', 'manipulation', 'dramatic irony', 'tribune'. They are the words you supply, from outside the text, to classify what the text is doing. The danger with Type B words is that they can be fired off without aim. So each entry tells you exactly where in Act 1 the term genuinely applies, and several carry a caution where the term is anachronistic or contested.

Every entry uses the same eight labels. **Keyword** names it. **Meaning** explains it in plain English, including the Elizabethan sense where that differs from ours. **Where it applies** fixes it to a moment in Act 1. **Textual evidence** gives you verified words to quote. **Character/Theme** tells you which answer the word belongs in. **How to use it in an ICSE answer** gives you one sample sentence, written at the standard an examiner rewards. **Common weak substitute** names the word students usually write instead, and **Better word** gives you the upgrade. Learn the entry; practise the sentence; the upgrade will become your habit.

::: insight Why precise vocabulary is itself analysis
LITERARY INTERPRETATION and EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE together. There is a common belief that analysis is a mental act and vocabulary is merely its clothing: first you understand, then you find words. In literary study this is false, and knowing why will change how you write. Consider the difference between 'Caesar is proud' and 'Caesar is self-mythologising'. The first is a feeling about him. The second makes a claim with moving parts: that Caesar has constructed a public image, that he speaks of that image rather than of himself, and that the image is doing political work. The moment you choose the second word you are obliged to look for evidence of all three parts, and Act 1 supplies it, because Caesar refers to himself in the third person and tells Antony "for always I am Caesar", as though 'Caesar' were an office and not a man. The precise word forced you to see something the vague word let you ignore. This is why examiners treat vocabulary as evidence of thought: a student who writes 'the fickle plebeians' has demonstrated that she has noticed the crowd's reversal from Pompey to Caesar and classified the people correctly; a student who writes 'the people were bad' has demonstrated neither. Strong vocabulary is not a reward for analysis finished elsewhere. It is the instrument with which the analysis is performed, and the shortest possible proof that it has been.
:::

::: icse Where these words earn their marks
EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE. Be clear about the examination position. Act 1 is the Class IX portion of the ICSE Julius Caesar syllabus, examined in school through extract-based ('reference to the context') questions. The Class X board paper sets its drama extracts from Acts III, IV and V. So you will not be handed an Act 1 extract in the board examination. But the Class X paper relies on Act 1 at every turn: it asks what earlier events a line refers to, why a character behaves as he does, what has motivated the conspiracy, and it rewards answers that trace a character's trait back to its origin. The words in this vault are therefore double-purpose. They answer Act 1 questions in Class IX, and they supply the explanatory vocabulary ('republicanism', 'manipulation', 'the fickle plebeians', 'portentous') that Act 3, 4 and 5 answers need. No question is guaranteed; the vocabulary is useful whatever is set.
:::

## Type A, Group One: The Street. Class, Crowd and Contempt (Scene 1)

The first scene teaches the play's political vocabulary before it introduces a single patrician. The tribunes' words for the commoners, and their words for Caesar's honours, are the seedbed of everything later.

**Keyword:** mechanical

**Meaning:** In Elizabethan English a 'mechanical' was a manual worker, a tradesman who worked with his hands. It is a class label, not an insult in itself, but Flavius turns it into one by using it to say the men have no right to be where they are.

**Where it applies:** The opening speech of the play, as Flavius drives the holidaying tradesmen off the street.

**Textual evidence:** "Being mechanical, you ought not walk / Upon a labouring day without the sign / Of your profession"

**Character/Theme:** Flavius; class division; the tribunes' contempt for the people they are supposed to protect.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Flavius's first word of classification, "mechanical", reduces the commoners to their trade and their tools; the tribune of the plebs opens the play by telling the plebs they are out of place.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'workers', 'poor people'

**Better word:** 'the mechanicals', 'the artisan class', 'tradesmen'

**Keyword:** idle creatures

**Meaning:** 'Idle' means both unemployed and worthless; 'creatures' denies them full humanity. The phrase is the very first thing spoken in the play.

**Where it applies:** Flavius's first line.

**Textual evidence:** "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!"

**Character/Theme:** Flavius; the crowd as a political object; the gap between the tribunes' office and their attitude.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The play begins with an insult: "idle creatures" is not addressed to Caesar's enemies but to Rome's own working men, and it tells us that the republican cause is defended, from its first line, by men who despise the republic's citizens.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'lazy people'

**Better word:** 'dehumanising address', 'contempt for the commons'

**Keyword:** vulgar

**Meaning:** The 'vulgar' were simply the common people (Latin vulgus, the crowd). The modern sense of coarse or indecent is a later narrowing, though the contempt is already audible in Flavius's mouth.

**Where it applies:** Flavius's closing instructions to Marullus.

**Textual evidence:** "I'll about / And drive away the vulgar from the streets"

**Character/Theme:** Flavius; the plebeians; public opinion as a thing to be herded.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Flavius does not argue with "the vulgar"; he drives them, as one drives cattle, and the verb exposes how little the tribunes believe in the people's judgement they claim to defend.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'the people', 'the public'

**Better word:** 'the populace', 'the commons', 'the plebeians'

**Keyword:** tributaries

**Meaning:** Conquered peoples or rulers who pay tribute to Rome. In a proper triumph, foreign captives walked before the general's chariot. Marullus's point is that Caesar's victory was over Romans, so there are no tributaries to show.

**Where it applies:** Marullus's great rebuke, the moment the Cobbler mentions Caesar's triumph.

**Textual evidence:** "What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?"

**Character/Theme:** Marullus; the illegitimacy of a civil-war triumph; Pompey's memory.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Marullus's rhetorical question about "tributaries" is a legal objection disguised as grief: a triumph without foreign captives is a triumph over fellow Romans, and therefore no triumph at all.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'prisoners', 'enemies'

**Better word:** 'tributaries', 'captives in chains', 'conquered foreigners'

**Keyword:** captive bonds

**Meaning:** The chains of the prisoners paraded in a triumph. The phrase carries the whole Roman theatre of victory in two words.

**Where it applies:** The same rhetorical question.

**Textual evidence:** "To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels"

**Character/Theme:** Marullus; triumph; the ceremony of power.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The chains that should "grace" Caesar's wheels are absent, and the absence is Marullus's whole case: Caesar has borrowed the ceremony of conquest for a victory that was really a bloodletting among Romans.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'chains'

**Better word:** 'captive bonds', 'the spectacle of conquest'

**Keyword:** replication

**Meaning:** Here, an echo; the river's banks threw the crowd's shout back. (The word is unrelated to its modern scientific sense.)

**Where it applies:** Marullus's memory of the people cheering Pompey.

**Textual evidence:** "That Tiber trembled underneath her banks / To hear the replication of your sounds / Made in her concave shores?"

**Character/Theme:** Marullus; the fickle crowd; memory versus the present.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Marullus recalls a Rome whose shout for Pompey made the Tiber tremble with its "replication"; the echo is the point, because the same voices now echo for Pompey's conqueror.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'noise', 'cheering'

**Better word:** 'the echoing acclamation', 'the crowd's replicated shout'

**Keyword:** Pompey's blood

**Meaning:** Literally the blood of Pompey's family, since the triumph celebrated victory over Pompey's sons; more broadly, the Roman blood shed in civil war.

**Where it applies:** The climax of Marullus's rebuke.

**Textual evidence:** "And do you now strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood?"

**Character/Theme:** Marullus; civil war; ingratitude; the crowd's reversal.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The flowers strewn for Caesar fall, in Marullus's phrase, on "Pompey's blood", and the collision of the two images turns a festival into a desecration.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'Pompey's defeat'

**Better word:** 'the blood of fellow Romans', 'a triumph over Roman dead'

**Keyword:** images, ceremonies, trophies

**Meaning:** The 'images' are Caesar's statues; 'ceremonies' here are the decorations (scarves, diadems) hung upon them; 'trophies' are the tokens of victory. Together they are the visible furniture of Caesar's rising status.

**Where it applies:** The tribunes' decision to strip the statues at the end of Scene 1; recalled by Casca in Scene 2.

**Textual evidence:** "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies." and "Let no images / Be hung with Caesar's trophies."

**Character/Theme:** Flavius and Marullus; political symbolism; the war of signs that precedes the war of daggers.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The tribunes' first act of resistance is symbolic: by disrobing the "images" they attack not Caesar's person but his public meaning, which is exactly where his power is being manufactured.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'statues with decorations', 'things'

**Better word:** 'Caesar's images', 'the emblems of his honour', 'the iconography of power'

**Keyword:** ordinary pitch

**Meaning:** From falconry. A hawk's 'pitch' is the height it climbs before stooping on its prey; an 'ordinary' pitch is a normal, unexceptional height. Flavius imagines plucking Caesar's feathers so he can fly no higher than other men.

**Where it applies:** Flavius's closing couplets in Scene 1.

**Textual evidence:** "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch"

**Character/Theme:** Flavius; ambition; the republican ideal of equality among the nobility.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Flavius's falconry image states the republican creed in miniature: no Roman should "soar above the view of men", and the honours stripped from the statues are the "growing feathers" that would carry Caesar out of reach.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'normal level'

**Better word:** 'an "ordinary pitch"', 'the level of his equals'

**Keyword:** servile fearfulness

**Meaning:** The fear of slaves. 'Servile' comes from the Latin for slave; the phrase names the condition the tribunes dread, a Rome in which free men live in the fear proper to the unfree.

**Where it applies:** The final line of Scene 1.

**Textual evidence:** "Who else would soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness."

**Character/Theme:** Flavius; liberty versus bondage; the republican fear of a king.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Scene 1 ends on "servile fearfulness", the phrase that Cassius will spend two scenes expanding, for his "bondage" and "bondman" are the same fear given a sharper edge.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'being scared of Caesar'

**Better word:** 'servile fearfulness', 'the subjection of free men'

## Type A, Group Two: Ceremony, Omen and the Body of Caesar (Scene 2)

These words belong to Caesar himself, or to the way others describe his body. Keep them separate from Cassius's hostile vocabulary: these are the words that show Caesar's power and vulnerability side by side.

**Keyword:** sterile curse

**Meaning:** Barrenness, imagined as a curse that the Lupercal runners' touch can remove. The phrase makes Caesar's childlessness a public and ritual matter.

**Where it applies:** Caesar's first full speech, instructing Antony to touch Calpurnia during the race.

**Textual evidence:** "The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; superstition; the private anxiety of a public man; the question of an heir.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Before he dismisses the Soothsayer, Caesar has already shown that he believes in ritual, publicly asking Antony to lift Calpurnia's "sterile curse"; his later scepticism is therefore selective, not principled.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'not being able to have children'

**Better word:** 'barrenness', 'the "sterile curse"', 'the want of an heir'

**Keyword:** ides

**Meaning:** HISTORICAL RECORD: in the Roman calendar the Ides fell on the fifteenth of March, May, July and October and the thirteenth of other months. In the play 'the ides of March' is the appointed day, the fixed point towards which everything moves.

**Where it applies:** The Soothsayer's warning and Caesar's dismissal of it.

**Textual evidence:** "Beware the ides of March." and "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass."

**Character/Theme:** The Soothsayer; Caesar; fate; dramatic irony; foreshadowing.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The Soothsayer's "Beware the ides of March" is the play's clock; from this line onward every scene is dated by its distance from a day the audience already knows to be fatal.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'the fifteenth of March', 'the day'

**Better word:** 'the ides', 'the appointed day', 'the fatal date'

**Keyword:** falling sickness

**Meaning:** Epilepsy, in its Elizabethan name. HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch and Suetonius record Caesar's seizures; the diagnosis is debated by modern historians. In the play the term is passed from Brutus (medical) to Cassius (political).

**Where it applies:** Casca's report of Caesar's swoon in the marketplace, and the exchange that follows.

**Textual evidence:** "'Tis very like; he hath the falling sickness." and "No, Caesar hath it not; but you and I / And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar's mortality; Cassius's wit; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus names Caesar's "falling sickness" as a doctor would; Cassius seizes the phrase and turns it into a political diagnosis of Rome, whose citizens have "fallen" into servitude while Caesar stands.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'fits', 'illness'

**Better word:** 'the falling sickness', 'his infirmity', 'the body's betrayal of the god'

**Keyword:** this ear is deaf

**Meaning:** Caesar's admission that he cannot hear on one side. Scholars note that the deafness has no basis in the ancient sources and appears to be Shakespeare's addition.

**Where it applies:** The end of Caesar's assessment of Cassius, as he turns to Antony.

**Textual evidence:** "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; the gap between the public colossus and the private body; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Within one speech Caesar declares "always I am Caesar" and confesses "this ear is deaf"; Shakespeare places the invented infirmity beside the boast so that the audience hears the man and the myth in the same breath.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'he can't hear properly'

**Better word:** 'his physical frailty', 'the deaf ear as an emblem of the man who will not listen'

**Keyword:** lean and hungry

**Meaning:** Thin and, by implication, unsatisfied and ambitious. HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch reports Caesar saying he feared pale, thin men rather than fat, long-haired ones; Shakespeare sharpens the remark into a portrait.

**Where it applies:** Caesar's aside to Antony as he returns from the games.

**Textual evidence:** "Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar's political insight; Cassius's envy; dramatic irony.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Caesar reads Cassius with perfect accuracy ("lean and hungry", "thinks too much") and then overrules his own reading, so that the speech demonstrates both his penetration and the self-image that makes the penetration useless.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'thin and jealous'

**Better word:** 'lean and hungry', 'ascetic and resentful', 'the discontented observer'

**Keyword:** well given

**Meaning:** Well disposed, of good inclination. Antony's phrase of reassurance about Cassius.

**Where it applies:** Antony's reply to Caesar's suspicion.

**Textual evidence:** "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given."

**Character/Theme:** Antony's misjudgement; dramatic irony; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Antony's "well given" is the play's first instance of a loyal friend being wrong about a conspirator, and it quietly establishes that Caesar, not Antony, is the better reader of men in Act 1.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'a good man'

**Better word:** 'well disposed', 'of good inclination', 'honourable in appearance'

## Type A, Group Three: Brutus's Inner War (Scene 2)

Brutus's vocabulary is the vocabulary of division: of a self at odds with itself, of eyes that cannot see their own face, of honour weighed against death. Cassius's words for Brutus belong here too, because they are chosen to fit the cracks.

**Keyword:** gamesome

**Meaning:** Fond of sport and amusement; playful. Brutus uses it of what he is not.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's excuse for not watching the Lupercal race.

**Textual evidence:** "I am not gamesome. I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; temperament; the contrast with Antony.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus's "I am not gamesome" is a self-portrait in three words: grave, withdrawn, and already measuring himself against Antony, the man who will later outplay him before the crowd.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'fun-loving', 'not sporty'

**Better word:** 'gamesome', 'convivial', 'light-spirited'

**Keyword:** quick spirit

**Meaning:** 'Quick' means lively, alive, animated. Brutus credits Antony with a vitality he lacks.

**Where it applies:** The same line.

**Textual evidence:** "that quick spirit that is in Antony"

**Character/Theme:** Antony as foil to Brutus; temperament and politics.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'By granting Antony the "quick spirit" he himself lacks, Brutus unknowingly names the quality that will defeat him: the conspirators' failure to take Antony seriously begins in this admiring, dismissive phrase.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'energy', 'liveliness'

**Better word:** 'quick spirit', 'animal vitality', 'liveliness of temper'

**Keyword:** passions of some difference

**Meaning:** Conflicting emotions; feelings at variance with one another. 'Passions' in Shakespeare's English means strong feelings of any kind, not only love.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's apology to Cassius for his coldness.

**Textual evidence:** "Vexèd I am / Of late with passions of some difference, / Conceptions only proper to myself"

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; moral conflict; the private self.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus's "passions of some difference" is deliberately vague, and the vagueness matters: he is a man who has not yet named his own conflict, and Cassius enters precisely through that unnamed space.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'mixed feelings', 'confused'

**Better word:** 'conflicting passions', 'inward division', 'unresolved conflict'

**Keyword:** with himself at war

**Meaning:** Internally divided; Brutus pictures his own mind as a battlefield.

**Where it applies:** The close of the same speech.

**Textual evidence:** "poor Brutus, with himself at war, / Forgets the shows of love to other men."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; moral conflict; the public cost of private struggle.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The military metaphor in "with himself at war" is prophetic: the civil war Brutus will help to start in Rome is first fought, and lost, inside him.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'confused', 'upset'

**Better word:** 'inwardly divided', 'self-divided', 'at war with himself'

**Keyword:** mirror, glass, shadow

**Meaning:** 'Mirror' and 'glass' both mean a looking-glass; 'shadow' here means reflection or image. Cassius offers to be the mirror that shows Brutus his hidden worth.

**Where it applies:** The central movement of Cassius's seduction, from the question about Brutus's face to the offer to be his 'glass'.

**Textual evidence:** "That you have no such mirrors as will turn / Your hidden worthiness into your eye, / That you might see your shadow." and "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius's manipulation; Brutus's self-knowledge; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'When Cassius offers to be Brutus's "glass", he claims the power of a mirror to show the truth while reserving the power of a painter to choose the image; the metaphor is the manipulation.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'Cassius says he will show Brutus who he really is'

**Better word:** 'the mirror conceit', 'Cassius as a distorting glass', 'reflected selfhood'

**Keyword:** honour

**Meaning:** For Brutus, a man's inner worth and public integrity, valued above life. Cassius uses the same word as the announced subject of a speech that is really about envy. (British spelling throughout; the Folger text prints 'honor'.)

**Where it applies:** Brutus's declaration after the first shout, and Cassius's reply.

**Textual evidence:** "For let the gods so speed me as I love / The name of honour more than I fear death." and "Well, honour is the subject of my story."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; Cassius; the word's double life as ideal and as bait.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus loves "the name of honour", and Cassius, hearing the word, instantly makes it "the subject of my story"; the whole seduction is an act of translation in which Cassius's grievance is rendered into Brutus's language.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'being good', 'pride'

**Better word:** 'honour', 'integrity', 'his sense of noble duty'

**Keyword:** general good

**Meaning:** The public welfare, the good of the whole state; the republican test by which Brutus says he will judge any proposal.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's conditional offer to Cassius.

**Textual evidence:** "If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently"

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; civic duty; political idealism.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus's one condition, "the general good", is the hinge of his character: it is the principle that makes him incorruptible by bribery and, for that very reason, corruptible by argument.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'for everyone', 'for Rome'

**Better word:** 'the general good', 'the public weal', 'the welfare of the republic'

**Keyword:** villager

**Meaning:** A country-dweller of no rank; for a Roman patrician, the lowest imaginable station. Brutus says he would rather be one than live as a Roman under tyranny.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's parting words before Caesar returns.

**Textual evidence:** "Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; liberty; the first sign that Cassius has succeeded.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'In preferring to be a "villager" rather than "a son of Rome" under Caesar, Brutus for the first time states a price he will not pay, and Cassius rightly hears it as "fire".'

**Common weak substitute:** 'a nobody', 'a peasant'

**Better word:** 'a villager', 'a man without rank', 'an exile from Roman dignity'

**Keyword:** honourable mettle

**Meaning:** 'Mettle' is temperament or spirit; the word was spelt interchangeably with 'metal', so Cassius's phrase also pictures a noble metal that can be 'wrought', hammered into a new shape.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's soliloquy, once Brutus has left.

**Textual evidence:** "Well, Brutus, thou art noble. Yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius's cynicism; Brutus's vulnerability; the ethics of the conspiracy.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The pun in "honourable mettle may be wrought" turns Brutus's virtue into raw material; Cassius admits in soliloquy that nobility is, to him, a metal to be forged.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'good nature', 'character'

**Better word:** 'honourable mettle', 'noble temper', 'malleable virtue'

**Keyword:** seduced

**Meaning:** Led astray, drawn from one's proper course; in Elizabethan usage not primarily sexual. Cassius uses it of what he intends to do to Brutus.

**Where it applies:** The same soliloquy.

**Textual evidence:** "For who so firm that cannot be seduced?"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; manipulation; the gap between the conspiracy's language of honour and its method.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's own verb for his project is "seduced": the man who has just told Brutus that "honour is the subject of my story" privately describes that story as a seduction.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'convinced', 'tricked'

**Better word:** 'seduced', 'subverted', 'drawn from his disposition'

## Type A, Group Four: Cassius's Arsenal (Scene 2)

Cassius's images are the most quoted in Act 1, and the most misquoted. Learn these exactly.

**Keyword:** Colossus

**Meaning:** The giant bronze statue of Helios at Rhodes, one of the Seven Wonders. HISTORICAL RECORD: the picture of it straddling the harbour is a medieval legend, which Shakespeare's image relies on. Cassius makes Caesar the statue and Romans the ships beneath.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's response to the second shout.

**Textual evidence:** "Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; Caesar's magnitude; envy; the diminishment of free men.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The "Colossus" image does two things at once: it magnifies Caesar to inhuman scale and shrinks the Romans to "petty men", so that the resentment is built into the proportions of the picture.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'a giant', 'very powerful'

**Better word:** 'colossal', 'bestriding', 'a monument that dwarfs its citizens'

**Keyword:** underlings

**Meaning:** Subordinates, inferiors; those who are under another. Cassius's word for what Romans have allowed themselves to become.

**Where it applies:** The most famous lines of the scene.

**Textual evidence:** "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; free will against fate; self-reproach as persuasion.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'By blaming "ourselves, that we are underlings" rather than the stars, Cassius flatters Brutus with responsibility: if the fault is in them, the remedy is in them too.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'servants', 'followers'

**Better word:** 'underlings', 'subordinates', 'men who have consented to inferiority'

**Keyword:** masters of their fates

**Meaning:** In control of their own destiny. The line is Cassius's statement of free will against astrological fate.

**Where it applies:** The same speech.

**Textual evidence:** "Men at some time are masters of their fates."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; fate and free will; the rhetoric of action.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's claim that men are "masters of their fates" is a philosophy offered as a provocation, and its irony is slow: the man who denies the stars will spend Act 1 Scene 3 reading omens.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'in charge of their lives'

**Better word:** 'masters of their fates', 'agents rather than victims', 'self-determining'

**Keyword:** breed of noble bloods

**Meaning:** The stock or lineage of noble families; Cassius laments that Rome has stopped producing great men.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's apostrophe to Rome and the age.

**Textual evidence:** "Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; the degeneration of Rome; ancestry (the old Brutus).

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The lament for "the breed of noble bloods" is aimed at Brutus's lineage, and Cassius completes the aim moments later by naming the ancestor who expelled a king.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'great men', 'brave people'

**Better word:** 'noble bloods', 'the patrician stock', 'the degenerate age'

**Keyword:** several hands

**Meaning:** Different handwritings. Cassius will forge letters in various hands so they seem to come from many citizens.

**Where it applies:** The plan announced in Cassius's soliloquy.

**Textual evidence:** "I will this night / In several hands in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens, / Writings"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; manipulation; the forged voice of the people.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The letters "in several hands" counterfeit public opinion itself: Brutus, who will act only for "the general good", is to be shown a general good that Cassius has written out by hand.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'fake letters'

**Better word:** 'forged letters', 'counterfeit petitions', 'a manufactured public voice'

## Type A, Group Five: Casca's Tongue (Scene 2)

Casca speaks prose in Scene 2 and verse in Scene 3, and his Scene 2 prose is the play's most contemptuous account of the crowd. His words give you the vocabulary of mob psychology and of political silencing.

**Keyword:** rabblement

**Meaning:** A rabble, a disorderly mob; the suffix makes the contempt heavier. It is Casca's word for the crowd at the crown-offering.

**Where it applies:** Casca's narration of the third refusal.

**Textual evidence:** "the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps"

**Character/Theme:** Casca; mob psychology; the crowd as spectacle.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's "rabblement", with its "chopped hands" and "sweaty nightcaps", reduces the Roman people to a smell and a noise; his report is evidence about the crowd but also about the patrician who watched it.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'the crowd', 'the mob'

**Better word:** 'the rabblement', 'the unruly populace', 'the hooting crowd'

**Keyword:** tag-rag people

**Meaning:** The ragged, lowest sort; riff-raff. Casca compares their behaviour to a theatre audience applauding or hissing actors.

**Where it applies:** Casca's explanation of why Caesar fell.

**Textual evidence:** "If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre, I am no true man."

**Character/Theme:** Casca; fickleness; politics as performance.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'In likening the "tag-rag people" to playgoers, Casca exposes the crown-offering as theatre, with Caesar as the actor whose refusals were played for applause; the image makes the crowd fickle and the ruler a performer in one stroke.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'ordinary people', 'poor people'

**Better word:** 'the tag-rag people', 'the fickle audience', 'the common herd'

**Keyword:** put to silence

**Meaning:** Silenced; deprived of their office and voice. HISTORICAL RECORD: the tribunes were deposed from the tribunate. Shakespeare's phrase leaves the method chillingly unspecified.

**Where it applies:** Casca's last item of news.

**Textual evidence:** "Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar's power; tyranny; the fate of dissent.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The tribunes who opened the play are "put to silence" in a subordinate clause of gossip; the casualness is the point, since it shows how routinely dissent is now erased in Caesar's Rome.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'punished', 'removed', 'killed'

**Better word:** 'silenced', 'stripped of office', 'suppressed'

**Keyword:** Greek to me

**Meaning:** Incomprehensible. Casca did not understand Cicero's Greek, or pretends not to.

**Where it applies:** Casca's reply when Cassius asks what Cicero said.

**Textual evidence:** "But for mine own part, it was Greek to me."

**Character/Theme:** Casca's affected bluntness; Cicero's exclusion; knowledge as a closed circle.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's "it was Greek to me" is a pose of ignorance from a man Brutus remembers as "quick mettle"; the phrase marks the gap between what the educated elite say to one another and what they let the people hear.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'he didn't understand'

**Better word:** 'affected incomprehension', 'a performed bluntness'

**Keyword:** quick mettle, tardy form

**Meaning:** 'Quick mettle' is a lively spirit; 'tardy form' is a slow, sluggish manner. Brutus and Cassius use the two phrases to distinguish the real Casca from the one he performs.

**Where it applies:** The exchange immediately after Casca leaves.

**Textual evidence:** "He was quick mettle when he went to school." and "However he puts on this tardy form. / This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit"

**Character/Theme:** Casca; appearance versus reality; the conspirators' assessment of one another.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's "tardy form" is a theatrical word, something "put on"; Casca's rudeness is a costume, and Cassius values it because a man who seems dull is a man who is not watched.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'rude', 'slow'

**Better word:** 'an affected sluggishness', 'his "tardy form"', 'a mask of bluntness'

## Type A, Group Six: The Storm, Bondage and the Beasts (Scene 3)

Scene 3 changes the register. The words become those of omen, slavery and animal nature, and Cassius's images turn from statues to wolves.

**Keyword:** prodigies

**Meaning:** Extraordinary, unnatural happenings taken as signs. Casca's list (the burning hand, the lion, the women, the owl at noon) is a catalogue of prodigies.

**Where it applies:** Casca's report to Cicero.

**Textual evidence:** "When these prodigies / Do so conjointly meet, let not men say / 'These are their reasons, they are natural'"

**Character/Theme:** Casca; the supernatural; the correspondence between nature and state.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca insists the "prodigies" are not "natural", and that insistence is a political act: to call the storm a sign is to say that something in Rome has broken the order of things.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'strange things', 'weird events'

**Better word:** 'prodigies', 'unnatural signs', 'portents'

**Keyword:** portentous

**Meaning:** Carrying a warning of the future; ominous. 'Portent' and 'portentous' are the exact words for the storm's political meaning.

**Where it applies:** The same speech.

**Textual evidence:** "For I believe they are portentous things / Unto the climate that they point upon."

**Character/Theme:** Casca; foreshadowing; omen.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The storm is "portentous" rather than merely violent: Casca reads it as pointing at a particular "climate", Rome, and the audience, who know the date, reads it as pointing at the Ides.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'scary weather', 'bad weather'

**Better word:** 'portentous', 'ominous', 'a storm of signs'

**Keyword:** construe

**Meaning:** To interpret, to put a meaning upon. Cicero's word for how men read the world to suit themselves; Brutus has already used it earlier of his own behaviour.

**Where it applies:** Cicero's sceptical reply to Casca; and Brutus's apology to Cassius in Scene 2.

**Textual evidence:** "But men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves." and "Nor construe any further my neglect"

**Character/Theme:** Cicero; scepticism; interpretation as a theme.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cicero's warning that men "construe things after their fashion" is the key to the whole scene, for within fifty lines Cassius will construe the same storm as a portrait of Caesar.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'understand', 'see'

**Better word:** 'construe', 'interpret', 'read into'

**Keyword:** thunder-stone

**Meaning:** A thunderbolt, imagined in Elizabethan belief as a stone hurled with the lightning. Cassius claims to have offered his bare chest to it.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's account of walking through the storm.

**Textual evidence:** "Have bared my bosom to the thunder-stone"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; defiance; the performance of courage.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's bared bosom before the "thunder-stone" is a theatrical self-presentation, courage staged for Casca's benefit, and it works: the frightened man is shamed into the conspiracy within the same scene.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'lightning'

**Better word:** 'the thunder-stone', 'the bolt of heaven'

**Keyword:** womanish

**Meaning:** Weak and submissive, in Cassius's gendered insult. LITERARY INTERPRETATION: Cassius equates manliness with resistance and submission with women; the play lets the equation stand unchallenged here, though Portia will complicate it in Act 2.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's complaint about present-day Romans.

**Textual evidence:** "our fathers' minds are dead, / And we are governed with our mothers' spirits. / Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; rhetoric of shame; Roman masculinity.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's "womanish" is a rhetorical goad rather than a description: by making submission feminine he leaves Casca no way to stay neutral that does not also unman him.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'weak', 'cowardly'

**Better word:** 'womanish (Cassius's gendered insult)', 'unmanned', 'servile'

**Keyword:** yoke and sufferance

**Meaning:** The yoke is the wooden bar placed on oxen; 'sufferance' is patient endurance. Together: the burden Romans carry and their willingness to carry it.

**Where it applies:** The same line.

**Textual evidence:** "Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; bondage; the complicity of the governed.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'In "yoke and sufferance" Cassius blames not only the yoke but the suffering of it: tyranny, in his account, is a partnership between the man who imposes and the men who endure.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'oppression', 'putting up with it'

**Better word:** 'the yoke', 'servile endurance', 'consented subjection'

**Keyword:** bondage, bondman

**Meaning:** Slavery; a slave. Cassius's language in Scene 3 repeatedly casts Romans as slaves and the dagger as the key to freedom.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's response to the news that the senators mean to crown Caesar; Casca's echo; Cassius's wary aside.

**Textual evidence:** "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius." and "So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity." and "I perhaps speak this / Before a willing bondman"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius and Casca; liberty versus slavery; suicide as the last freedom.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's "bondage" is a word of absolute politics: there is no middle condition between the free Roman and the "bondman", and so there is no middle course between killing Caesar and consenting to slavery.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'being controlled', 'not free'

**Better word:** 'bondage', 'servitude', 'enslavement'

**Keyword:** wolf, sheep

**Meaning:** Predator and prey. Cassius says Caesar would not be a wolf if the Romans were not sheep.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's answer to his own question about why Caesar should be a tyrant.

**Textual evidence:** "Poor man, I know he would not be a wolf / But that he sees the Romans are but sheep"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; tyranny as a relationship; the people's responsibility.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The wolf-and-sheep image is Cassius's theory of tyranny in one line: Caesar's appetite is created by Roman timidity, so the cure lies in the sheep, not the wolf.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'Caesar is like a predator'

**Better word:** 'the predator-prey image', 'the tyrant as wolf', 'the populace as sheep'

**Keyword:** hinds

**Meaning:** Female deer, timid and hunted; the word also meant farm servants, and both senses of weakness are active.

**Where it applies:** The same speech.

**Textual evidence:** "He were no lion, were not Romans hinds."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; animal imagery; the degeneration of Rome.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's "hinds" doubles the insult, for the word means both the deer that lions hunt and the servant who tends another man's fields; Romans are at once prey and peasantry.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'deer', 'weaklings'

**Better word:** 'hinds', 'timid prey', 'the hunted'

**Keyword:** offal

**Meaning:** Refuse, waste, the discarded parts of a butchered animal. Cassius's most violent word for Rome.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's outburst that Rome has become fuel for Caesar's fire.

**Textual evidence:** "What trash is Rome, / What rubbish, and what offal when it serves / For the base matter to illuminate / So vile a thing as Caesar!"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; contempt; the paradox of a patriot who despises his city.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'When Cassius calls Rome "offal", the republican's love of his city has curdled into disgust, and Shakespeare lets us hear that the conspiracy is fuelled as much by contempt as by principle.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'rubbish', 'garbage'

**Better word:** 'offal', 'base matter', 'the refuse of a degraded Rome'

**Keyword:** factious

**Meaning:** Forming a faction, a party of resistance. Casca's word, as he commits himself.

**Where it applies:** Casca's pledge, with his hand.

**Textual evidence:** "Be factious for redress of all these griefs, / And I will set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest."

**Character/Theme:** Casca; conspiratorial politics; the conspiracy's self-description.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's "Be factious" is revealing in its honesty: he names the conspiracy as a faction, a party pursuing its own grievances, at the very moment Cassius is dressing it as the salvation of Rome.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'rebellious', 'start a group'

**Better word:** 'factious', 'form a faction', 'conspiratorial'

**Keyword:** redress

**Meaning:** Remedy, the setting-right of a wrong. Casca speaks of redress of griefs; the word gives the conspiracy the colour of legal grievance.

**Where it applies:** The same line.

**Textual evidence:** "Be factious for redress of all these griefs"

**Character/Theme:** Casca; the conspiracy's justification; grievance as motive.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The conspirators' word for assassination is "redress", a term from the law courts; the vocabulary shows them translating murder into the language of remedy before a blow has been struck.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'fixing things', 'revenge'

**Better word:** 'redress', 'remedy of grievances', 'restitution'

**Keyword:** honourable-dangerous

**Meaning:** Cassius's compound: an enterprise at once honourable and dangerous. The hyphen fuses the two ideas so that neither can be separated from the other.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's revelation that others are already committed.

**Textual evidence:** "an enterprise / Of honourable-dangerous consequence"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; the moral ambiguity of the conspiracy; honour as recruitment.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's compound "honourable-dangerous" is the conspiracy's whole self-image in a hyphen: it offers Casca the honour as payment for the danger, and never asks whether the two are compatible.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'risky but good'

**Better word:** 'honourable-dangerous', 'a perilous honour', 'noble hazard'

**Keyword:** alchemy

**Meaning:** The medieval art of turning base metal into gold. Casca says Brutus's reputation will transform the conspirators' crime into virtue.

**Where it applies:** Casca's final speech of the act.

**Textual evidence:** "His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus's reputation; appearance versus reality; the conspiracy's need for legitimacy.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's "alchemy" is a confession dressed as a compliment: what the conspirators do would "appear offence", and only Brutus's face can transmute it, which means they know it is base metal.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'make it look good'

**Better word:** 'alchemy', 'transmutation', 'the laundering of a crime through reputation'

**Keyword:** conceited

**Meaning:** Here, conceived or understood; expressed as a conceit (an idea). Not the modern sense of vain.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's reply to Casca's alchemy speech.

**Textual evidence:** "You have right well conceited."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; the conspirators' shared understanding of Brutus's use.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's approving "right well conceited" shows that the use of Brutus as a moral cover is not Casca's private cynicism but the conspiracy's agreed plan.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'vain', 'proud' (a misreading of the word)

**Better word:** 'conceived', 'understood', 'aptly expressed'

## Type B, Group One: The Politics of Rome

These are the terms that let you say what kind of state Rome is, what Caesar threatens to make it, and what the conspirators think they are defending. Several are HISTORICAL RECORD and must be used with their Roman meanings, not their modern ones.

**Keyword:** republicanism

**Meaning:** The belief that the state belongs to its citizens and must be governed by elected magistrates, shared power and law, never by one man. HISTORICAL RECORD: Rome's republic dated its birth from the expulsion of King Tarquin, traditionally 509 BC, and Romans held a deep cultural hatred of the title 'rex'.

**Where it applies:** The tribunes' resistance in Scene 1; Brutus's fear that the people "Choose Caesar for their king"; Cassius's appeal to the old Brutus.

**Textual evidence:** "There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; Flavius and Marullus; the ideological core of the conspiracy.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus's republicanism is inherited as well as reasoned: Cassius invokes the ancestor who expelled a king precisely because the name "Brutus" carries a political obligation.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'wanting democracy', 'being against Caesar'

**Better word:** 'republicanism', 'republican principle', 'the libertas of the res publica'

**Keyword:** tyranny

**Meaning:** Rule by one man above the law, exercised in his own interest. In Act 1 the word is used by Cassius and Casca of the Caesar they fear, not of the Caesar we see; the play keeps the question of whether Caesar is a tyrant open.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's storm speech and Casca's reply.

**Textual evidence:** "That part of tyranny that I do bear / I can shake off at pleasure." and "And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then?"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; the conspiracy's justification; the gap between fear and fact.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius speaks of the "tyranny" he bears as a present fact, but Shakespeare shows us a Caesar who has refused a crown three times; the word is an anticipation disguised as a description.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'being a bad king', 'dictatorship' (used loosely)

**Better word:** 'tyranny', 'autocratic rule', 'arbitrary power'

**Keyword:** autocracy

**Meaning:** Government by a single ruler with unlimited authority. Useful as the neutral term where 'tyranny' would prejudge the question.

**Where it applies:** The crown-offering; the rumour that the senators will make Caesar king "In every place save here in Italy".

**Textual evidence:** "Indeed, they say the Senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king"

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; the drift of the state; political anxiety.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Act 1 shows Rome sliding towards autocracy by rumour and ceremony rather than by decree, which is why the conspirators act on what "they say" the Senate will do.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'one-man rule' (acceptable), 'kingship' (too narrow)

**Better word:** 'autocracy', 'personal rule', 'monarchy in all but name'

**Keyword:** political legitimacy

**Meaning:** The recognised right to hold power. Marullus's objection to the triumph, the tribunes' silencing and Casca's 'alchemy' speech are all arguments about legitimacy: who has the right to rule, to celebrate, to resist.

**Where it applies:** Marullus's rebuke; Casca on Brutus's reputation.

**Textual evidence:** "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home?" and "that which would appear offence in us / His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue"

**Character/Theme:** The tribunes; the conspiracy; Brutus as a source of legitimacy.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Both sides in Act 1 hunger for legitimacy: Caesar borrows it from ceremony and acclamation, while the conspirators plan to borrow it from Brutus's face.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'having the right', 'being allowed'

**Better word:** 'legitimacy', 'sanction', 'constitutional right'

**Keyword:** dictator

**Meaning:** HISTORICAL RECORD: an emergency Roman office, traditionally limited to six months. Caesar held it repeatedly and, in February 44 BC, was made dictator for life, breaking the limit. The play never uses the word in Act 1, so use it as historical background, not as Shakespeare's term.

**Where it applies:** Background to every reference to Caesar's "new honours".

**Textual evidence:** "I do believe that these applauses are / For some new honours that are heaped on Caesar."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; the collapse of republican limits.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Historically Caesar was already dictator for life when the Lupercal was held; Shakespeare omits the title and lets Brutus's vague "new honours" register the same unease.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'dictator' used in the modern sense of tyrant

**Better word:** 'dictator (in the Roman sense of an emergency magistrate)', 'perpetual dictator'

**Keyword:** tribune

**Meaning:** HISTORICAL RECORD: a tribune of the plebs was an elected officer whose person was sacrosanct and whose task was to protect the common people, with the power to veto legislation. Flavius and Marullus hold this office, which makes their contempt for the commoners a dramatic irony of its own.

**Where it applies:** Scene 1, and Casca's report that they "are put to silence".

**Textual evidence:** "Go, go, good countrymen, and for this fault / Assemble all the poor men of your sort"

**Character/Theme:** Flavius and Marullus; the people's protectors against the people.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The irony of Scene 1 depends on knowing what a tribune was: the officers created to protect the plebeians spend the opening of the play scolding them, and are then themselves silenced by the man the plebeians cheer.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'officials', 'soldiers', 'guards'

**Better word:** 'tribunes of the plebs', 'the people's magistrates'

**Keyword:** plebeian

**Meaning:** HISTORICAL RECORD: a Roman citizen not of the old patrician families; by the late Republic plebeians could hold all offices, but the word kept its sense of the common mass. In the play, the commoners of Scene 1 and the crowd of Casca's report.

**Where it applies:** Scene 1; Casca's narrative; Casca's praise of Brutus's popularity.

**Textual evidence:** "O, he sits high in all the people's hearts"

**Character/Theme:** The crowd; public opinion; class.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Shakespeare gives the plebeians prose and wit in Scene 1 and then lets Casca describe them as a stinking "rabblement" in Scene 2; the student's task is to notice that we see them twice, once directly and once through patrician eyes.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'the people', 'the public', 'commoners' (acceptable)

**Better word:** 'the plebeians', 'the populace', 'the commons'

**Keyword:** patrician

**Meaning:** HISTORICAL RECORD: a member of Rome's old aristocratic families. Brutus, Cassius, Casca and Caesar belong to the governing class; the conspiracy is an aristocratic affair.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's lament for "the breed of noble bloods"; the whole Scene 2 dialogue between men of rank.

**Textual evidence:** "Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; class; the conspiracy as a quarrel within the elite.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The conspiracy is a patrician project: Cassius's grievance is that one noble has risen above his peers, not that the plebeians are unfree, and his contempt for the "sheep" confirms it.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'rich people', 'nobles' (acceptable)

**Better word:** 'patrician', 'the senatorial class', 'the aristocracy'

**Keyword:** triumph

**Meaning:** HISTORICAL RECORD: a procession granted by the Senate to a victorious general, with captives, spoils and a laurel-crowned general in a chariot; a triumph for victory over fellow Romans was considered improper. Caesar's Spanish triumph of 45 BC celebrated the defeat of Pompey's sons.

**Where it applies:** The occasion of Scene 1.

**Textual evidence:** "we / make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his / triumph" and "That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood?"

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; Marullus; ceremony and legitimacy.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Marullus does not object to triumphs; he objects to this one, because a triumph over "Pompey's blood" parades Roman defeat as foreign conquest.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'victory parade', 'celebration'

**Better word:** 'triumph', 'the triumphal procession', 'a civil-war triumph'

**Keyword:** liberty

**Meaning:** Freedom from domination; for a Roman, the condition of being a citizen rather than a subject or slave. HISTORICAL RECORD: libertas was a core republican value.

**Where it applies:** Scene 1's "servile fearfulness"; Cassius's "born free as Caesar"; the bondage speeches of Scene 3.

**Textual evidence:** "I was born free as Caesar; so were you"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; Brutus; the conspiracy's declared cause.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius defines liberty negatively, as not being "In awe of such a thing as I myself"; his liberty is equality with Caesar, which is why it is impossible to tell where his love of freedom ends and his envy begins.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'freedom' (acceptable), 'rights'

**Better word:** 'liberty', 'libertas', 'republican freedom'

**Keyword:** civic duty

**Meaning:** The obligation of a citizen to put the state before himself. Brutus's governing principle; the standard by which he says he will judge Cassius's proposal.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's "general good" speech.

**Textual evidence:** "If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other"

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; political idealism; the ethics of the conspiracy.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus frames the decision as civic duty from the first moment, and Cassius learns from this that the way to Brutus runs through Rome, not through Brutus's own interest.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'doing the right thing', 'patriotism' (close but vaguer)

**Better word:** 'civic duty', 'public obligation', 'service to the republic'

**Keyword:** dignitas

**Meaning:** HISTORICAL RECORD: the Roman concept of a nobleman's personal standing, the honour and rank he had earned and was entitled to defend; ancient sources, including Caesar's own account of the civil war, present him as fighting to protect his dignitas. In the play the word is not used, but the idea underlies Cassius's humiliation at having to "bend his body".

**Where it applies:** Cassius's Tiber and fever anecdotes.

**Textual evidence:** "And this man / Is now become a god, and Cassius is / A wretched creature and must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; resentment; the aristocratic code.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's grievance is best described as wounded dignitas: not that Rome is oppressed, but that a man he once rescued now receives the bow that Cassius's rank should spare him.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'pride', 'ego'

**Better word:** 'dignitas', 'standing', 'aristocratic self-worth'

**Keyword:** authority

**Meaning:** The acknowledged power to command. Caesar's authority in Act 1 is shown as instantaneous obedience: he speaks and it is done.

**Where it applies:** Antony's reply to Caesar's instruction about Calpurnia.

**Textual evidence:** "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; power; Antony's loyalty.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Antony's "it is performed" defines Caesar's authority as the collapse of the gap between word and deed, and the whole conspiracy can be read as an attempt to reopen that gap.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'power' (acceptable but vaguer), 'control'

**Better word:** 'authority', 'command', 'the force of his word'

## Type B, Group Two: Persuasion and the Crowd

Act 1 is a play about how minds are moved: Brutus's by Cassius, Casca's by Cassius, the crowd's by Caesar. These terms let you name the methods.

**Keyword:** manipulation

**Meaning:** Influencing someone by indirect, concealed or dishonest means so that they act as you wish while believing the choice is their own. The exact word for Cassius's method with Brutus.

**Where it applies:** The whole Brutus-Cassius dialogue; the forged letters.

**Textual evidence:** "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; Brutus's vulnerability; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's manipulation works by concealment of its own purpose: he never asks Brutus to do anything, only to see himself, and leaves Brutus to draw the conclusion that Cassius has arranged for him to draw.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'tricks', 'convinces', 'brainwashes'

**Better word:** 'manipulates', 'insinuates', 'works upon'

**Keyword:** rhetoric

**Meaning:** The art of persuasive speech: the use of questions, repetition, imagery and appeals to emotion to move an audience. Marullus, Cassius and Caesar are all rhetoricians in Act 1.

**Where it applies:** Marullus's rebuke (rhetorical questions and repeated "And do you now"); Cassius's weighing of names.

**Textual evidence:** "And do you now put on your best attire? / And do you now cull out a holiday? / And do you now strew flowers in his way"

**Character/Theme:** Marullus; Cassius; political rhetoric.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Marullus's rhetoric builds through triple repetition of "And do you now", each question tightening the charge of ingratitude until the final line names the crime.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'speech', 'talking well'

**Better word:** 'rhetoric', 'oratory', 'rhetorical questioning'

**Keyword:** persuasion

**Meaning:** The open attempt to change someone's mind by argument. Distinguish it from manipulation: persuasion declares its goal; manipulation hides it. Cassius's storm speech to Casca is closer to open persuasion than his Scene 2 seduction of Brutus.

**Where it applies:** Cassius and Casca in Scene 3.

**Textual evidence:** "Now could I, Casca, name to thee a man / Most like this dreadful night"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; Casca; political rhetoric.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'With Casca Cassius persuades; with Brutus he manipulates. The difference in method measures the difference in the men: Casca can be frightened into action, Brutus must be led to believe the action is his own.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'convincing'

**Better word:** 'persuasion', 'open argument', 'exhortation'

**Keyword:** populism

**Meaning:** Politics that claims to speak for 'the people' against an elite, and courts the crowd directly. Use with care: the term is modern. In Act 1 it describes the crown-offering as a bid for popular acclamation outside the Senate.

**Where it applies:** Casca's report of Caesar offering his throat to the crowd.

**Textual evidence:** "he plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut"

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; public opinion; politics as theatre.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Caesar's offer of his throat to the "common herd" is populist theatre: he seeks his sanction from the crowd's forgiveness rather than from the Senate's vote, and the conspirators understand the threat.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'showing off', 'being popular'

**Better word:** 'populist gesture', 'courting the crowd', 'direct appeal to the populace'

**Keyword:** public opinion

**Meaning:** What the mass of people think and will applaud. In Act 1 it is a force that every faction tries to read, shape or forge.

**Where it applies:** The shouts offstage in Scene 2; Cassius's letters "all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name".

**Textual evidence:** "Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name"

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; Brutus; the crowd.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Public opinion in Act 1 is heard before it is seen: Brutus and Cassius interpret the offstage shouts, and Cassius then decides to manufacture an opinion of his own in "several hands".'

**Common weak substitute:** 'what people think'

**Better word:** 'public opinion', 'popular sentiment', 'the voice of Rome (real or forged)'

**Keyword:** demagoguery

**Meaning:** Winning power by appealing to the crowd's emotions and prejudices rather than its reason. In Act 1 the word fits Caesar's theatrical refusals and foreshadows Antony in Act 3.

**Where it applies:** Casca's report of the crown scene.

**Textual evidence:** "the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands"

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; the crowd; the rhetoric of power.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's account shows a demagogue at work: Caesar reads the crowd's mood, refuses the crown when they cheer the refusal, and turns his own collapse into an appeal for pity.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'playing to the crowd' (acceptable), 'lying'

**Better word:** 'demagoguery', 'demagogic appeal', 'crowd-pleasing manipulation'

**Keyword:** fickleness

**Meaning:** Changeability of loyalty; the readiness to switch allegiance. The defining charge against the Roman crowd from Scene 1 onward.

**Where it applies:** Marullus's contrast of the people's love for Pompey with their cheers for Caesar.

**Textual evidence:** "Knew you not Pompey?" and "Pray to the gods to intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude."

**Character/Theme:** The plebeians; mob psychology; the instability of power built on applause.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The crowd's fickleness is established in Scene 1 as a kind of original sin, and the play will return to it when the same populace is turned by Antony in Act 3.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'changing their minds', 'disloyal'

**Better word:** 'fickle', 'inconstant', 'mutable'

**Keyword:** conspiratorial politics

**Meaning:** Politics conducted secretly, by a faction, outside legitimate institutions. Scene 3's night-time meeting, the passwords, the letters and "Pompey's Porch" are its furniture.

**Where it applies:** The whole of Cassius's Scene 3 recruitment.

**Textual evidence:** "Who's there?" and "A Roman." and "And I do know by this they stay for me / In Pompey's Porch."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius, Casca, Cinna; secrecy; the conspiracy takes shape.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The exchange "Who's there?" and "A Roman." marks the moment the play's politics go underground: men who should debate in the Senate now identify themselves by voice in a storm.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'plotting', 'secret plans'

**Better word:** 'conspiratorial politics', 'clandestine faction', 'the conspiracy's night-world'

**Keyword:** political anxiety

**Meaning:** The pervasive fear that the state is about to change, felt before anything has actually happened. Act 1 is saturated with it: the tribunes fear, Brutus fears, Casca fears, and even Caesar fears.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's reaction to the first shout; Caesar on Cassius.

**Textual evidence:** "What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; the mood of the act; anticipation as a dramatic device.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Act 1 dramatises political anxiety rather than political events: no crown is accepted, no blow is struck, but every character interprets the shouts, the storm and one another as signs of a change already under way.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'worry', 'tension'

**Better word:** 'political anxiety', 'foreboding', 'the fear of a change of state'

## Type B, Group Three: Character and Mind

These terms describe motive and inner life. They are the words that lift a character answer from a list of adjectives to an account of a person.

**Keyword:** political idealism

**Meaning:** Acting from principle about how the state ought to be, rather than from interest or calculation. Brutus's defining quality, and (LITERARY INTERPRETATION) the source of his vulnerability.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's "general good" and "villager" speeches.

**Textual evidence:** "Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions"

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; honour; the ethics of the conspiracy.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus's political idealism is genuine, but idealism is exactly what Cassius's forged letters are designed to feed: an idealist can be moved by an idea, and Cassius supplies the idea in writing.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'being noble', 'good intentions'

**Better word:** 'political idealism', 'principled commitment', 'devotion to the republic'

**Keyword:** political naïveté

**Meaning:** A failure to see how power and people actually work, often caused by assuming others share one's own honesty. In Act 1 Brutus's trust in Cassius, and his inability to see that he is being handled, are its first signs.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's reply to Cassius's seduction.

**Textual evidence:** "That you do love me, I am nothing jealous. / What you would work me to, I have some aim."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; manipulation; dramatic irony.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus's "I am nothing jealous" is the sentence of a politically naive man: he half-sees what Cassius "would work" him to, yet never suspects that the working itself is dishonest.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'stupid', 'innocent', 'too trusting' (acceptable)

**Better word:** 'political naïveté', 'misplaced trust', 'an idealist's blindness to method'

**Keyword:** resentment

**Meaning:** Bitter indignation at being treated unfairly, nursed over time. The best single word for Cassius's motive in Scene 2, where personal injury keeps surfacing through political argument.

**Where it applies:** The Tiber and fever anecdotes.

**Textual evidence:** "'Tis true, this god did shake." and "As a sick girl."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; envy; the private roots of public action.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's resentment leaks through his politics: the man who claims to speak of "honour" cannot resist recalling that Caesar cried for drink "As a sick girl", a detail that serves no republican argument and every personal one.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'jealous', 'hates Caesar'

**Better word:** 'resentful', 'envious', 'aggrieved'

**Keyword:** ambition

**Meaning:** The desire for power and greatness beyond one's present station. In Act 1 'ambition' is what Caesar's enemies attribute to him; the text shows a man who refuses a crown and a reporter who thinks the refusal was reluctant.

**Where it applies:** Casca's report; Cassius's plan to glance at "Caesar's ambition" in the letters.

**Textual evidence:** "to my thinking, he would fain have had it" and "Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at"

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; Cassius; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'In Act 1 Caesar's ambition exists chiefly as other men's inference: Casca thinks he "would fain have had" the crown, and Cassius plans letters in which it "shall be glancèd at", so that the charge is built from hints before it is ever proved.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'wanting to be king', 'greedy'

**Better word:** 'ambition', 'aspiration to monarchy', 'the hunger for sovereignty'

**Keyword:** honour (as an analytical term)

**Meaning:** Used analytically, honour is a code: the set of values by which a Roman noble measures himself and expects to be measured. Different characters mean different things by it, and your job is to say which.

**Where it applies:** Brutus (inner integrity); Cassius (standing and equality); Casca ("honourable-dangerous").

**Textual evidence:** "I know that virtue to be in you, Brutus, / As well as I do know your outward favour. / Well, honour is the subject of my story."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; Cassius; the contested meaning of the play's central word.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Honour in Act 1 is not one idea but a battleground: Brutus's honour is a private conscience, Cassius's a public rank, and the conspiracy is formed in the space where the two meanings are allowed to blur.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'respect', 'being honest'

**Better word:** 'the honour code', 'competing conceptions of honour'

**Keyword:** self-mythologising

**Meaning:** Constructing and performing a legendary version of oneself. Caesar speaks of 'Caesar' in the third person as though describing a public institution.

**Where it applies:** Caesar's speeches on the Soothsayer and on Cassius.

**Textual evidence:** "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; public persona; hubris (with caution).

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Caesar's self-mythologising reaches its purest form in "for always I am Caesar", where the name has become a standard he must live up to, and the sentence that follows, admitting a deaf ear, shows the standard already failing.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'proud', 'arrogant', 'full of himself'

**Better word:** 'self-mythologising', 'assured of his authority', 'performing his own legend'

**Keyword:** public persona

**Meaning:** The self a figure presents to the world, as distinct from the private person. Caesar's persona is the invulnerable 'Caesar'; Casca's is the blunt cynic; Cassius's, in Scene 3, is the fearless Roman.

**Where it applies:** Caesar's return from the games; Casca's "tardy form".

**Textual evidence:** "Would he were fatter! But I fear him not."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; Casca; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Caesar's public persona requires him to say "I fear him not" immediately after describing exactly why Cassius should be feared; the persona overrides the perception.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'image', 'how he acts in public'

**Better word:** 'public persona', 'the performed self', 'the mask of office'

**Keyword:** private self

**Meaning:** The inner person beneath the persona: the body that shakes, the mind at war with itself, the grievance behind the principle. Act 1 repeatedly lets the private self show through.

**Where it applies:** Caesar's deaf ear; Brutus's "with himself at war"; Cassius's soliloquy.

**Textual evidence:** "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar, Brutus, Cassius; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Shakespeare gives Cassius the only soliloquy in Act 1, and in it the private self speaks plainly: "Caesar doth bear me hard" is a personal wound, confessed only when the stage is empty.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'his real feelings', 'inside'

**Better word:** 'private self', 'inner life', 'the man behind the name'

**Keyword:** hubris (use with caution)

**Meaning:** In Greek thought, overweening pride that offends the gods and invites downfall. CAUTION: the term belongs to Greek tragedy and religion. Julius Caesar is a Roman play built from Plutarch, and it never says that Caesar's death is a divine punishment. Use 'overconfidence' or 'self-mythologising' unless you define 'hubris' and show you know its origin.

**Where it applies:** Caesar's dismissal of the Soothsayer; "always I am Caesar".

**Textual evidence:** "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; fate; the limits of a classical label.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Caesar's dismissal of the Soothsayer is often called hubris, but the Greek word imports a theology the play does not supply; it is more exact to say that Caesar's self-image as "Caesar" forbids him to be seen heeding a street prophet.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'arrogance' (vaguer but at least not anachronistic)

**Better word:** 'overconfidence', 'self-mythologising', 'the refusal to appear afraid'

**Keyword:** moral conflict

**Meaning:** A struggle between two things a person believes to be good or two duties he owes. Brutus's conflict is between love for Caesar and love for Rome.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's reply to Cassius's question about Caesar as king.

**Textual evidence:** "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well."

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; friendship versus duty.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus's moral conflict is stated in a single line, "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well", whose "yet" is the hinge on which the rest of the play turns.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'confused', 'torn' (acceptable), 'doesn't know what to do'

**Better word:** 'moral conflict', 'divided loyalty', 'a conflict of duties'

**Keyword:** ideological conflict

**Meaning:** A clash between systems of political belief, here between republicanism and the drift towards one-man rule. Distinguish it from Brutus's moral conflict, which is personal; the ideological conflict is between Rome's past and its possible future.

**Where it applies:** The tribunes against the holiday crowd; the "two Brutuses" (the ancestor and the descendant).

**Textual evidence:** "Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man."

**Character/Theme:** The whole act; republicanism versus autocracy.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's pun on "Rome" and "room" states the ideological conflict as a matter of space: a republic has room for many men, an autocracy for one.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'disagreement', 'different opinions'

**Better word:** 'ideological conflict', 'a clash of political creeds', 'republic against monarchy'

**Keyword:** stoicism (Brutus, cautiously)

**Meaning:** The philosophy that the wise man governs his passions by reason and regards external fortune, including death, with calm. HISTORICAL RECORD: Brutus was the nephew by marriage of Cato the Younger, a famous Stoic-leaning Roman. CAUTION: Act 1 never names the philosophy, and in Act 5 Brutus's own remarks about suicide do not fit a simple Stoic label; say 'Stoic-leaning' or 'a temper many critics call Stoic'.

**Where it applies:** Brutus's willingness to look on honour and death "indifferently".

**Textual evidence:** "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently"

**Character/Theme:** Brutus; honour; self-command.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Brutus's promise to look on honour and death "indifferently" has a Stoic colouring that many critics note, though Act 1 never names the school; the safer claim is that he values principle above survival.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'calm', 'brave', 'doesn't care about dying'

**Better word:** 'Stoic-leaning', 'self-governed', 'indifferent to fortune'

**Keyword:** epicureanism (Cassius, cautiously)

**Meaning:** The philosophy that the gods do not intervene in human affairs, so omens are meaningless. HISTORICAL RECORD: the historical Cassius was an Epicurean. CAUTION: Act 1 never names Epicurus, and in Scene 3 Cassius reads the storm as a sign, whether sincerely or as a tool to move Casca. In Act 5 he says he once held Epicurus's opinion strongly and now changes his mind. Use the term only to point out the gap between Cassius's reputation and his behaviour.

**Where it applies:** Cassius's reading of the storm as "instruments of fear and warning".

**Textual evidence:** "heaven hath infused them with these spirits / To make them instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state."

**Character/Theme:** Cassius; scepticism versus superstition; rhetoric.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Whether Cassius believes the storm is a warning or merely uses it is left open; a student who knows his Epicurean reputation can argue that the omens are, for him, a rhetorical instrument rather than a conviction.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'not religious', 'doesn't believe in signs'

**Better word:** 'Epicurean (by reputation)', 'sceptical of omens', 'instrumental use of the supernatural'

## Type B, Group Four: The Supernatural and the Reader's Toolkit

The last group holds the terms of dramatic technique and of the play's supernatural layer. Two carry cautions that examiners will respect you for knowing.

**Keyword:** dramatic irony

**Meaning:** The gap between what a character knows and what the audience knows. Because the audience knows that Caesar will die on the Ides of March, almost everything said about the future in Act 1 is charged with it.

**Where it applies:** The Soothsayer; Caesar on Cassius; Antony's "well given".

**Textual evidence:** "He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous." and "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous."

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; the Soothsayer; fate.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'The dramatic irony of Caesar's exchange with Antony is doubled: Caesar is right about Cassius and wrong to dismiss his own rightness, while Antony, who will later avenge him, is simply wrong.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'irony' (undefined), 'the audience knows'

**Better word:** 'dramatic irony', 'proleptic irony', 'the audience's foreknowledge'

**Keyword:** foreshadowing

**Meaning:** Hints that prepare for later events. In Act 1 the Soothsayer's warning, the storm, Cassius's "worse days", and Brutus's "with himself at war" all look ahead.

**Where it applies:** The end of Cassius's soliloquy; Scene 3's omens.

**Textual evidence:** "And after this, let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure."

**Character/Theme:** Structure; fate; the conspiracy's trajectory.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius's couplet foreshadows with a double edge: "we will shake him" anticipates the assassination, and "worse days endure" anticipates, without his knowing it, the civil war that follows it.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'hints at the future', 'shows what will happen'

**Better word:** 'foreshadows', 'anticipates', 'prefigures'

**Keyword:** supernatural imagery

**Meaning:** Language drawn from omens, spirits and divine anger. In Act 1 it is concentrated in Scene 3, but note that the supernatural is reported and interpreted, never shown directly on stage.

**Where it applies:** Casca's prodigies; Cassius's storm speech.

**Textual evidence:** "Either there is a civil strife in heaven, / Or else the world, too saucy with the gods, / Incenses them to send destruction."

**Character/Theme:** Casca; Cassius; the correspondence between heaven and state.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's supernatural imagery offers two readings of the storm, "civil strife in heaven" or divine anger at a "saucy" world, and both are political metaphors before they are religious ones.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'scary stuff', 'magic'

**Better word:** 'supernatural imagery', 'omen imagery', 'the language of portent'

**Keyword:** political symbolism

**Meaning:** Objects or gestures that stand for political meanings: the crown, the scarves on the statues, the triumph itself. Act 1's politics are conducted almost entirely through symbols.

**Where it applies:** The decorated images; the coronet; Caesar's bared throat.

**Textual evidence:** "yet 'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets"

**Character/Theme:** Caesar; the tribunes; appearance versus reality.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's correction, "'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets", is a lesson in political symbolism: the object matters less than what it is seen to mean, and Caesar's refusal is a performance with a symbol as its prop.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'things that represent'

**Better word:** 'political symbolism', 'emblem', 'the iconography of power'

**Keyword:** pathetic fallacy (use with caution)

**Meaning:** The attribution of human feelings to nature ('the angry sky'). CAUTION: the term was coined by John Ruskin in the nineteenth century and describes a habit of poetic description. The storm of Scene 3 is not nature sharing a character's mood; it is read by the characters as a portent, a sign of disorder in the state. Prefer 'omen', 'portent' or 'correspondence between nature and state'.

**Where it applies:** The storm, if at all.

**Textual evidence:** "the scolding winds / Have rived the knotty oaks"

**Character/Theme:** The storm; the limits of a critical term.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's "scolding winds" might tempt one to call the storm pathetic fallacy, but the characters do not treat the weather as feeling; they treat it as meaning, and the apt term is portent.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'the weather matches the mood'

**Better word:** 'portent', 'omen', 'the disordered macrocosm'

**Keyword:** correspondence between nature and state

**Meaning:** The Elizabethan belief that the natural world (the macrocosm) and the human body or the state (the microcosm) mirror one another, so disorder in one appears as disorder in the other. Many critics read the storm of Scene 3 through this belief.

**Where it applies:** Casca's prodigies; Cassius's comparison of Caesar to the night.

**Textual evidence:** "A man no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action, yet prodigious grown, / And fearful, as these strange eruptions are."

**Character/Theme:** The storm; Caesar; political disorder.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cassius exploits the correspondence between nature and state by making Caesar "Most like this dreadful night": if the heavens are disordered, then by Elizabethan logic the state must be, and Caesar is named as the disorder.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'the storm shows something bad will happen'

**Better word:** 'correspondence of macrocosm and microcosm', 'the disordered heavens as political sign'

**Keyword:** omen, portent, prodigy

**Meaning:** Three near-synonyms with different weights. An omen is any sign of the future; a portent is a sign that warns; a prodigy is an unnatural happening (a lion in the street, a hand that burns unscorched) that is itself the sign. Act 1 uses 'prodigies' and 'portentous'.

**Where it applies:** Scene 3 throughout; the Soothsayer in Scene 2.

**Textual evidence:** "It is the part of men to fear and tremble / When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us."

**Character/Theme:** Casca; the Soothsayer; fate and superstition.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca's "dreadful heralds" are prodigies read as portents: he moves from the events themselves to the warning they carry, and his fear is the proper Roman response that Cassius will shame him out of.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'signs', 'warnings'

**Better word:** 'omen', 'portent', 'prodigy' (choose the exact one)

**Keyword:** scepticism

**Meaning:** The refusal to accept a claim without good evidence, especially about the supernatural. Cicero is the act's sceptic; Caesar's "dreamer" is a sceptic's dismissal, though undercut by his earlier faith in the Lupercal ritual.

**Where it applies:** Cicero's reply to Casca.

**Textual evidence:** "Indeed, it is a strange-disposèd time. / But men may construe things after their fashion"

**Character/Theme:** Cicero; interpretation; the play's refusal to settle the question of fate.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Cicero's scepticism is the play's own hedge: by letting one character warn that men "construe things after their fashion", Shakespeare keeps the storm's meaning open and makes every interpreter of it, Cassius included, a suspect.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'not believing', 'doubtful'

**Better word:** 'scepticism', 'rational caution', 'interpretive doubt'

**Keyword:** rationalism

**Meaning:** The habit of explaining events by natural causes and reason rather than by divine agency. In Act 1 it is the position Casca argues against ("These are their reasons, they are natural") and Cicero gently represents.

**Where it applies:** Casca's refusal of natural explanation; Cicero's reply.

**Textual evidence:** "let not men say / 'These are their reasons, they are natural,' / For I believe they are portentous things"

**Character/Theme:** Casca; Cicero; superstition against reason.

**How to use it in an ICSE answer:** 'Casca anticipates the rationalist objection in order to reject it, and the fact that Shakespeare gives the rationalist position a voice at all shows how carefully the play balances superstition against reason.'

**Common weak substitute:** 'being logical', 'scientific'

**Better word:** 'rationalism', 'naturalistic explanation', 'reasoned doubt'

::: icse Do not force a keyword where it does not fit
EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE. The vault is a set of precise tools, and a precise tool used in the wrong place does more harm than a blunt one. Three rules. First, a Type A word belongs only to the moment it comes from: do not describe the crowd in Scene 1 as a "rabblement", because that is Casca's word for a different crowd on a different occasion, and the examiner who knows the text will mark the confusion. Second, a Type B word must be earned by evidence in the same sentence: 'Cassius manipulates Brutus' is an assertion; 'Cassius manipulates Brutus by offering to be his "glass"' is an analysis. Third, the cautions in this chapter are not optional. Calling the storm 'pathetic fallacy', calling Caesar's death 'hubris' or labelling Brutus 'a Stoic' as if the play said so will be read as reaching for impressive words without understanding them, and in a Class X answer on Acts III to V that habit costs more marks than plain English would. If the only word you are sure of is the plain one, use the plain one and add the evidence. Vocabulary is scored as a sign of thought, never as a substitute for it.
:::

::: revision How to drill the vault
Work through one group a day, covering the right-hand side of each entry and reconstructing it from the keyword alone: the meaning, one piece of verified evidence, the character or theme, and a sentence of your own in the style of the sample. Then reverse the drill: read the sample sentence and name the keyword it is built around. Finally, take any paragraph you wrote in Class IX about Act 1, underline every word in the left-hand column of the conversion table, and rewrite the paragraph with the right-hand column. The rewritten paragraph will be shorter, sharper and worth more. For the quotations themselves in longer form, pair this chapter with 'The Act 1 Quotation Bank'; for the devices the Type B terms often accompany (rhetorical question, metaphor, soliloquy), pair it with 'The Act 1 Literary-Device Bank'; for how an examiner actually awards the marks these words earn, read 'ICSE Examiner Mode: How Marks Are Earned'.
