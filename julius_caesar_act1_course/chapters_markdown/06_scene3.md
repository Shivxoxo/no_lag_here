# Act 1 Scene 3 — The Storm: Omens, Fear and the Conspiracy Takes Shape

*What this chapter does: It takes the shortest and strangest scene of Act 1 — a night of thunder, a burning hand, a lion in the street — and teaches it as a complete dramatic unit, from the plain question 'what happened?' up to the level at which you can use it in an ICSE answer. You will learn how Shakespeare turns weather into politics, how Cassius turns Casca's terror into an oath, how the forged letters compromise the conspiracy before Brutus has even joined it, and why one line given to Cicero is a key to the whole play. Every important passage is taught line by line, and the chapter ends with a one-minute revision sheet.*

## Before you read: where we are

When you last saw Cassius, he was alone on a daylit street at the end of Act 1 Scene 2, promising himself that he would throw forged letters in at Brutus's windows and that "we will shake him, or worse days endure." That was the afternoon of the Lupercal, 15 February by the Roman calendar. Now, without a word of explanation, Shakespeare drops you into a night of thunder and lightning, and a question asked halfway through the scene — "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" — tells you that a whole month has passed. It is the night of 14 March. Tomorrow is the Ides.

Hold on to three things before you start reading closely.

First, this scene was written for a theatre with no roof and no electric lighting. At the Globe in 1599 the afternoon sun was shining on the stage while the actors spoke of darkness and fire. The night, the flashes, the terror — all of it had to be made out of words, drums and fireworks. That is why the language of this scene is so extreme: it is doing the work that a lighting rig does today.

Second, the scene has four speakers, but the two people it is really about never appear. Caesar has just been escorted home by Casca; Brutus is asleep — or not asleep — in his house, and every plan made in the scene points towards his door. Learn to notice when Shakespeare builds a scene around an absent person.

Third, this is a recruitment scene. Under the noise of the storm, one man is signed up to a murder and a second man is being softened for it with forged paper. If you read the scene only as 'atmosphere', you will miss its politics; if you read it only as politics, you will miss why the atmosphere is there.

## The scene as a complete dramatic unit: a twenty-point anatomy

A scene is not a heap of quotations. It is a machine with a setting, a set of people who want different things, a change that happens, and a set of consequences. The twenty headings below are the questions a professional reader asks of any scene. Work through them in order the first time; later you can use them as a checklist.

### 1 Setting

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** The Folio gives one stage direction: thunder and lightning. Everything else about the setting is built from the dialogue. Cicero's greeting "Good even, Casca" tells you it is evening; Cassius's closing words "it is after midnight" tell you the scene has carried us past midnight; Casca's remark that "yesterday the bird of night did sit / Even at noonday upon the marketplace" reaches back a day. So the scene spans the hours from dusk to the small hours of the Ides of March, though it plays in about ten minutes. The place is a street in Rome, near enough to the Capitol for Casca to have met his lion "Against the Capitol" (here 'against' means 'opposite' or 'close by').

Notice how different this is from the two scenes before it. Scene 1 was a festival street in daylight, full of commoners; Scene 2 was a public place full of a shouting crowd. Scene 3 is empty. Cassius says so: "There is no stir or walking in the streets." The crowd that decided everything in the first two scenes has gone indoors, and the men who walk the streets now are the men who intend to decide things without the crowd.

::: textnote Casca's sword
The Folger text opens simply with the thunder direction. The Globe-edition text used in many school editions adds that Casca enters 'with his sword drawn'. This is an editorial direction, not Shakespeare's own, but it is justified by Casca's own words a few lines later: "I ha' not since put up my sword" ('ha'' = have; 'put up' = sheathed). Picture him: a senator alone in the street, weapon out, breathless, sweating. The visual image of a drawn sword hangs over a scene that ends with plans for stabbing.
:::

### 2 Characters

Four men speak. Two others rule the scene from off stage.

| Character | Who he is | What he does here | What to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cicero | Rome's most famous orator and a senior senator; historically never part of the conspiracy | Meets Casca, hears the prodigies, doubts what they mean, asks whether Caesar comes to the Capitol tomorrow, goes home | The one voice of reason — and the first to leave |
| Casca | A senator in Caesar's circle (he called for silence when Caesar spoke in Scene 2 and has just walked Caesar home); the sour cynic of Scene 2 | Reports the omens in terror; is recruited by Cassius; gives his hand and swears to go as far as any man | His change from mocker to sworn conspirator |
| Cassius | Praetor, Brutus's brother-in-law, the organiser of the plot | Walks bare-chested under the lightning, reinterprets the omens as warnings against Caesar, recruits Casca, dispatches the forged letters | Every speech is a tool |
| Cinna | Lucius Cornelius Cinna, a praetor and conspirator — not the poet of Act 3 Scene 3 | Arrives in haste from Pompey's Porch, takes the letters | The messenger whose one wish is Brutus |

**Absent but present:** Caesar, whom Casca "Brought … home", and Brutus, who is named again and again ("win the noble Brutus", "Where Brutus may but find it", "See Brutus at his house"). **Named but unseen:** Decius Brutus, Trebonius and Metellus Cimber, waiting at "Pompey's Porch"; Antonius, who is to send word to Cicero.

### 3 Political situation

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** By March 44 BC Caesar had been made dictator for life (in February of that year), an office that in the old Republic was an emergency post limited to six months. He had pardoned his enemies from the civil war, including Brutus and Cassius, and given them the praetorships of 44 BC. The Senate still met and voted, but everyone understood where power lay.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** The play sharpens this into a crisis with a deadline. Casca reports a rumour: "they say the Senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king, / And he shall wear his crown by sea and land / In every place save here in Italy." Caesar is expected at the Capitol in the morning. The tribunes who tore the decorations from his statues have been "put to silence" (Scene 2). And a group of senators — Cassius, Casca, Cinna, Decius Brutus, Trebonius, Metellus Cimber — has already agreed to act. What the conspiracy lacks is the one man whose name would make the killing look like a Roman duty rather than a private murder. The whole political situation of the scene, then, is a race: crown Caesar tomorrow, or kill him tomorrow, and the deciding factor is whether Brutus can be brought in tonight.

### 4 What happens

::: level LEVEL 1 — What happened?
1. Thunder and lightning. Cicero meets Casca, who is breathless and staring. Casca says he has never before been through "a tempest dropping fire" and that either there is "a civil strife in heaven" or the gods are punishing a world grown "too saucy" (insolent).
2. Casca lists the prodigies he has seen: a slave whose hand burned like twenty torches and was unhurt; a lion near the Capitol that stared at him and walked past; a hundred terrified women who swore they saw men walking the streets on fire; and an owl hooting in the marketplace at noon.
3. Cicero agrees it is a strange time but warns that men interpret things to suit themselves. He asks whether Caesar will be at the Capitol tomorrow; Casca says yes. Cicero goes home.
4. Cassius enters, recognises Casca by his voice, calls the night "A very pleasing night to honest men", and boasts that he has walked the streets with his shirt open, offering his chest to the lightning.
5. Casca rebukes him for tempting the heavens. Cassius calls him dull and says the omens are heaven's warning against "some monstrous state" — and against a man "Most like this dreadful night". Casca: it is Caesar you mean, is it not?
6. Cassius laments that Romans have become "womanish". Casca gives his news: the senators mean to make Caesar king tomorrow, everywhere except Italy.
7. Cassius says he knows where he will wear his dagger; he will free himself from bondage by suicide if need be. Casca agrees every slave can end his own captivity. Cassius: Caesar is only a wolf because Romans are sheep; "What trash is Rome". He then seems to check himself — perhaps he speaks "Before a willing bondman".
8. Casca gives his hand: he is "no fleering telltale"; he will go as far as anyone. Cassius reveals that other noble Romans are waiting for him at Pompey's Porch.
9. Cinna arrives, wishing aloud that Brutus could be won. Cassius gives him papers to leave in the praetor's chair, to throw in at Brutus's window, and to fix with wax on the statue of old Brutus. Decius Brutus and Trebonius are at the Porch; Metellus Cimber has gone to Cassius's house.
10. Cassius and Casca resolve to visit Brutus before dawn: "Three parts of him / Is ours already." Casca says Brutus's reputation will turn their offence into virtue. Cassius agrees; it is after midnight.
:::

### 5 Why it happens

::: level LEVEL 2 — What does it mean?
Ask 'why now?' and 'why these people?'. The scene happens because the clock has run out. Scene 2 gave Cassius a month; Casca's news gives him one night. The deadline forces three things at once: the recruitment of Casca (a man close to Caesar, with access), the delivery of the letters (the softening of Brutus), and the gathering at Pompey's Porch (the organisation of the strike).

Why is Casca recruitable tonight when he was merely sour in Scene 2? Because fear has broken him open. The man who found the Lupercal "mere foolery" is now shaking. Cassius's genius is to offer that fear a meaning and a target: the storm is not random terror, it is a warning about Caesar. Terror that has a name can be acted upon; terror without a name only paralyses.

Why does Cicero leave? Because the scene needs to clear the stage of the one man who would have asked awkward questions. Dramatically, Cicero's exit is the removal of scepticism so that persuasion can work.

Why the letters? Because Cassius's honest arguments in Scene 2 produced only "thus much show of fire" from Brutus. Cassius needs Brutus to believe that Rome is asking, not Cassius. So he manufactures Rome's voice.
:::

### 6 What each character wants

**Cicero** wants information and safety. His two speeches are a question about the omens and a question about Caesar's movements; his exit line — "This disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in" — is the voice of a man who intends to survive. He commits to nothing.

**Casca** wants, first, to be told what the horror means. Look at how he speaks to Cicero: "Are not you moved …?" — he needs another man to share his terror. Then, once Cassius has supplied a meaning, he wants a role and an assurance that he will not be exposed: "You speak to Casca, and to such a man / That is no fleering telltale." He wants to be trusted. (A 'fleering telltale' is a sneering informer.)

**Cassius** wants two men: Casca tonight and Brutus before dawn. Every speech he makes is shaped by that. He also wants — and this is subtler — to own the interpretation of the storm. If the omens mean 'the gods are angry with Rome', men stay indoors and pray; if they mean 'the gods warn us against Caesar', men act. Cassius fights for the second reading.

**Cinna** wants Brutus. His one substantial line is a wish: "O Cassius, if you could / But win the noble Brutus to our party—". He is the conspiracy's conscience speaking aloud: without Brutus, what we are doing does not feel right.

### 7 What changes during the scene

Learn to state changes as before → after pairs; examiners reward this because it shows you understand a scene as movement rather than as a list.

- Casca: terrified spectator → sworn conspirator who offers his hand.
- Cassius: the lone plotter of the Scene 2 soliloquy → the leader of an organised group with a meeting place, a messenger and a timetable.
- The conspiracy: a private grievance → a plan with a deadline (tomorrow, the Capitol) and a method (the letters).
- The storm: a terror to be fled (Cicero goes home) → a sign to be welcomed ("A very pleasing night to honest men") → a mirror of the deed itself ("Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible").
- Brutus: a friend Cassius hopes to persuade → a prize whose capture is calculated in fractions ("Three parts of him / Is ours already").
- Time: an unspecified evening → "after midnight", a few hours from the Ides.

### 8 What the audience learns

An audience learns things in a scene that the characters may not notice themselves learning. Here you learn, first, the date: the Soothsayer's "Beware the ides of March" was a warning without a calendar, and now Cicero's question pins the danger to tomorrow morning. You learn that a conspiracy already exists, that it has a headquarters (Pompey's Porch), members with names, and a courier. You learn that the plan Cassius announced alone in Scene 2 — letters "As if they came from several citizens" — is being carried out tonight. You learn that Casca is in and Cicero is out. You learn the rumour of the crown, which gives the plot its excuse and its urgency.

Less obviously, you learn how Cassius's mind works when he is not talking to Brutus. With Brutus he was gentle and flattering; with Casca he is brisk, insulting ("You are dull, Casca"), theatrical and quick to adapt. The audience is being taught that Cassius has a different voice for every listener — which is exactly what will make his handling of Brutus in Act 2 so dangerous.

### 9 What Shakespeare deliberately withholds

::: level LEVEL 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way?
Good dramatists are defined as much by what they leave out as by what they put in. Notice what this scene refuses to show you.

**Brutus.** The whole scene is aimed at him, and he never appears. Shakespeare saves Brutus's decision for the orchard scene that opens Act 2, where we watch him reason alone. Had Brutus walked on here, the storm would have swallowed his thinking; kept off stage, he becomes the silent centre the scene circles.

**Caesar.** Casca has just brought him home. We do not see how Caesar spends this night until Act 2 Scene 2, when Calpurnia's dream and the priests' sacrifices show us the same storm from inside his house. Shakespeare splits the night between two houses and shows you the conspirators' side first.

**The letters' words.** Cassius hands over "this paper" three times and never reads one aloud. You will hear a letter's contents only when Brutus reads it in his orchard. The withholding matters: we know the letters are forged before Brutus does, so when he takes them as the voice of Rome, we watch him deceived.

**Whether the rumour is true.** Casca says "they say". Nobody confirms that the Senate really means to crown Caesar. The conspiracy is built on a report, and the play never settles it.

**Cassius's sincerity.** Does he believe the storm is a warning about Caesar, or is that a line he has found for Casca? The text leaves it open (see the Professor's Insight later in this chapter).

**A judge.** There is no chorus, no honest bystander, no comment from outside. After Cicero leaves, you hear the conspiracy only from inside it. Shakespeare makes you do the judging.
:::

### 10 Important language

Casca spoke prose in Scene 2 — the language of gossip and sneering. In Scene 3 he speaks verse, and so does everyone else. Fear has raised his register. This is worth saying in an answer: Shakespeare signals a change of mood partly through a change of form.

Learn these words; the scene turns on them.

| Word | Meaning in the scene |
|---|---|
| "prodigies" / "portentous" | wonders that warn of the future / carrying an omen |
| "construe" | interpret; read a meaning into |
| "conjointly" | all together, at the same time |
| "unbracèd" | with doublet unfastened; shirt open |
| "thunder-stone" | the thunderbolt, imagined as a stone hurled from the sky |
| "thews" | muscles, bodily strength |
| "sufferance" | patient endurance; putting up with something |
| "bondman" | a slave; one in bondage |
| "factious" | active in forming a faction, a party |
| "redress" | remedy, setting right |
| "incorporate" | joined into one body with; made a member |
| "hie" | hurry |
| "conceited" | conceived, expressed (nothing to do with vanity) |
| "offal" | refuse, waste scraps |
| "fleering" | sneering, mocking |

Two features of Cassius's language deserve a name. The first is **anaphora** — the repetition of an opening word: "Why all these fires, why all these gliding ghosts, / Why birds and beasts from quality and kind, / Why old men, fools, and children calculate". The hammering 'why' makes his interpretation sound like an inevitable answer to an obvious question. The second is speaking of himself in the third person, which rhetoricians call **illeism**: "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius." That habit belongs, in this play, to Caesar ("for always I am Caesar"). When Cassius borrows it, he is — consciously or not — measuring himself against the man he hates.

### 11 Important images

The scene's images run in five families, and the best answers show how they interlock.

**Fire.** A "tempest dropping fire"; a hand that burned "Like twenty torches joined"; "Men all in fire"; "all these fires"; a "mighty fire" begun "with weak straws"; Rome as base matter that serves "to illuminate / So vile a thing as Caesar"; the night "Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible". Fire is the storm, the omen, the anger and the deed.

**Beasts.** The "lion" at the Capitol becomes Cassius's roaring Caesar and then the lion that would not exist "were not Romans hinds" (female deer). Caesar is a "wolf", Romans are "sheep". The "bird of night" is the owl. Beasts out of their places — a lion in the street, an owl at noon — mean a world out of order.

**Bondage.** "yoke", "bondage", "bondman", "captivity", "worldly bars", "stony tower", "walls of beaten brass", "airless dungeon", "strong links of iron". The republican case is made as the imagery of prison.

**The body.** Cassius bares his "bosom"; the lightning opens "The breast of heaven"; Casca offers "My hand" and "this foot of mine"; Cassius wears a "dagger". Politics is done with bodies here, as it will be on the Ides.

**Alchemy.** Rome is "base matter" (the alchemist's raw metal); Brutus's face is "richest alchemy" that turns "offence" into "virtue". Both images are about transformation, and both, read carefully, concede that something base is being dressed up.

### 12 Literary devices

Only the devices genuinely at work here, each with its anchor:

- **Pathetic fallacy** (a term coined by Ruskin in the nineteenth century, used loosely for nature that seems to share human feeling): the "strange impatience of the heavens"; the "scolding winds"; the "ambitious ocean". Careful: Shakespeare's characters believe the correspondence is real, not a fallacy.
- **Foreshadowing:** "opens graves"; "this dagger"; "Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible".
- **Dramatic irony:** "A very pleasing night to honest men".
- **Simile:** "Like twenty torches joined"; "like richest alchemy"; "Shakes like a thing unfirm".
- **Metaphor:** wolf and sheep; lion and hinds; fire and straws; "Our yoke".
- **Personification:** "the scolding winds"; "Th' ambitious ocean"; "But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?" (which is also **apostrophe**, direct address to an abstraction).
- **Anaphora:** the "Why …" series; "Therein, you gods … Therein, you gods".
- **Antithesis and paradox:** "you make the weak most strong".
- **Rhetorical question:** "Who ever knew the heavens menace so?"; "And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then?"
- **Euphemism:** life that "Never lacks power to dismiss itself" — suicide named without the word.
- **Hyperbole:** "a hundred ghastly women"; "all the sway of earth".
- **Illeism:** "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius".
- **Synecdoche:** "My hand"; "this foot of mine" — the part standing for the whole man's commitment.
- **Tricolon with repetition:** "Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible".

### 13 Themes

The scene is a junction where several of the play's great themes meet. **Fate and free will:** Cassius, who told Brutus that "Men at some time are masters of their fates", now reads the heavens for signs. **Superstition and interpretation:** three men, three readings of one storm. **Appearance versus reality:** letters that appear to come from citizens; a storm that appears to be divine anger; a conspiracy that will appear virtuous once Brutus's face is on it. **Liberty and bondage:** Cassius's suicide speech turns the republican slogan into a personal creed. **Honour:** the conspiracy claims it and, in the same breath, forges evidence. **Manliness and Roman virtue:** "womanish", "sparks of life / That should be in a Roman". **Fear as a political instrument:** Cassius converts Casca's fear into loyalty. **Order and disorder:** the storm as the sky's version of what is about to happen in the state.

### 14 Historical context

::: history What is Plutarch's and what is Shakespeare's
Shakespeare's source was Sir Thomas North's 1579 English translation of Plutarch. Plutarch's Life of Caesar records prodigies before the murder: lights in the sky, crashing noises at night, birds of ill omen settling in the Forum, men who seemed to be walking about on fire, and a slave whose hand poured out flame yet was unharmed when it stopped. Casca's burning slave and "Men all in fire" therefore come straight from the source, as does his owl in the marketplace (Plutarch's 'birds of omen' in the Forum). The lion "Against the Capitol" is not in Plutarch's list of omens so far as can be verified; most editors treat it as Shakespeare's own addition — and an Elizabethan audience knew lions as the exotic beasts kept in the menagerie at the Tower of London. Plutarch also records the rumour Casca reports: that the Senate meant to name Caesar king of the provinces outside Italy, wearing a diadem by land and sea. The bare-chested Cassius, the suicide speech, and the alchemy of Brutus's reputation are Shakespeare's invention.

Two further facts sharpen the scene. Cassius was, historically, an Epicurean — a follower of a philosophy that denied that the gods send omens — which makes his omen-reading here doubly interesting. And Brutus was urban praetor for 44 BC, which is why Cinna can leave a letter "in the Praetor's chair": that chair was Brutus's official seat. Ancient sources say Cassius resented receiving the lesser praetorship; Shakespeare never mentions this, but it lies behind the historical Cassius's bitterness.
:::

The Elizabethan side of the context — why a London audience in 1599 would have taken a storm of omens seriously — is treated in the section 'Why the storm is not simply bad weather' below.

### 15 Foreshadowing

This scene is the most forward-looking in the act. Every important image points at something that will happen.

- The storm continues into Act 2 Scene 1 (Brutus reads by the light of the flashes) and Act 2 Scene 2, where Calpurnia reports omens of her own and begs Caesar to stay at home. The night begun here does not end until Caesar is dead.
- "opens graves" — Cassius's picture of a Caesar-like night that opens graves anticipates the dead who will not stay buried in this play: Caesar's Ghost visits Brutus in Act 4.
- Casca's drawn sword, unsheathed all night, prepares us for the fact — from Plutarch, and kept by Shakespeare — that Casca strikes the first blow on the Ides.
- "I know where I will wear this dagger then; / Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius." At Philippi in Act 5 Cassius has himself killed by his own servant with his own sword. The line is fulfilled to the letter.
- Cinna carrying the letters is the conspirator Cinna; in Act 3 Scene 3 a mob tears apart Cinna the poet purely because of his name. A scene about how men "construe things after their fashion" plants the seed of the play's most terrible misreading.
- Cicero's exit foreshadows his exclusion from the conspiracy in Act 2 Scene 1 and, further off, his death in the proscriptions reported in Act 4.
- "Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible" is a three-word forecast of the assassination and the civil war.
- "Pompey's Porch" and "Pompey's Theatre" prepare the moment in Act 3 Scene 1 when Caesar falls at the base of Pompey's statue.

### 16 Dramatic irony

Dramatic irony is the gap between what the audience knows and what a character knows. The scene is thick with it.

The audience heard Cassius, alone, plan the forged letters; Cinna and Casca handle them as ordinary papers, and Brutus will receive them as the voice of Rome. Casca trembles because the gods "by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us" — and the audience, remembering the Soothsayer, suspects the heralds are for Caesar, whom Casca himself will be the first to strike. Cicero reasons calmly that omens mean whatever men make of them, while the audience has already been told the date of the danger. Cassius calls the night "very pleasing … to honest men" while planning a killing. He boasts that he can "shake off" tyranny by suicide "at pleasure", and anyone who knows Roman history knows he will. Most piercingly, he counts Brutus as three parts won, a possession to be secured — and the audience will watch Brutus, once won, take over the conspiracy and make the decisions (sparing Antony, refusing Cicero, allowing the funeral speech) that destroy it.

### 17 Character development

**Casca** is the scene's great change. In Scene 2 he was the man who "after his sour fashion" mocked everything — the crown, the crowd, Cicero's Greek. Brutus remembered him as "quick mettle when he went to school" and Cassius promised he would be quick again "in execution / Of any bold or noble enterprise". Scene 3 makes good on that. The cynic's armour turns out to have been thin; underneath is a frightened man who wants a cause. By the end he is not only sworn but eloquent: the "richest alchemy" speech is the most intelligent thing anyone says about Brutus in Act 1.

**Cassius** develops from analyst to leader. In Scene 2 he diagnosed the disease (Caesar's greatness, Rome's servility); in Scene 3 he is administering the cure — recruiting, organising, dispatching. We also see him take a risk and check himself ("But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?"), which shows that the cool manipulator can be carried away by his own passion.

**Cicero** does not develop; he is a fixed point of reason against which the others are measured, and then he is removed.

**Cinna** is earnest and practical, the conspirator who says out loud what the others feel: they need Brutus.

### 18 Scene connections

::: level LEVEL 4 — How does it connect to the whole play?
Think of Scene 3 as the hinge of the play's first movement. Behind it stand the two daylight scenes; ahead of it lies a single night and day that runs, without a break in dramatic time, from here to the murder of Cinna the poet.

Backwards: Flavius feared that Caesar would "keep us all in servile fearfulness" (Scene 1) — Cassius now names that servility "bondage" and "yoke". Cassius told Brutus that many were "groaning underneath this age's yoke" (Scene 2) — the yoke returns as "Our yoke and sufferance". Cassius's soliloquy promised writings "wherein obscurely / Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at" — here they are, in Cinna's hands. Casca's sneer that Cicero "spoke Greek" is answered by Cicero speaking, in plain Latin-Roman English, the wisest line of the act. Caesar said Cassius "thinks too much" — Scene 3 shows the thinking turned into action.

Forwards: the storm rolls straight into Brutus's orchard and Caesar's bedroom; the letters are read; the conspirators from Pompey's Porch arrive at Brutus's door; the question of Cicero is raised and refused; Casca's sword falls first; Cinna's name kills a poet; Cassius's dagger turns on Cassius. Everything the scene sets in motion completes itself.
:::

### 20 ICSE exam relevance

Let me be exact about the syllabus, because vagueness here wastes your time. **EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE.** For the ICSE Class X board examination the prescribed portion of *Julius Caesar* is Acts III, IV and V. Act 1 — this scene included — is the Class IX portion, examined in school through reference-to-context and extract questions. So you will not be handed an extract from Scene 3 in the board paper. But the board paper leans on Act 1 constantly: it assumes you know who the conspirators are and why they conspired, what the storm and omens were, why Casca strikes first, who Cinna the conspirator is when a different Cinna is torn apart, why Cicero is absent, and what Cassius means when his fate at Philippi echoes his old words. In the 2025 paper the Section B drama questions were extract-based, worth 16 marks each, with five sub-questions marked [3], [3], [3], [3], [4]; the sub-questions typically ask what has just happened, what a phrase means, why a speaker says something, what is revealed about character, what happens next, and for a short evaluation. Act 1 knowledge is what lets you answer 'what has just happened' and 'why' with authority when the extract is from Act 3, 4 or 5.

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
Three uses, in ascending order of value.

**(a) Class IX extract questions on this scene.** Expect an extract from Casca's prodigies, Cicero's reply, Cassius's bared bosom, the suicide speech, or the letters. You will be asked who is speaking to whom and where; what the prodigies were; what Cicero means by construing "after their fashion"; why Cassius says the night is pleasing to honest men; what Cinna is told to do with the papers; and what happens next. Answer from the text, in your own words, then quote a phrase.

**(b) Context for Act 3–5 extracts.** When an Act 3 extract shows Casca striking first, add one sentence: 'Casca, who in Act 1 Scene 3 kept his sword drawn all night and swore to go as far as any man, is fittingly the first to strike.' Such sentences earn the marks reserved for 'understanding of the whole play'.

**(c) Character and theme questions.** The scene supplies the clearest evidence in Act 1 for Cassius as manipulator, for the theme of superstition, and for the honour problem of the forged letters. Use it as your first example, then move to Acts 2–5.

## WHY THE STORM IS NOT SIMPLY BAD WEATHER

A weak reader treats the storm as scenery. A strong reader sees that it does three separate jobs at once — dramatic, symbolic and structural — and can name each.

### The dramatic function: atmosphere, pace, night

Remember the Globe: an open roof, an afternoon performance, spectators standing in daylight. Shakespeare had drums and probably rolled cannonballs for thunder, and fireworks for lightning, but the darkness itself could only be spoken into existence. So the first job of the storm language is to build night and terror out of nothing — "what night is this!", "this fearful night", "This disturbèd sky". Every character who enters remarks on the weather, and that repetition is not carelessness: it keeps repainting the darkness for an audience standing in the sun.

The storm also sets the pace. Look at the first exchange: Cicero asks two quick questions; Casca answers in a torrent. Then the dialogue tightens into short, breathless lines — "Who's there?" / "A Roman." / "Casca, by your voice." Nobody has time. The storm is a clock as well as a sky.

Finally, the storm provides cover. Conspiracies belong to night, and this one needs empty streets: "For now, this fearful night, / There is no stir or walking in the streets". The weather that terrifies Casca is, for Cassius, a gift of privacy — and the play makes the point aloud when the thunder answers the suicide speech (the Folio marks it: thunder still). The storm is almost a fifth speaker.

### The symbolic function: disorder, divine anger, civil strife in heaven

The storm is a picture of disorder. Casca's phrase is the key: "Either there is a civil strife in heaven, / Or else the world, too saucy with the gods, / Incenses them to send destruction." He offers two readings, and both are political. A 'civil strife' among the gods projects the coming Roman civil war on to the sky. A world "too saucy with the gods" — insolent, presuming above its station — points at a Rome in which a man is being turned into a god; Cassius has already sneered in Scene 2 that Caesar "Is now become a god". Either way, the sky is saying what the characters are about to do.

The symbol is also a test. Put four men under the same storm and each reveals himself: Casca fears and reports, Cicero reasons and goes home, Cinna hurries and wonders, Cassius bares his chest and boasts. The weather is a device for showing character under pressure — which is exactly how Shakespeare will use it again in *King Lear*, where a king's mind and a storm break together.

### The structural function: bridging the month

Structurally the storm is a hinge. Scenes 1 and 2 were one public day; Scene 3 begins the one long night that runs — without a break in dramatic time — through Brutus's orchard, Caesar's house, the streets at dawn, the Capitol, the Forum and the killing of Cinna the poet. The month between the Lupercal and the Ides is simply skipped, and the storm covers the join: an audience swept into thunder does not stop to ask what day it is. Cicero's question then quietly supplies the date.

The storm also carries across the act break. The thunder that begins here is still flashing when Brutus reads his letter in Act 2 Scene 1 and when Calpurnia describes her night in Act 2 Scene 2. So Scene 3 is not the end of something; it is the opening of the night that will kill Caesar.

### The storm: thunder, lightning, "a tempest dropping fire"

Now the opening passage, line by line. This is the speech that makes the night.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** Are not you moved, when all the sway of earth / Shakes like a thing unfirm? … But never till tonight, never till now, / Did I go through a tempest dropping fire. / Either there is a civil strife in heaven, / Or else the world, too saucy with the gods, / Incenses them to send destruction.

**Simple English:** Aren't you shaken, when the whole solid, ordered earth trembles like something loose? I have seen storms split oaks and seen the sea rear up to the clouds — but never until tonight have I walked through a storm that rained fire. Either the gods are at war with one another, or the world has grown so insolent towards them that it has provoked them to destroy it.

**Difficult words:** "moved" — emotionally shaken, not physically; "sway" — the ordered motion and government of the earth (the same root as 'hold sway'); "unfirm" — unsteady; "rived" (in the lines between) — split apart; "knotty" — gnarled; "exalted" — raised up; "saucy" — insolent, presumptuous; "Incenses" — enrages.

**Literal meaning:** The earth itself seems to shake; this storm is unlike any Casca has known because it drops fire (lightning so continuous it seems to rain); its cause must be supernatural — either quarrelling gods or gods angered by human pride.

**Subtext:** The opening is a question, not a statement — "Are not you moved …?" Casca needs Cicero to share his fear; a frightened man wants company. The whole speech is an appeal for confirmation.

**Hidden meaning:** "civil strife in heaven" quietly transfers Rome's future on to the sky. The gods are imagined as a Roman state at war with itself. And a world "too saucy with the gods" is a world in which a man has grown god-like — the very complaint Cassius has been making about Caesar.

**Character psychology:** This is the man who called the Lupercal "mere foolery". The cynic's pose has been stripped off by fear. Notice the hyperbole — "all the sway of earth" — a frightened mind sees everything as total.

**Political meaning:** Casca's two alternatives are two political diagnoses. 'Strife among the great' is one Roman disease; 'a presumptuous man over-reaching' is the other. He cannot decide which the storm means, and that indecision is exactly the gap that Cassius will fill.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Romans took prodigies (prodigia) seriously as official business: unnatural events were reported to the Senate and expiated by priests, because they showed the gods' goodwill had been broken. Plutarch lists prodigies before Caesar's death. Shakespeare's Elizabethan audience had its own habit of reading storms and comets as warnings to the state.

**Literary devices:** Simile ("Shakes like a thing unfirm"); personification ("the scolding winds", "Th' ambitious ocean"); repetition for emphasis ("never till tonight, never till now"); hyperbole ("all the sway of earth"); the balanced either/or structure that offers two interpretations at once.

**Effect:** The audience, standing in daylight, is plunged into a night of fire in seven lines. The word "tempest" will echo through the scene ("this fearful night", "this dreadful night"), and "fire" will run through it like a fuse.

**Dramatic significance:** The scene opens at its highest pitch of fear. Everything Cassius does afterwards depends on the terror this speech establishes.

**Foreshadowing:** "civil strife" is the whole second half of the play in two words. "Destruction" is what the storm announces and what the conspirators will deliver.

**Scene connection:** In Scene 2 Cassius mocked Caesar because "this god did shake". Now the earth shakes. The word links the frail body of the man-god to the shaking world around him.

**ICSE keywords:** omen, portent, pathetic fallacy, hyperbole, disorder, foreshadowing, divine anger.

**Exam application:** If asked why Shakespeare opens the scene with Casca rather than Cassius, write: 'By giving the terror to Casca — the sneerer of Scene 2 — Shakespeare shows how completely the storm has unsettled Rome; his phrase "a civil strife in heaven" turns the weather into a forecast of the civil war to come.'

### Political disorder and natural disorder: the idea of correspondence

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION, with historical grounding.** Many Elizabethans inherited a way of thinking in which the universe was a set of matching levels: the heavens, the state and the individual human being were built on the same pattern, so that disorder in one showed itself in the others. A king was to his kingdom what the sun was to the sky and reason to the soul. When this ordered chain was broken — by rebellion, usurpation or murder — nature itself was expected to protest. The official Tudor homilies read in churches taught obedience partly on this ground: the whole creation was arranged in degrees and ranks, and to disturb them was to disturb creation. The scholar E. M. W. Tillyard made this 'world picture' famous in the twentieth century; later critics have rightly warned that not every Elizabethan believed it and that the plays often question it. But as a shared vocabulary it was available to everyone in the Globe, and Shakespeare uses it repeatedly: the night of Duncan's murder in *Macbeth* brings an owl that kills a falcon and horses that eat one another; Lear's madness is played out in a storm.

Apply this to Scene 3 with care. The storm does not prove that the play believes in correspondence; it shows the characters using the belief. Casca is a straightforward believer. Cicero is a sceptic who says the belief tells you about the believer. Cassius uses the belief as a lever. And in Act 2 Scene 2 Calpurnia will use it in the opposite direction, arguing that the heavens are announcing Caesar's death, not his tyranny. The correspondence between sky and state is real inside the play — but its meaning is fought over, which is why the storm is a battlefield rather than a signpost.

::: insight Why the storm mirrors political instability — and why the mirror is cracked
School guides say the storm 'reflects the disorder in Rome'. True, but stop there and you miss what Shakespeare is actually doing. Look at what the storm is made to mean within a single night. Cassius tells Casca that the night is like a man — "Most like this dreadful night, / That thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars". In his reading, Caesar *is* the storm: the monstrous growth that has thrown the state out of its "ordinance". Yet in Act 2 Scene 2 Calpurnia will treat the same storm as heaven's announcement of Caesar's death; there Caesar is the storm's victim, not its likeness. One sky; two opposite politics.

That is the point. A stable state has stable meanings: everyone knows what a crown or a comet signifies. An unstable state is one in which even the sky can be read for either side. So the storm mirrors instability not only by being violent but by being *interpretable* — by lending itself to Casca's terror, Cicero's doubt, Cassius's ambition and Calpurnia's dread in turn. The disorder in Rome is, at bottom, a disorder of interpretation: nobody agrees any longer what Caesar is (god? tyrant? friend? "a dreamer"?), and so nobody agrees what the heavens are saying about him. Cassius's own phrase gives the game away: heaven sends warnings "Unto some monstrous state". 'State' means both a condition and *the* State — and the monstrous thing may be tyranny, or the murder that will cure it. Shakespeare lets the word carry both.
:::

## THE SUPERNATURAL SIGNS

### The prodigies one by one

Cicero asks, "Why, saw you anything more wonderful?" ('wonderful' here means 'astonishing', not 'delightful'). Casca's answer is a catalogue of four prodigies. Learn them as a set; extract questions ask for them, and each carries a meaning.

| Prodigy | What Casca reports | Source | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|---|
| The slave's burning hand | "A common slave" held up his left hand, which burned "Like twenty torches joined" yet stayed "unscorched" | Plutarch | Fire that does not consume: nature's first law suspended. A slave — the lowest man — made a vessel of divine fire |
| The lion "Against the Capitol" | It "glazed upon me and went surly by / Without annoying me" | Not in Plutarch; apparently Shakespeare's addition | The king of beasts loose at the seat of the state, staring and unhurried — an image many readers connect with Caesar |
| The "hundred ghastly women" | Huddled "Upon a heap", "Transformèd with their fear", they "swore they saw / Men all in fire walk up and down the streets" | Plutarch (men on fire) | Mass terror; fear that transforms those who feel it — a preview of what fear will do to the crowd |
| "the bird of night" at noon | An owl sat "Even at noonday upon the marketplace, / Hooting and shrieking" | Plutarch's birds of omen in the Forum | Time inverted: the night bird in the noon sun, in the very marketplace where Caesar swooned in Scene 2 |

Three details repay attention. First, the lion's strange calm: "glazed" (the Folger reading; other editions print 'glared') means it looked with a fixed, glassy stare and went "surly" — sullenly — past. Casca is not attacked; he is ignored, as a lesser man is ignored by a great one. Second, the women's testimony is hedged — they "swore they saw" — so even inside Casca's report the evidence is second-hand; the play is careful about who saw what. Third, "ghastly" and "Transformèd with their fear" make fear itself the agent of change: the storm does not only frighten people, it alters them. Keep this for Level 4: fear is what transforms the Roman crowd into a mob in Act 3.

::: textnote 'glazed' or 'glared'
The First Folio reads 'glaz'd'. The Folger text keeps "glazed" (stared glassily); the Globe-edition text behind many school editions prints 'glared'. Either is acceptable in an answer; if your edition says 'glared', quote it as your edition has it and do not lose sleep over the variant.
:::

### The conclusion of the catalogue, line by line

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** When these prodigies / Do so conjointly meet, let not men say / 'These are their reasons, they are natural,' / For I believe they are portentous things / Unto the climate that they point upon.

**Simple English:** When so many wonders happen together like this, nobody should say 'there are natural explanations for them'. I believe they are omens for the country they are aimed at.

**Difficult words:** "prodigies" — unnatural wonders that warn; "conjointly" — all together; "reasons" — causes, explanations; "portentous" — full of omen, ominous; "climate" — region, country (not 'weather'); "point upon" — are aimed at.

**Literal meaning:** One marvel might be a coincidence; four at once cannot be. Therefore they are signs, and signs are directed at a particular place — Rome.

**Subtext:** Casca is arguing with an imagined rationalist before Cicero has spoken — "let not men say". He knows the sceptical reply is coming and tries to block it. The believer is already on the defensive.

**Hidden meaning:** The word "climate" reveals the political reading buried inside the religious one. Omens 'point at' a country; the country in question is a Rome about to crown a king. Even Casca half-knows what the storm is about.

**Character psychology:** He reasons from accumulation — 'so many things together cannot be chance'. It is the logic of a frightened but not stupid man; he is trying to make his terror respectable.

**Political meaning:** The debate 'natural causes or divine warning?' is not academic. If the omens are natural, the state is not in question; if they are portents, something in the state is wrong and must be put right. To accept the omens is already to accept that Rome needs redress.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Roman religion regarded the simultaneous occurrence of prodigies as a matter for the Senate and the priestly colleges; Plutarch and later Roman poets (Virgil, Ovid) all record clusters of portents around Caesar's death. The Elizabethan pamphlet press did the same with English storms and monstrous births. Casca's reasoning would have sounded orthodox to both audiences.

**Literary devices:** An anticipated objection answered in advance (a rhetorical move); the embedded quotation of an imagined opponent; the abstract diction of "portentous things" against the homely "they are natural".

**Effect:** The catalogue ends not on an image but on an argument, which invites Cicero's reply. Shakespeare turns a list of horrors into a debate about interpretation.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the cue for the scene's central intellectual moment — Cicero's answer. Without Casca's insistence, Cicero's scepticism would have nothing to bite on.

**Foreshadowing:** The question 'natural or portentous?' returns in Caesar's house in Act 2 Scene 2, where Caesar first dismisses the omens and is then persuaded by Calpurnia, before Decius Brutus reinterprets her dream and leads him out. The play keeps re-staging this argument until it kills its subject.

**Scene connection:** Casca in Scene 2 refused to interpret anything — Cicero's Greek "was Greek to me"; the crown business was "mere foolery". Here he insists on interpretation. The mocker has become a reader of signs.

**ICSE keywords:** omen, portent, superstition, interpretation, rationalism, foreshadowing.

**Exam application:** On the theme of superstition: 'Casca's refusal to accept natural explanations — "let not men say / 'These are their reasons, they are natural'" — sets up the play's running argument about whether the heavens speak, an argument Caesar himself will lose on the Ides.'

### Three readings of one storm

The scene stages three responses to the same sky. Learn them as a triangle; it is the most useful single structure in the scene.

| | Casca | Cicero | Cassius |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the storm is | The gods' anger or the gods' war; "portentous things" | A "strange-disposèd time" about which men will believe what suits them | Heaven's warning "Unto some monstrous state" — and a portrait of Caesar |
| What one should do | "fear and tremble" | Go home: "This disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in" | Walk in it, bare-chested; act |
| Type of mind | Believer, frightened | Sceptic, prudent | Opportunist, defiant |
| Fate or free will? | The gods decide | Men decide what the gods mean | Men decide — and use the gods to recruit |
| Later echo | Casca strikes first at the Capitol | Excluded from the plot; killed in the proscriptions | Half-believes in omens by Act 5; dies by his own sword |

### Casca: terror against the cynicism of Scene 2

Put Casca's two scenes side by side and you have a small masterclass in how Shakespeare builds a minor character. In Scene 2 he is all prose, all sneer: the crowd are "tag-rag people" with "sweaty nightcaps" and "stinking breath"; Caesar's swoon was "mere foolery"; he would not laugh "for fear of opening my lips and receiving the bad air". His weapons are contempt and distance; he watches Rome as if it were a bad play.

In Scene 3 the distance collapses. He is "breathless", he stares, his sword is out, he speaks verse, and his first words beg another man to share his fear. When Cassius boasts of walking in the lightning, Casca answers with an appeal to piety that would have astonished his Scene 2 self: "It is the part of men to fear and tremble / When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us." ('Tokens' are signs; 'heralds' are messengers who announce what is coming; to 'astonish' is to stun.) The cynic turns out to have been a believer all along; the mockery was a way of not looking.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Why does Shakespeare give the terror to Casca? Partly for contrast: fear is more frightening in a man who mocked. Partly because Casca is the recruit, and a frightened man is the easiest man to recruit — he wants meaning, and Cassius sells meaning. And partly because Casca is Caesar's man. In Scene 2 it was Casca who cried "Peace, ho! Caesar speaks"; tonight Cicero asks him "Brought you Caesar home?" He has walked Caesar to his door and then, in the same night, given his hand to Caesar's killers. The betrayal is from inside the household, and the play wants you to feel that. Remember, too, Cassius's promise in Scene 2 that Casca is quick "in execution / Of any bold or noble enterprise, / However he puts on this tardy form" — 'execution' is a grim pun the play will pay off when Casca strikes first.

## CICERO: THE SCEPTICAL RATIONALIST

Cicero speaks four times in the whole play, all of them here. Two of his speeches are questions; one is a warning; one is a goodnight. Yet what he says is, in my judgement, one of the keys to the whole play.

### The line, taught line by line

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cicero:** Indeed, it is a strange-disposèd time. / But men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves. / Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?

**Simple English:** Yes, these are strange, unsettled times. But people interpret events to suit their own way of thinking, and their interpretations may have nothing to do with what the events really mean. Is Caesar coming to the Capitol tomorrow?

**Difficult words:** "strange-disposèd" — strangely arranged, abnormally inclined; "construe" — interpret, explain; "after their fashion" — in their own way, according to their own bent; "Clean from" — entirely away from; "purpose" — real meaning, intention.

**Literal meaning:** Cicero neither denies the prodigies nor accepts Casca's reading of them. He grants that the time is strange and then makes a general point: interpretation reveals the interpreter. Then he changes the subject to practical business.

**Subtext:** The change of subject is the point. Cicero's real question is not about the sky but about Caesar's movements. The rationalist has one eye on the weather and both eyes on politics.

**Hidden meaning:** The line is a warning that the play's characters will ignore. Casca construes the storm as divine wrath; Cassius construes it as an indictment of Caesar; Calpurnia will construe it as Caesar's death; Decius Brutus will construe her dream into a compliment; Brutus will construe forged letters as the voice of Rome; the mob will construe a poet's name as a conspirator's. Every catastrophe in the play is a construing "Clean from the purpose of the things themselves".

**Character psychology:** Cicero is calm, dry and detached. He does not argue with Casca's fear; he steps around it. Notice "Indeed": he agrees before he disagrees, the orator's courtesy.

**Political meaning:** Cicero's scepticism is politically neutral, which in a crisis is itself a position. He will not read the heavens for a party. That is why the conspirators, in Act 2 Scene 1, will consider him and reject him; he is not a man who follows.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** The real Cicero wrote a treatise on divination that examines omens, dreams and prophecy with a sceptic's eye, so Shakespeare's portrait — whether or not he knew that work directly — fits the man. He was not part of the conspiracy, though he approved of it afterwards, and he was killed on the triumvirs' orders in December 43 BC. In Scene 2 Casca reported that at the Lupercal Cicero "spoke Greek": the man of learning who talks over the heads of the crowd.

**Literary devices:** Aphorism (a compact general truth); antithesis between "their fashion" and "the purpose of the things themselves"; the abrupt shift of register from philosophy to a plain question — a kind of bathos that is dramatically deliberate.

**Effect:** For a moment the temperature drops. Amid fire and terror one man speaks quietly and sensibly, and the audience is invited to stand where he stands — outside the interpretations, watching men construe.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the play's statement of its own epistemology (its theory of how we know things). It is also the only cool head in the scene, and Shakespeare removes it at once: "Good night then, Casca." The moment reason leaves, Cassius enters.

**Foreshadowing:** Every misreading in the play, from Brutus's letter to Cinna the poet's death to Cassius's misreading of the battle at Philippi, is foreshadowed by this line.

**Scene connection:** Brutus used the same verb in Scene 2 — "Nor construe any further my neglect" — asking Cassius not to over-interpret his coldness. Cassius did exactly that. The play's characters keep asking not to be construed and keep construing others.

**ICSE keywords:** scepticism, rationalism, interpretation, appearance versus reality, dramatic irony, aphorism.

**Exam application:** In a theme answer on appearance and reality or on superstition: 'Cicero's warning that "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves" is proved true within minutes, when Cassius construes the same storm as a portrait of Caesar; the line is the play's own comment on how its characters misread signs, letters, speeches and even names.'

### The question that fixes the date

"Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" does quiet, essential work. Until this moment the play has had a warning without a date; the Soothsayer said "Beware the ides of March" and Caesar called him "a dreamer". Now a bystander's practical question tells the audience that tomorrow is the day and the Capitol is the place. Casca's answer — Caesar "did bid Antonius / Send word to you he would be there tomorrow" — adds two more facts: Caesar is still using Antony as his messenger and manager, and Caesar still counts Cicero among the senators he expects to see. From this line onward every speech in the scene is spoken under a deadline. **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Shakespeare relocates the killing: historically Caesar died in the Curia of Pompey, part of Pompey's theatre complex, not on the Capitol. The play's "Capitol" is a poet's Rome, where the seat of the state and the scene of the killing are one.

::: insight Why Cicero is given the sceptic's line and then removed
Shakespeare had a famous man at his disposal — the greatest orator Rome produced — and used him for forty lines and a single idea. Why waste Cicero so?

Because the idea needs a voice the audience will trust and the plot cannot use. Trust first: Cicero's authority makes "men may construe things after their fashion" sound like wisdom rather than cynicism; from Casca it would be fear, from Cassius a tactic. But the plot cannot use a sceptic. Conspiracies run on conviction, and the scene's whole business — turning a storm into a mandate — depends on nobody asking Cicero's question. So Shakespeare has him ask it once, lets it hang in the air, and sends him indoors: "This disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in." The rational man goes home; the men who stay out in the storm make history.

There is a second, sadder reason. In Act 2 Scene 1 the conspirators will discuss recruiting Cicero and Brutus will veto him on the grounds that he will never follow anything other men have begun — a compliment disguised as a rejection. In Act 4 we learn he has been killed in the proscriptions. Cicero's arc in this play is: speak the truest line, be excluded by the good men, be murdered by the bad ones. That is Shakespeare's judgement on what happens to independent reason in a time of faction, and it is delivered almost entirely through absence. When you write about the storm, do not forget the man who refused to walk in it.
:::


## CASSIUS IN THE STORM

Cicero goes in; Cassius comes out. The stage direction is simple, but the substitution is the hinge of the scene: the man who would not walk in the sky is replaced by the man who has been walking in it on purpose. Everything Cassius says from here to Cinna's entrance is one continuous act of persuasion, and the best way to study it is to watch his tactics change as Casca changes.

### "A very pleasing night to honest men": the entrance

The first exchange is in fragments — "Who's there?" / "A Roman." / "Casca, by your voice." Casca does not give his name in the dark; he gives his nationality, and the word will be thrown back at him within a minute ("those sparks of life / That should be in a Roman you do want"). Cassius knows him by ear: "Your ear is good," says Casca — a small, telling compliment, because the whole scene turns on how well Cassius listens.

Then Casca asks the question the whole of Rome is asking — "what night is this!" — and Cassius gives the answer nobody else would give: "A very pleasing night to honest men." Hear the dramatic irony. Casca means 'what a terrible night'; Cassius hears 'what kind of night is this?' and answers as if it were a riddle. To the frightened it is terror; to "honest men" — the conspirators' word for themselves, which the play will test to destruction — it is pleasing, because the streets are empty and the sky seems to agree with them. When Casca presses — "Who ever knew the heavens menace so?" — Cassius answers with a line that quietly moves the cause of the storm from the sky to the city: "Those that have known the Earth so full of faults." The heavens threaten because the earth deserves it. In two lines he has turned a weather report into a political judgement, and he has not yet mentioned Caesar.

### Baring his bosom to "the thunder-stone"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** And thus unbracèd, Casca, as you see, / Have bared my bosom to the thunder-stone; / And when the cross blue lightning seemed to open / The breast of heaven, I did present myself / Even in the aim and very flash of it.

**Simple English:** And with my doublet undone, Casca, as you can see, I have bared my chest to the thunderbolt; and when the forked blue lightning seemed to split open the sky, I stood exactly where it was aimed, right in the flash.

**Difficult words:** "unbracèd" — with the fastenings of the doublet (the close-fitting Elizabethan jacket) undone; "bosom" — chest, breast; "thunder-stone" — the thunderbolt, which popular belief imagined as a stone hurled from the clouds; "cross" — forked, zigzag; "the breast of heaven" — the sky imagined as a body that the lightning cuts open; "present myself" — place myself deliberately; "aim" — the target line of the bolt.

**Literal meaning:** Cassius has spent the storm walking the streets half-dressed, deliberately standing where the lightning struck, and he has not been harmed.

**Subtext:** This is a performance for an audience of one. "as you see" makes Casca look; "I did present myself" makes him picture it. Cassius is showing, before he argues, that he is not afraid — and that a man who is not afraid of heaven need not be afraid of Caesar.

**Hidden meaning:** A bared chest offered to a blow is, in this play, the gesture of a man inviting death on his own terms. In Scene 2 Casca reported that Caesar "plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut". Cassius now makes the same gesture to the sky. Two rivals, two bared breasts: Caesar's a piece of crowd-theatre, Cassius's a piece of recruitment-theatre. And there is a grim symmetry ahead — a bosom bared tonight, daggers in a body tomorrow.

**Character psychology:** The hunger for risk that Caesar diagnosed — "He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous." — here takes physical form. Cassius needs to prove to himself, as much as to Casca, that fear does not govern him. There is also vanity in it: he has made himself the hero of a story and is telling it.

**Political meaning:** To stand in the lightning is to deny that the gods are on Caesar's side. If heaven wanted to punish the enemies of its favourite, here was its chance; it did not take it. Cassius's unburnt body is offered as evidence that the storm's anger is aimed elsewhere.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** The real Cassius followed the philosophy of Epicurus, which taught that the gods take no interest in human affairs and that thunder has natural causes. A historically minded reader therefore sees a man acting out his philosophy — defying signs he does not believe in. Shakespeare never names the philosophy, but Plutarch's Cassius is an Epicurean and the play's Cassius behaves like one here. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Whether Shakespeare intended the allusion is uncertain; what is certain is the stage image of a bare-chested man under a stormy sky, which an Elizabethan audience would have found both heroic and impious.

**Literary devices:** Personification and bodily metaphor — the lightning opens "The breast of heaven" as Cassius opens his own breast, so sky and man mirror each other; the sharp visual adjective "cross blue"; the deliberate self-dramatisation of "I did present myself".

**Effect:** The audience, who have just watched Casca shake, now see his opposite. The contrast is the point: fear and defiance under the same sky.

**Dramatic significance:** This speech announces Cassius's reading of the storm before he states it. His body has already argued that the heavens are not against him.

**Foreshadowing:** The man who offers his chest to the lightning will, at Philippi, offer it to his own sword. Defiance of death is Cassius's constant; it ends by consuming him.

**Scene connection:** In Scene 2 Cassius proved his courage with a story — the swim in the Tiber, with Caesar crying "Help me, Cassius, or I sink!" Here he proves it with a live demonstration. Both times the lesson is the same: Cassius is braver than the god.

**ICSE keywords:** defiance, hubris, dramatic irony, foreshadowing, characterisation through action, impiety.

**Exam application:** On Cassius's character: 'Cassius's boast that he has "bared my bosom to the thunder-stone" shows a man who treats even the heavens as a rival to be outfaced; the gesture both proves his courage to Casca and reveals the recklessness that will end with his own sword at Philippi.'

### Reinterpreting the omens: "a man / Most like this dreadful night"

Casca's reply to the boast is pious horror: "But wherefore did you so much tempt the heavens? / It is the part of men to fear and tremble / When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us." ('Wherefore' means 'why'; to 'tempt' the heavens is to provoke them; 'the part of men' is men's proper role.) This is the moment Cassius has been waiting for. Casca has stated the orthodox view — omens are heaven's messengers; men should tremble — and Cassius does not deny it. He accepts that the omens are messages and then changes the address on the envelope.

He begins with an insult: "You are dull, Casca, and those sparks of life / That should be in a Roman you do want, / Or else you use not." Casca "want[s]" (lacks) the Roman fire, or has it and does not use it; the word "sparks" picks up the fire imagery and turns it inward — the real fire should be in the man, not in the sky. Then comes the catalogue of "Why …" questions already discussed under Important language, and finally the claim that "heaven hath infused them with these spirits / To make them instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state." Now the portrait:

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Now could I, Casca, name to thee a man / Most like this dreadful night, / That thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars / As doth the lion in the Capitol; / A man no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action, yet prodigious grown, / And fearful, as these strange eruptions are.

**Simple English:** I could name you a man, Casca, who is exactly like this terrible night — who thunders, flashes lightning, opens graves and roars like the lion at the Capitol; a man no stronger than you or me in what he can personally do, yet who has grown into something monstrous and frightening, just like these strange outbreaks in nature.

**Difficult words:** "name to thee" — name for you; "Most like" — most resembling; "opens graves" — splits the earth so the dead are exposed (an earthquake image); "personal action" — individual physical capacity; "prodigious" — monstrous, portent-like (the adjective of "prodigies"); "fearful" — causing fear (not 'afraid'); "eruptions" — violent outbreaks.

**Literal meaning:** Caesar — unnamed — is compared to the storm: as the night has grown unnaturally violent, so an ordinary man has grown unnaturally great.

**Subtext:** Cassius does not say the name. He makes Casca say it — "'Tis Caesar that you mean, is it not, Cassius?" — and then refuses to confirm: "Let it be who it is." A recruiter who makes the recruit speak the treason first has protected himself and committed the other man. It is the same technique Cassius used on Brutus, drawing out "I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king" before saying anything of his own.

**Hidden meaning:** "A man no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action" is the Scene 2 argument in miniature: "I was born free as Caesar". The storm image adds something new, though. A storm is not wicked; it is simply out of proportion. Cassius's deepest charge against Caesar is not vice but scale — a man has become a weather system.

**Character psychology:** Notice how quickly Cassius moves from mocking Casca's fear to exploiting it. He does not want Casca unafraid; he wants Casca's fear pointed at Caesar. The insult ("dull") and the flattery of being trusted with a secret ("Now could I … name to thee") work together, as a hard word followed by a confidence often does.

**Political meaning:** By making Caesar the storm, Cassius makes the removal of Caesar the restoration of calm. The political killing is reframed as a return to natural order — which is exactly how Brutus will later justify it to himself.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Caesar was dictator for life by February 44 BC and was being voted extraordinary honours by a Senate that had largely stopped resisting him; a contemporary could reasonably describe his power as having grown out of all proportion to the old offices. The lion "in the Capitol" refers back to Casca's own prodigy; Cassius is turning Casca's evidence into Cassius's argument.

**Literary devices:** Extended simile shading into metaphor (Caesar as the night that "thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars"); asyndeton in the four verbs, which gives the line a pounding rhythm; antithesis between "no mightier … In personal action" and "prodigious grown"; the deliberate withholding of the name, which is a kind of periphrasis.

**Effect:** The storm, which a minute ago was simply frightening, is now 'about' something. The audience experiences the same conversion that Casca does — chaos acquires a cause.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the central act of interpretation in the scene — the moment Cicero's warning is proved true. Men "construe things after their fashion", and here is the construing, performed in front of us, by a master.

**Foreshadowing:** "opens graves" anticipates Caesar's Ghost in Act 4 and, nearer at hand, Calpurnia's report in Act 2 Scene 2 of graves yielding up their dead. Shakespeare uses the same prodigy on both sides of the argument.

**Scene connection:** The "lion in the Capitol" carries Casca's prodigy forward and prepares Cassius's later "He were no lion, were not Romans hinds." The lion is Caesar in both places, but in the second the blame has shifted from the lion to the deer.

**ICSE keywords:** extended simile, interpretation, manipulation, Caesar's greatness, natural order, foreshadowing.

**Exam application:** On the theme of superstition and manipulation: 'Cassius never denies that the omens are heaven's warnings; he simply redirects them, calling Caesar a man "Most like this dreadful night" so that Casca's terror of the sky becomes terror of Caesar. The storm thus serves not fate but rhetoric.'

::: insight Why Cassius, who says men master their fates, now reads omens
In Scene 2 Cassius gave the play its most famous statement of free will: "Men at some time are masters of their fates. / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings." A month later he stands in a thunderstorm telling Casca that "heaven hath infused" the beasts and the fires "with these spirits / To make them instruments of fear and warning". Has he changed his mind?

Read the two speeches as tactics and the contradiction disappears. Brutus is a philosopher who respects reason; to him Cassius talks reason — stars do not rule us, we do. Casca is a frightened believer who has just said that it is "the part of men to fear and tremble"; to him Cassius talks omens. Cassius does not believe or disbelieve the storm; he uses it. That is why he can call the night "very pleasing" in one breath and a divine warning in the next. He is the opposite of Casca, who believes what he says, and of Cicero, who says what he believes. Cassius says what will work.

But Shakespeare does not leave it there, and this is the detail a school guide misses. Twice in the scene Cassius's control slips: once when his bitterness carries him into "What trash is Rome" and he has to pull himself back ("But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?"), and once at the end when the sky becomes, for him too, a sign — "the complexion of the element / In favour's like the work we have in hand". The man who uses the omens begins, very slightly, to be used by them. By Act 5 he will confess that he has changed his mind about signs. The play's judgement is quietly made: nobody in a superstitious time stays an unbeliever by will alone, and the manipulator of fear is not immune to it. When you write about Cassius and fate, do not say he 'contradicts himself'. Say that his two speeches are two tools, and that the second tool begins, by the end of the scene, to turn in his hand.

### "Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Let it be who it is. For Romans now / Have thews and limbs like to their ancestors. / But, woe the while, our fathers' minds are dead, / And we are governed with our mothers' spirits. / Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish.

**Simple English:** Never mind who I mean. Romans today have muscles and bodies like their forefathers; but — alas for these times — the spirit of our fathers is dead, and we are ruled by the spirit of our mothers. The yoke we wear, and the patience with which we bear it, prove us unmanly.

**Difficult words:** "Let it be who it is" — leave the name unspoken; "thews" — sinews, muscular strength; "like to" — like; "woe the while" — alas for the age, grief for these times; "governed with" — ruled by; "spirits" — temperaments; "yoke" — the wooden bar laid on oxen; by metaphor, servitude; "sufferance" — patient endurance; "womanish" — effeminate, weak (in the Roman and Elizabethan sense of the word).

**Literal meaning:** The Romans have not degenerated physically; they have degenerated in spirit. Their willingness to be ruled is a failure of manliness.

**Subtext:** This is a challenge disguised as a lament. Cassius is not mourning Rome; he is daring Casca to prove he is his father's son. The 'we' is generous — Cassius includes himself in the shame — but the arrow is aimed at the listener.

**Hidden meaning:** The word "sufferance" is doing double work. It means 'endurance', but it also contains 'suffering'. To bear the yoke patiently is both to endure and to be hurt, and Cassius wants Casca to feel the hurt he has been numbing with cynicism.

**Character psychology:** Cassius thinks in terms of manhood and shame; it is his deepest register. In Scene 2 the proof of Caesar's unfitness was that he cried "As a sick girl"; here the proof of Rome's unfitness is that it is "womanish". For Cassius, politics is a contest of virility, and a Rome that submits has lost it. Keep this in mind when you judge him: it is a narrow and, to modern readers, an uncomfortable way of thinking, but it is coherent and it is his.

**Political meaning:** The republican ideal is here equated with ancestral masculinity: the "fathers" who expelled kings had "minds" that the sons have lost. This is Roman nostalgia of a kind the historical Cato and Cassius really felt — a belief that the Republic was a system for free men and that submitting to one man was a form of slavery.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Roman moralists habitually blamed political decline on a loss of ancestral virtue (the mos maiorum, the custom of the forefathers), and the Latin word virtus — 'manliness', 'excellence' — ties courage to maleness in exactly the way Cassius's speech does. An Elizabethan audience, ruled by a queen, might have heard the misogyny differently; the play does not endorse it, but gives it to the character to whom it belongs.

**Literary devices:** Antithesis (fathers' minds / mothers' spirits; thews and limbs / minds); the exclamatory interjection "woe the while"; metaphor ("yoke"); the compact summarising line "Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish", in which abstract nouns are made to deliver a verdict.

**Effect:** Shame is a stronger recruiting tool than fear, because fear makes men hide and shame makes them act. Casca, who a moment ago was defending the gods, now volunteers news that helps the plot. The speech has worked.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the turn. Up to here Casca has been resisting Cassius; from here he is feeding him. Watch for the speech after which a character changes sides — examiners love you to find it.

**Foreshadowing:** The language of manly firmness versus womanish softness returns when Portia, in Act 2 Scene 1, insists that she is more than an ordinary woman and wounds her own thigh to prove her constancy. Shakespeare lets a woman answer Cassius's slur.

**Scene connection:** "yoke" was Cassius's word in Scene 2 for the condition of Rome's nobles, "groaning underneath this age's yoke". He returns to it because it worked. Note also how Scene 2's "underlings" has become Scene 3's "womanish" — the same servility, a harsher name.

**ICSE keywords:** rhetoric, shame, manliness, republican nostalgia, antithesis, persuasion.

**Exam application:** On how Cassius persuades: 'Having failed to shame Casca out of his fear with the insult "You are dull", Cassius shames him more deeply with "Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish", converting political submission into a failure of manhood; it is after this speech that Casca begins to help rather than resist him.'

## CASCA'S NEWS, THE DAGGER AND THE OATH

The middle of the scene is a negotiation, though neither man calls it that. Casca offers information; Cassius offers a creed; Cassius risks a confession; Casca gives his hand. Read it as four moves.

### Casca's news: a king "In every place save here in Italy"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** Indeed, they say the Senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king, / And he shall wear his crown by sea and land / In every place save here in Italy.

**Simple English:** Yes — people are saying that tomorrow the senators intend to set Caesar up as a king, and that he will wear his crown everywhere, over sea and land, except here in Italy itself.

**Difficult words:** "Indeed" — truly, yes; "they say" — it is rumoured; "establish" — set up formally, by decree; "by sea and land" — throughout the empire; "save" — except.

**Literal meaning:** A rumour reports that the Senate will tomorrow grant Caesar the title of king over the provinces, with Italy alone excluded.

**Subtext:** Casca is offering this to Cassius as a gift — notice "Indeed", which agrees with the shaming speech just before. He has changed from arguing against Cassius to supplying him. The rumour is his way of saying 'I am with you' before he dares to say it in words.

**Hidden meaning:** The exemption of Italy is not a softening; it is the whole scandal. Romans could tolerate a king over foreigners; the title rex inside Italy was the one thing the Republic had been founded to forbid. So the proposal is a fig leaf — kingship everywhere that matters militarily, with a legal fiction at home. Cassius does not even bother to discuss the exemption; to him a crown anywhere is a crown.

**Character psychology:** Casca is a gossip by temperament (remember his Scene 2 report), and under stress he reverts to type: he knows what people are saying. But look at the shift in what he does with gossip. In Scene 2 he passed on news to amuse; here he passes it on to arm.

**Political meaning:** This is the play's deadline. Whatever the senators intend, the conspirators believe that tomorrow Caesar becomes a king — so the killing must be tomorrow. Everything that follows, including the hurried visit to Brutus before dawn, is governed by this one piece of hearsay.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Plutarch reports exactly this rumour — that a Sibylline prophecy was to be cited to the Senate saying that only a king could conquer the Parthians, and that Caesar would therefore be offered the title of king over the provinces outside Italy. Caesar was indeed preparing a Parthian campaign for 44 BC. Whether the Senate would actually have voted this on the Ides is unknown; the murder forestalled it, and historians disagree about how far the plan had gone. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** The play keeps it as "they say" and never confirms it.

**Literary devices:** The repeated 'they say' construction (reported speech as evidence); the pairing "by sea and land" as a merism — two opposites that together mean 'everywhere'; the ironic qualification "save here in Italy".

**Effect:** The audience feels the clock start. From here to the end of the scene every verb is in the future tense of urgency — "tomorrow", "ere day", "after midnight".

**Dramatic significance:** This is the information that converts a plot into an emergency. Without it the conspirators might have waited; with it, they cannot.

**Foreshadowing:** The crown returns on the Ides: Caesar will be killed on the day he is rumoured to have been about to receive it, and Antony will use the refused crown in the Forum as proof that Caesar was not ambitious. Both halves of the play's argument about kingship pass through this rumour.

**Scene connection:** Scene 2's crown was a coronet offered three times in the marketplace and refused; Scene 3's crown is a decree in the Senate. The theatre of refusal in public is about to become the fact of kingship in law — or so Casca believes.

**ICSE keywords:** rumour, kingship, republicanism, urgency, deadline, dramatic tension.

**Exam application:** On why the conspiracy acts when it does: 'Casca's report that the senators "Mean to establish Caesar as a king" the next day gives the plot its deadline; the conspirators move not because Caesar has become a tyrant but because they believe tomorrow he will, and Shakespeare pointedly leaves the rumour unconfirmed — "they say".'

### The suicide and liberty speech: "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** I know where I will wear this dagger then; / Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius. / Therein, you gods, you make the weak most strong; / Therein, you gods, you tyrants do defeat.

> **Cassius:** But life, being weary of these worldly bars, / Never lacks power to dismiss itself. / If I know this, know all the world besides, / That part of tyranny that I do bear / I can shake off at pleasure.

**Simple English:** Then I know where I will wear this dagger — in my own heart. Cassius will free Cassius from slavery. In that power, you gods, you make the weak strongest of all; in that power you defeat every tyrant. (No tower, no brass wall, no dungeon, no iron chain can imprison a strong spirit.) Life, when it is weary of these worldly prison-bars, never lacks the power to end itself. If I know this, let the whole world know it too: the share of tyranny that falls on me, I can throw off whenever I choose.

**Difficult words:** "wear this dagger" — a grim pun: a dagger is 'worn' at the belt, but he means he will wear it in his breast; "bondage" — slavery; "deliver" — set free; "Therein" — in that respect, by that means; "beaten brass" — hammered bronze; "retentive to" — able to hold in; "worldly bars" — the prison of earthly life; "dismiss itself" — release itself, end; "shake off" — throw off, as a yoke is shaken off.

**Literal meaning:** If Caesar is crowned, Cassius will kill himself rather than live as a subject. Suicide is the ultimate freedom: no prison can hold a man who is willing to die, and therefore no tyrant can truly enslave him.

**Subtext:** This sounds like despair but functions as a boast. Cassius is telling Casca that he is beyond fear, and therefore beyond Caesar's power to frighten. A man who holds his own death in his hand cannot be bought or threatened — the ideal recruiter's credential.

**Hidden meaning:** "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" is self-reference three times over: the speaker names himself twice and makes himself both rescuer and rescued. In Scene 2 he complained that Caesar's name was "sounded more than yours"; now he sounds his own name as Caesar sounds his. The liberator is already imitating the man he would destroy.

**Character psychology:** This is the Cassius of the lean and hungry look turned inward: proud, absolute, in love with the idea of mastery. Note the lack of middle ground — either Rome is free or Cassius dies. It is magnificent and it is dangerous, because a man who has decided he can always escape by death is a man with nothing to lose.

**Political meaning:** The speech makes liberty a personal, not a civic, possession. Earlier the republican case was about Rome; here it is about one man's refusal to be ruled. That is a narrowing, and the play notices it: Casca immediately broadens it again — "So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity" — and Cassius has to turn the thought from suicide to tyrannicide with "And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then?"

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** The idea that a free man can always escape tyranny by death was a commonplace of Stoic philosophy, and it had a recent Roman hero: Cato the Younger, Brutus's uncle and father-in-law, who killed himself at Utica in 46 BC rather than accept Caesar's pardon. The historical Cassius was an Epicurean, not a Stoic, but the sentiment was Roman property, not the possession of one school. Both Brutus and Cassius did in fact die by their own hands after Philippi in 42 BC. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** The euphemism for suicide, and the dignity given to it, reflect a Roman value an Elizabethan Christian audience would have found troubling; Shakespeare lets the Roman speak as a Roman.

**Literary devices:** Anaphora ("Therein, you gods … Therein, you gods"; "Nor … nor … Nor … nor"); paradox ("you make the weak most strong"); illeism ("Cassius … Cassius"); the accumulating catalogue of prison images; euphemism ("dismiss itself"); the pun on "wear"; the thunder that follows (the Folio's thunder still) as a stage-level answer to the speech.

**Effect:** The speech lifts the scene from gossip to creed. For a moment the conspiracy sounds like a philosophy rather than a plot — which is what it needs to sound like to win men such as Casca and, tomorrow, Brutus.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the first time in the play that a dagger is named. It appears not as a weapon against Caesar but as a weapon against the self; Shakespeare introduces the blade through the idea of suicide, and the play will end with that idea fulfilled.

**Foreshadowing:** Exact and terrible. At Philippi Cassius will have his servant run him through with the very sword he used against Caesar, and he will speak of freeing himself from the world. "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" is a promise he keeps.

**Scene connection:** The prison imagery answers Scene 2's Colossus: there "we petty men / Walk under his huge legs"; here no "stony tower" nor "walls of beaten brass" can hold the spirit. The giant has become a gaoler, and the answer to the gaoler is the dagger.

**ICSE keywords:** liberty, bondage, suicide, Stoicism (as context), paradox, anaphora, foreshadowing, illeism.

**Exam application:** When an Act 5 extract shows Cassius's death: 'His end fulfils his own words in Act 1 Scene 3 — "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" — where he claimed that no tyranny could bind a man willing to die; the dagger he first named as the instrument of his freedom becomes exactly that.'

### Wolf and sheep, lion and hinds, fire and straws, "What trash is Rome"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Poor man, I know he would not be a wolf / But that he sees the Romans are but sheep; / He were no lion, were not Romans hinds. / Those that with haste will make a mighty fire / Begin it with weak straws. What trash is Rome, / What rubbish, and what offal when it serves / For the base matter to illuminate / So vile a thing as Caesar!

**Simple English:** Poor Caesar — I know he would never be a wolf if he did not see that the Romans are only sheep; he could not be a lion if the Romans were not timid deer. People who want to build a great fire quickly start it with flimsy straws. What rubbish Rome is — what refuse, what waste — when it serves only as the cheap fuel that lights up something as worthless as Caesar!

**Difficult words:** "Poor man" — contemptuous pity; "But that" — except that; "hinds" — female deer, timid creatures; "with haste" — quickly; "straws" — dry stalks, kindling; "trash", "rubbish", "offal" — three words for waste, the last the inedible scraps of a slaughtered animal; "base matter" — the alchemist's term for cheap, impure material; "illuminate" — light up, make glorious; "vile" — worthless, low.

**Literal meaning:** Caesar's tyranny is not his own achievement but Rome's gift: predators exist because prey consents. A great blaze is started with trivial fuel; Rome has become that fuel, burned up to make Caesar shine.

**Subtext:** Cassius is shifting the blame from Caesar to the Romans — and therefore to Casca, who is a Roman standing in front of him. 'You made him' is a far more painful accusation than 'he is wicked', and it carries an implied remedy: unmake him.

**Hidden meaning:** The pity in "Poor man" is real as well as ironic. Cassius's wolf is not evil; it is a wolf because sheep are available. This is the most honest moment of his political analysis — tyranny as a relationship, not a character — and it is also the moment his contempt for Rome breaks through. He despises Caesar; he despises the Romans more.

**Character psychology:** The three nouns — "trash", "rubbish", "offal" — are too many. A controlled speaker would stop at one. Cassius is being carried by his own disgust, and in the next line he knows it: "But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?" The passage shows the manipulator losing control of his instrument, which is his own eloquence.

**Political meaning:** The crowd of Scene 1, who worshipped Pompey and now worship Caesar, and the "rabblement" of Scene 2, who clapped and hissed as Caesar pleased them, are the sheep. Cassius's republicanism has no love for the people; it is an aristocrat's liberty, the freedom of nobles not to have a master. Keep this when you read Brutus in the Forum promising to die for Rome's liberty: the conspiracy's idea of Rome never included the Romans.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** The historical conspirators were senators and ex-Pompeians, and ancient sources are clear that the urban populace was largely pro-Caesar; after the murder, the plebs turned on the assassins. Cassius's scorn for "sheep" reflects a real gap between the senatorial idea of libertas and the people's experience of Caesar as a benefactor. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** The alchemical colouring of "base matter" and "illuminate" belongs to Shakespeare's London, where alchemy was a living (and much-mocked) pursuit; Casca will pick the same vocabulary up at the end of the scene.

**Literary devices:** Paired animal metaphors (wolf/sheep, lion/hinds) in parallel lines; the proverbial-sounding fire-and-straws image; climactic tricolon ("trash … rubbish … offal"); alchemical metaphor ("base matter … illuminate"); exclamatio ("What trash is Rome"); apostrophe to grief.

**Effect:** The fire that began the scene as lightning is now Caesar's glory, fed by Rome's degradation. The audience feels the imagery of the storm being pulled, image by image, into the argument for killing.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the speech that nearly goes too far — and its excess is what prompts the risk Cassius takes next. Shakespeare shows that recruitment is not a smooth operation but a thing that can slip.

**Foreshadowing:** The Romans as sheep and the alchemy of "base matter" both point at Act 3 Scene 2, where Antony will light the mob as straws and the people who were "sheep" become wolves who tear Cinna the poet apart. Cassius is right that Rome is kindling; he is wrong about who will hold the match.

**Scene connection:** The lion was Casca's prodigy and Cassius's image for Caesar; now it needs "hinds" to exist. Within forty lines one animal has been a sign from heaven, a portrait of a tyrant and a proof that the tyrant is the people's fault. Trace that lion in an answer and you will have shown how the scene's imagery thinks.

**ICSE keywords:** metaphor, tricolon, animal imagery, contempt for the mob, aristocratic republicanism, fire imagery, alchemy.

**Exam application:** On Cassius's attitude to the people: 'Unlike Brutus, Cassius has no faith in the Romans; his wolf-and-sheep metaphor and his outburst "What trash is Rome" blame tyranny on the cowardice of the ruled, so that the conspiracy, from its first night, despises the very people in whose name it will act.'

### "Before a willing bondman": the risk test of Casca

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me? I perhaps speak this / Before a willing bondman; then, I know / My answer must be made. But I am armed, / And dangers are to me indifferent.

**Simple English:** But oh, my grief — where have you carried me? Perhaps I have said all this in front of a man who is content to be a slave; if so, I know I shall have to answer for it. But I am armed, and I do not care about danger.

**Difficult words:** "grief" — passionate sorrow, here almost 'indignation'; "led me" — carried me away; "willing bondman" — a man who accepts slavery, a contented subject of Caesar — and therefore a potential informer; "My answer must be made" — I shall be called to account, I shall have to answer the charge; "armed" — carrying a weapon, and also morally prepared; "indifferent" — matters of no concern.

**Literal meaning:** Cassius appears to realise he has spoken treason to a man whose loyalty he has not tested. He acknowledges the danger of denunciation and declares himself ready to meet it.

**Subtext:** Is this a genuine lapse or a calculated move? **LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** The text supports both readings, and a strong answer says so. Read one way, Cassius's passion really has outrun his prudence and he is recovering his balance. Read the other way, the 'slip' is the oldest trick in recruitment: by saying 'perhaps you are not with me', he forces Casca to say 'I am'. The suspicion "willing bondman" is an insult Casca cannot let stand, and the boast "I am armed" tells him that betrayal would be dangerous.

**Hidden meaning:** "I am armed" is literally true — he wears the dagger he has just promised to turn on himself — and the double sense of 'armed' against informers and 'armed' against Caesar lets the threat hang in the air between the two men. Cassius has, in effect, told Casca: inform on me and you will find I am not easy to arrest.

**Character psychology:** Whichever reading you prefer, the speech shows a man who converts weakness into strength instantly. Exposed, he does not retreat; he raises the stakes. That reflex — attack when threatened — is Cassius all through the play, from his quarrel with Brutus at Sardis to his death.

**Political meaning:** Conspiracy is a world of informers. The word "telltale" in Casca's answer, and Cassius's fear of being denounced, remind the audience that under Caesar a free conversation about freedom is already a crime. Shakespeare's own London, with its network of informers and its laws against seditious words, knew this atmosphere well.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** The real conspiracy numbered some sixty men and was kept secret for weeks — a remarkable fact that ancient writers remark on. Plutarch stresses that the conspirators swore no formal oath and relied on mutual trust; Shakespeare's Brutus will make the same point in Act 2 Scene 1 when he refuses to let the conspirators swear. Casca's informal handclasp here is the play's version of that trust.

**Literary devices:** Apostrophe and personification ("O grief, / Where hast thou led me?"); dramatic pause marked by the broken line; understatement ("dangers are to me indifferent"); the loaded term "bondman", which makes acceptance of Caesar equal to slavery.

**Effect:** The tension rises for the first time inside the conversation itself. Until now the danger was the sky; now it is the other man. The audience waits to see which way Casca will jump.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the test that produces the oath. Shakespeare makes Casca commit himself under challenge rather than by invitation, so that his commitment looks like courage rather than compliance.

**Foreshadowing:** Cassius's readiness to be 'called to account' and his armed indifference to danger look ahead to the Capitol, where he will whisper to Brutus to be ready, and to the quarrel scene, where he will offer Brutus his dagger and his breast.

**Scene connection:** The "bondman" picks up the "bondage" of the suicide speech, and Casca's own "bondman" from his reply. The word has moved from 'slave of Caesar' to 'possible traitor to us' in a dozen lines — a sign that the conspiracy already has an inside and an outside.

**ICSE keywords:** risk, recruitment, informer, test of loyalty, calculated ambiguity, apostrophe.

**Exam application:** On Cassius's methods: 'Cassius's apparent lapse — "I perhaps speak this / Before a willing bondman" — may be genuine or feigned, but its effect is the same: challenged as a possible slave and informer, Casca must either betray or join, and he joins. Shakespeare shows recruitment working through pressure, not invitation.'

### Casca's oath: "no fleering telltale. Hold. My hand."

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** You speak to Casca, and to such a man / That is no fleering telltale. Hold. My hand. / Be factious for redress of all these griefs, / And I will set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest.

**Simple English:** You are talking to Casca — to a man who is no sneering informer. Here: take my hand. Form a party to put right all these wrongs, and I will go as far as the man who goes farthest.

**Difficult words:** "fleering" — grinning mockingly, sneering; "telltale" — informer, tattler; "Hold" — here, take this (an offer), with a secondary sense of 'wait, stop'; "factious" — active in forming a faction or party (not yet the purely negative modern sense); "redress" — remedy, setting right; "griefs" — grievances, wrongs; "set this foot of mine" — commit myself, go.

**Literal meaning:** Casca denies that he is an informer, offers his hand as a pledge, and commits himself to go to any length the conspiracy requires.

**Subtext:** "You speak to Casca" — he names himself as Cassius named himself; the two men are now speaking the same language of self-assertion. The word "fleering" is a confession: it describes exactly the man he was in Scene 2, the mocker. He is saying: I know what I have seemed; I am not that.

**Hidden meaning:** "Be factious" is a startling phrase from a man who has just been defending the gods and the proper fear of men. To be 'factious' is to divide the state; Casca asks for it as a remedy. The Republic's enemies were always called a faction; now its defenders ask to be one. The vocabulary of the play's politics has already been turned inside out.

**Character psychology:** The pledge is physical — "My hand", "this foot of mine" — because Casca's conversion is bodily, not intellectual. He has not been argued into the plot; he has been frightened, shamed and then challenged, and his body answers before his mind. "As who goes farthest" is a competitive promise: he will not be outdone. That competitiveness, under Cassius's shaming, is what has been recruited.

**Political meaning:** Casca attaches a condition: "for redress of all these griefs". The conspiracy is framed as a remedy for wrongs, not as murder. This is the formula every conspirator will use, and it is the formula that lets honourable men do what they are about to do.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Publius Servilius Casca Longus was indeed a member of the conspiracy and, by all ancient accounts, struck the first blow. Shakespeare therefore makes the man who will begin the killing also the first man we see being recruited — a neat compression that is his own choice, not Plutarch's, since Plutarch does not stage Casca's recruitment at all.

**Literary devices:** Synecdoche ("My hand", "this foot of mine" — parts of the body standing for the whole man's commitment); the broken, emphatic monosyllables "Hold. My hand."; the superlative construction "as far / As who goes farthest"; the euphemistic political phrase "redress of … griefs".

**Effect:** Relief, and a shiver. The audience has watched a man join a murder plot in a thunderstorm by shaking hands, and the ordinariness of the gesture is more disturbing than any speech.

**Dramatic significance:** The scene's first objective is achieved: Casca is in. From here Cassius can move from recruiting to organising.

**Foreshadowing:** The hand that is given here is the hand that will strike first at the Capitol. Casca's own earlier phrase for Caesar — he "offered them his throat to cut" — now acquires a terrible literalness: Casca will accept the offer.

**Scene connection:** Cassius in Scene 2 asked Brutus to "chew upon this"; Brutus promised to hear more later and committed nothing. Casca commits everything in five lines. Shakespeare sets the two recruitments side by side so that you feel the difference between a man who thinks and a man who is swept.

**ICSE keywords:** oath, pledge, synecdoche, conversion, faction, redress, foreshadowing.

**Exam application:** When an Act 3 extract shows the stabbing: 'Casca strikes first because Shakespeare has prepared us for it: in Act 1 Scene 3 he was the first man recruited, pledging "I will set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest". His blow at the Capitol is that oath kept.'

### "There's a bargain made": the conspiracy revealed

Cassius closes the deal in four words — "There's a bargain made" — and the word 'bargain' is worth pausing on. Casca has spoken of griefs and redress; Cassius answers in the language of trade. For him it is a transaction, and the thing bought is a man. Only now, with the hand given, does Cassius reveal what he withheld during the whole persuasion: "Now know you, Casca, I have moved already / Some certain of the noblest-minded Romans / To undergo with me an enterprise / Of honourable-dangerous consequence." Learn the order. First he tested Casca; only then did he tell him there were others. A recruiter who reveals the network before the recruit is committed has endangered the network. Cassius does it properly.

The phrase "honourable-dangerous" is a compound Shakespeare makes for the occasion, and it is the conspiracy in a single hyphen: the two adjectives are yoked because the conspirators cannot separate them, and the hyphen quietly asks whether they can be joined at all. Then the meeting place — "they stay for me / In Pompey's Porch" — and the storm's last transformation: "the complexion of the element / In favour's like the work we have in hand, / Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible." The sky's 'complexion' (its appearance, its colouring) resembles, 'in favour' (in face, in look), the deed to come. The night that was divine anger for Casca and a strange time for Cicero is now the conspiracy's own face reflected in the clouds. Cassius has finished with the storm as a tool and begun — almost without noticing — to see it as an omen for himself.

::: quote The storm becomes the deed
> **Cassius:** And the complexion of the element / In favour's like the work we have in hand, / Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible.

Three adjectives, two of them weighted by "Most", the middle one carrying the whole scene's fire. This is the line to quote if you are asked how the storm reflects the conspiracy: the conspirators themselves say that it does.
:::

## CINNA, THE CONSPIRACY AND THE FORGED LETTERS

The last third of the scene changes gear. Persuasion is over; logistics begin. The language becomes plain — names, places, errands, times — and that plainness is itself expressive: this is what a murder looks like the night before, when it is still a list of things to do.

### Cinna

Casca sees a figure first — "Stand close awhile, for here comes one in haste" ('stand close' means 'hide, keep still') — and his instinct is to conceal himself: the new conspirator has already acquired the reflexes of one. Cassius identifies the newcomer by his walk — "'Tis Cinna; I do know him by his gait. / He is a friend." — just as he identified Casca by his voice. In the dark, the conspirators know each other by body and sound; Shakespeare keeps reminding you that the stage is supposed to be black.

Cinna's first words are a mistake that tells you a great deal: "Who's that? Metellus Cimber?" He expects to meet a conspirator and names one, so the audience learns a new name and learns that the plot is large enough for its members to be mistaken for each other. Cassius corrects him with a phrase that formally enrols Casca: "No, it is Casca, one incorporate / To our attempts." To be 'incorporate' is to be made part of one body — the conspiracy is imagined as a corporation, a single organism of which Casca is now a limb. It is the political version of the handclasp.

Then Cinna, like everyone else, remarks on the weather — "What a fearful night is this! / There's two or three of us have seen strange sights." — and Cassius, impatient, cuts across him: "Am I not stayed for? Tell me." (Is nobody waiting for me?) He asks twice. The leader wants to know that his men are assembled; the omens no longer interest him. Finally Cinna says the thing the whole conspiracy is thinking: "Yes, you are. O Cassius, if you could / But win the noble Brutus to our party—". The dash is Shakespeare's: Cassius interrupts him. He does not need to be told.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Why give this wish to Cinna, the least important speaker in the scene? Because it is more powerful as a general feeling than as Cassius's private calculation. When the messenger blurts it out, you understand that the need for Brutus is not one man's strategy but the conspiracy's shared anxiety: they do not feel legitimate without him. Cinna's adjective matters too — "the noble Brutus". Nobody in Act 1 calls Cassius noble to his face.

::: history Two Cinnas, one name
**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The Cinna of this scene is Lucius Cornelius Cinna, praetor in 44 BC, a brother-in-law of Caesar and nonetheless a supporter of the assassins. The Cinna of Act 3 Scene 3 is Gaius Helvius Cinna, a poet and a friend of Caesar, whom the mob killed by mistake on the day of the funeral because he shared the conspirator's name. Plutarch tells the story of the poet's death; Shakespeare stages it. The scene you are studying introduces the first Cinna so that the second Cinna's death can land with full force two acts later: the man torn apart for a name is torn apart because of the man who carried the letters tonight. Keep the two distinct in any answer; confusing them is a common and costly error.
:::

### The conspiracy: Pompey's Porch and the names

By the end of the scene you can draw the conspiracy's organisation chart, and you should, because the Class X paper will assume you know who these men are when they appear at the Capitol.

| Name | What Scene 3 tells you | Role later in the play |
|---|---|---|
| Cassius | Organiser; has "moved already / Some certain of the noblest-minded Romans" | Co-leader with Brutus; dies at Philippi |
| Casca | Recruited tonight; "one incorporate / To our attempts" | Strikes the first blow at the Capitol |
| Cinna | Courier; carries the letters; wishes for Brutus | Among the assassins; his name kills the poet |
| Decius Brutus | Waiting at Pompey's Porch | Reinterprets Calpurnia's dream and brings Caesar to the Senate |
| Trebonius | Waiting at Pompey's Porch | Draws Antony away from the Capitol during the killing |
| Metellus Cimber | Has gone "To seek you at your house" | Presents the petition that gives the signal for the attack |
| Brutus | Absent; the object of every plan | Joins in Act 2; becomes the moral leader |

The headquarters is named three times — "In Pompey's Porch", "Repair to Pompey's Porch", "repair to Pompey's Theatre" — and the repetition is not carelessness. **HISTORICAL RECORD.** The Theatre of Pompey, built in 55 BC, was Rome's first permanent stone theatre; its great colonnaded portico ('porch') was a public walk, and the Senate-house attached to the complex, the Curia of Pompey, was where Caesar was actually killed on 15 March 44 BC. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** The play moves the killing to the Capitol, but it keeps Pompey's name on the conspirators' meeting place and, in Act 3, has Caesar fall at the foot of Pompey's statue. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** The effect is to make the conspiracy gather in the shadow of the man Caesar defeated. Marullus's reproach in Scene 1 — "Knew you not Pompey?" — is answered: these men did know Pompey, and they meet under his roof to avenge him. Whether that makes them patriots or merely the losing side of the civil war is one of the questions the play never settles.

Notice also what the meeting place says about class. Scene 1's commoners were in the street; Scene 2's crowd was in the marketplace; the conspirators meet in a colonnade attached to a theatre built by a great aristocrat. The geography of the act is a social map.

### The forged letters

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Be you content. Good Cinna, take this paper, / And look you lay it in the Praetor's chair, / Where Brutus may but find it; and throw this / In at his window; set this up with wax / Upon old Brutus' statue. All this done, / Repair to Pompey's Porch, where you shall find us.

**Simple English:** Don't worry. Good Cinna, take this paper and make sure you lay it on the praetor's official chair, where only Brutus will find it; throw this one in at his window; fix this one with wax on the statue of his ancestor, the old Brutus. When all that is done, go back to Pompey's Porch, where you will find us.

**Difficult words:** "Be you content" — be satisfied, set your mind at rest; "look you" — see to it that; "Praetor's chair" — the official seat of the praetor, the senior magistrate below consul (Brutus held the urban praetorship for 44 BC); "may but find it" — may be the only one to find it; "set … up with wax" — fix it with wax to the stone; "old Brutus" — Lucius Junius Brutus, who drove out the last king of Rome; "Repair" — go, return.

**Literal meaning:** Cassius hands over three papers and gives three delivery points — Brutus's official seat, his private house and his ancestor's public statue — then names the rendezvous.

**Subtext:** "Be you content" answers Cinna's wish for Brutus with the papers themselves: here is how we win him. Cassius is also displaying competence to his new recruit Casca, who stands watching. The brisk imperatives ("take", "lay", "throw", "set", "Repair") are a leader's voice.

**Hidden meaning:** The three places are three aspects of Brutus. The praetor's chair is his public office — Rome's magistrate is being asked to act. The window is his private self — the citizen at home is being disturbed. The statue of old Brutus is his ancestry — the descendant is being summoned by the founder of the Republic. Cassius has designed a campaign aimed at the whole man, and the audience knows, because they heard the Scene 2 soliloquy, that all three voices will be one voice: his.

**Character psychology:** Compare the soliloquy's plan — "In several hands, in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens" — with its execution. Cassius has done exactly what he said. He is a man whose plans become actions; that reliability is a virtue in a general and a danger in a plotter. Note also his certainty that Brutus will read anonymous paper as the voice of Rome: Cassius has studied his brother-in-law and knows that Brutus's weakness is his honour.

**Political meaning:** This is the moment the conspiracy stops being a group of men who agree and becomes an operation that deceives one of its own members. The letters are aimed not at Caesar but at Brutus. The first victim of the plot's dishonesty is the man who will give it its honour.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Plutarch records that Brutus's praetor's tribunal and the statue of his ancestor were covered with anonymous messages — 'Brutus, are you asleep?', 'You are no true Brutus' — urging him to act. Plutarch does not say Cassius wrote them, though he describes Cassius working on Brutus by other means. Shakespeare's decisive change is to make the letters Cassius's forgeries, announced in advance to the audience. The history offers an awakening of Brutus by the people; the play offers a manipulation of Brutus by his friend. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** The statue is "old Brutus' statue" — the ancestor Cassius invoked in Scene 2: "There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king."

**Literary devices:** Tricolon of commands (chair, window, statue), each beginning with a verb; the anaphora-like repetition of "this paper … this … this"; dramatic irony, since the audience alone knows the papers are forged; symbolic setting in the three locations.

**Effect:** The audience watches a deception being physically handed from one man to another. The papers are props, and props make abstractions concrete: 'manipulation' is now something you can see crossing the stage.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the execution of the plan announced in the Scene 2 soliloquy and the direct cause of the first event of Act 2, where Brutus reads one of these letters by the light of the storm. The two scenes are hinged on these papers.

**Foreshadowing:** Brutus will read a letter in his orchard and fill in its gaps himself, promising Rome that he will give it what it asks. The letters will then disappear from the play — nobody ever discovers the forgery — which is Shakespeare's quiet way of saying that the deception worked completely.

**Scene connection:** Scene 2 ended with the plan; Scene 3 ends with its delivery; Act 2 Scene 1 opens with its reception. Across the three the audience's knowledge stays one step ahead of Brutus's — the longest sustained dramatic irony in the first half of the play.

**ICSE keywords:** forgery, manipulation, dramatic irony, honour, deception, appearance versus reality, ancestry.

**Exam application:** When an Act 2 extract shows Brutus reading the letter: 'The letter Brutus takes as the voice of Rome is one of the papers Cassius gave Cinna in Act 1 Scene 3 to "lay … in the Praetor's chair" and "throw … / In at his window"; the audience, knowing it is forged, watches Brutus's honour being used against him.'

::: insight The honour problem of the forged letters
Brutus will say, in his orchard and in the Forum, that he acts for honour and for Rome. The conspiracy's whole claim to be something better than a murder rests on that word. Yet the night before Brutus joins, the conspiracy forges documents to deceive him. That is the honour problem, and it is not a small one.

Consider what the letters do. They do not lie to Caesar, the enemy; they lie to Brutus, the friend. They do not persuade him by argument — Cassius tried argument in Scene 2 and got only "thus much show of fire" — but by counterfeiting the one voice Brutus cannot refuse, the voice of the Roman people. The forger knows that Brutus would not kill Caesar for Cassius; he must think he is killing him for Rome. So the honourable man's honour is used as the handle by which he is lifted into a dishonourable act. Casca's image at the end of the scene — Brutus's face as "richest alchemy" that turns "offence" into "virtue" — is honest about the chemistry: the conspirators know their deed looks like offence, and they want Brutus's reputation to launder it.

Here is the point a school guide misses. Shakespeare never has anyone discover the forgery. Brutus goes to his grave believing Rome called him. The play does not punish the lie; it lets it work and lets the consequences unfold — the sparing of Antony, the funeral speech, the mob, the civil war — all of them flowing from decisions Brutus makes because he thinks he is the people's instrument. The honour problem is therefore not that the conspirators are hypocrites (though Cassius is, a little). It is that honour itself, in this play, is a thing that can be forged, and that the man most certain of his own is the man most easily deceived. When you write about Brutus's tragic flaw, this is where the evidence starts: not in Act 2, when he reasons himself into the murder, but here, in the wax and paper that make sure he will.
:::

### Conspiracy recruitment as a method

Step back from the particular speeches and look at the shape of what Cassius has done to Casca, because it is a method, and the same method is applied to Brutus across Scene 2 and Act 2. Being able to describe the method — rather than merely saying 'Cassius is manipulative' — is what separates a Level 3 answer from a Level 2 one.

```chain
Read the man → Casca is frightened, pious, proud of being 'a Roman'
↓
Show, don't tell → the bared bosom proves fearlessness before any argument is made
↓
Accept his premise, redirect its target → the omens ARE warnings — about Caesar
↓
Shame rather than frighten → 'dull', 'womanish': submission as a failure of manhood
↓
Let him name the enemy → "'Tis Caesar that you mean, is it not, Cassius?"
↓
Offer a creed, not a crime → liberty, the dagger, no prison can hold the spirit
↓
Blame the victim's people → wolf and sheep: tyranny is Rome's own fault, so Rome must act
↓
Test before you trust → "Before a willing bondman … But I am armed"
↓
Take the pledge physically → "Hold. My hand."
↓
Only then reveal the network → "Now know you, Casca, I have moved already …"
↓
Give him a task and a rendezvous → 'incorporate', Pompey's Porch, Brutus before dawn
```

Three features of the method deserve comment. First, Cassius never asks for anything directly. Casca volunteers his hand; Casca volunteers Caesar's name; Casca volunteers the rumour. A good recruiter makes the recruit feel that he has chosen. Second, the method is tailored. With Brutus in Scene 2 Cassius used mirrors, honour and the names ("'Brutus' and 'Caesar'"); with Casca he uses lightning, shame and the dagger. The constant is not the argument but the attention — Caesar was right that Cassius "looks / Quite through the deeds of men". Third, the method depends on secrecy about its own structure: Casca does not know that his fear has been managed, and Brutus will never know that the letters were forged. Recruitment, in this play, is persuasion that hides the persuader.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Shakespeare was writing in a decade when conspiracies against the English state were a lived reality and the government's own methods — informers, forged correspondence, entrapment — were widely known. The play does not make a topical allegory of this, and you should not claim that it does; but an audience of 1599 knew what a recruitment looked like, and would have recognised Cassius's craft as craft.

::: keyword Vocabulary for writing about recruitment and manipulation
- **Manipulation** — steering another person's choices while hiding the steering. Use it for Cassius, but always say *how* he manipulates.
- **Rhetoric** — the art of persuasion; name the devices (anaphora, rhetorical question, antithesis) when you use the word.
- **Dramatic irony** — the audience knows the letters are forged; Casca and Cinna do not; Brutus will not.
- **Appearance versus reality** — letters that appear to come from citizens; a storm that appears to be about Caesar; a plot that will appear virtuous.
- **Legitimacy** — the quality of being rightful; what the conspiracy lacks and what Brutus's name supplies.
- **Faction** — Casca's own word for the party he joins ("Be factious"); a term with a negative weight the conspirators try to reverse.
- **Redress** — the setting right of a grievance; the conspirators' preferred name for what they are doing.
- **Incorporate** — made one body with; Cassius's word for Casca's new status.
:::

## THE PRIZE: WHY THE CONSPIRACY NEEDS BRUTUS

The scene ends where it has been pointing all along — at Brutus's door. Cinna's errand is explained — "Is Decius Brutus and Trebonius there?" / "All but Metellus Cimber, and he's gone / To seek you at your house. Well, I will hie / And so bestow these papers as you bade me." — and Cassius sends him on ("That done, repair to Pompey's Theatre."). Then he turns to Casca, and the two last speeches of the scene state, more clearly than anywhere else in Act 1, what Brutus is for.

### "Three parts of him / Is ours already"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Come, Casca, you and I will yet ere day / See Brutus at his house. Three parts of him / Is ours already, and the man entire / Upon the next encounter yields him ours.

**Simple English:** Come, Casca — before daybreak you and I will go and see Brutus at his house. Three quarters of him is already ours, and at our next meeting the whole man will give himself over to us.

**Difficult words:** "yet ere day" — still before dawn; "Three parts" — three quarters, three parts out of four; "Is ours" — the singular verb with a plural subject is normal Elizabethan grammar; "the man entire" — the whole man; "encounter" — meeting; "yields him" — surrenders himself.

**Literal meaning:** Cassius judges that Brutus is nearly won and that one more conversation will finish the work.

**Subtext:** This is said to Casca, the new recruit, and it is partly reassurance: the man you are all worrying about is as good as ours. It is also Cassius's own confidence speaking — the analyst's satisfaction at a calculation coming right.

**Hidden meaning:** Listen to the vocabulary. Brutus is measured in fractions, like a cargo or a sum; he is "ours", a possession; he "yields", as a city yields to a siege. Cassius does not speak of persuading a friend but of completing an acquisition. The man who hates being Caesar's subject speaks of Brutus as something to be owned. The play lets you hear that without comment.

**Character psychology:** Cassius's confidence is both earned and misplaced. Earned, because his reading of Brutus's "honourable mettle" was accurate and the letters will work. Misplaced, because he imagines that a Brutus who yields will remain his. In fact the whole man, once he comes in, will take over, and the three quarters Cassius thinks he owns will outvote him on every decision that matters.

**Political meaning:** The arithmetic reveals how the conspiracy understands itself. Casca is a hand; Cinna is a courier; Brutus is three parts of a legitimacy that the rest of them cannot supply. The plot is a body searching for a head it can put a famous face on.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Plutarch says that the conspirators believed the deed required Brutus — that men would not accept the killing as just unless he were part of it — and that Cassius worked hard to bring him in, partly through the anonymous messages, partly in person. Shakespeare keeps the substance and compresses the courtship into a month and a night.

**Literary devices:** Metaphor of possession and fractions; the contrast between the mathematical "Three parts" and the humane "the man entire"; the military colouring of "yields".

**Effect:** The scene has built towards Brutus for ten minutes, and this line fixes the next scene's agenda: before dawn, Brutus's house. The audience knows where Act 2 will open.

**Dramatic significance:** It closes Scene 3's second objective. Casca is won; Brutus is scheduled. The conspiracy now has a timetable from midnight to the Ides.

**Foreshadowing:** "yields him ours" is the forecast; the reality in Act 2 Scene 1 is a Brutus who, within minutes of joining, overrules Cassius on the oath, on Cicero and on Antony. The man who was supposed to yield commands.

**Scene connection:** The soliloquy of Scene 2 said that Brutus's mettle "may be wrought / From that it is disposed" — may be worked, like metal, out of its natural shape. "Three parts" is the smith reporting progress. Then Casca, in the next speech, changes the metal-working image to a far richer one.

**ICSE keywords:** possession imagery, legitimacy, overconfidence, dramatic irony, foreshadowing.

**Exam application:** On the relationship between Brutus and Cassius: 'Cassius ends Act 1 Scene 3 counting Brutus as "Three parts … ours already", as if he were a prize to be collected; the irony is that once Brutus joins, it is he, not Cassius, who commands — the acquired man becomes the leader.'

### Casca's "richest alchemy" speech and Cassius's reply

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Casca:** O, he sits high in all the people's hearts, / And that which would appear offence in us / His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness.

> **Cassius:** Him and his worth and our great need of him / You have right well conceited. Let us go, / For it is after midnight, and ere day / We will awake him and be sure of him.

**Simple English:** Oh, he is held very high in all the people's affections; and what would look like a crime if we did it, his support — like the finest alchemy — will transform into virtue and worth. — Cassius: You have expressed him, his worth and our great need of him exactly right. Let's go: it is past midnight, and before daybreak we will wake him and make sure of him.

**Difficult words:** "sits high" — is placed high, is honoured; "offence" — crime, wrongdoing; "countenance" — his face, and also his support or approval (to 'countenance' a thing is to sanction it); "alchemy" — the medieval and Renaissance pursuit of turning base metals into gold; "richest" — most precious, most potent; "worthiness" — merit, honour; "conceited" — conceived, understood and expressed (not 'vain'); "be sure of him" — make certain of him, secure him.

**Literal meaning:** Casca says that Brutus's popularity will make the killing look virtuous. Cassius agrees that Casca has understood exactly why they need Brutus, and sets the time: before dawn.

**Subtext:** Casca's speech is an admission. "that which would appear offence in us" — he knows how the deed will look. The conspirators are not deceived about themselves; they are planning how to be seen. Cassius's reply, "our great need of him", is the frankest statement in Act 1 that the conspiracy cannot stand on its own.

**Hidden meaning:** Alchemy was both a hope and a byword for fraud. To the Elizabethans the alchemist was the man who promised gold from lead and usually produced nothing but smoke — Ben Jonson built a whole comedy on the swindle. So when Casca calls Brutus's face "richest alchemy", the image praises Brutus and, at the same time, quietly admits that the transformation is a kind of trick: lead will still be lead; it will only look like gold. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Whether Casca intends the second sense is doubtful; the play, I think, does. The word "appear" has already given it away: what alchemy changes here is appearance.

**Character psychology:** This is the most intelligent thing Casca says in the play, and it comes from the man who, in Scene 2, pretended not to understand Greek. The cynic's eye for how things look has found its true use: he can see how Rome will see the murder. Cassius's "right well conceited" is the teacher's praise to a pupil who has grasped the lesson — and perhaps a flicker of surprise.

**Political meaning:** Here is the conspiracy's theory of legitimacy in four lines: power to kill they have; the right to kill must be borrowed from a man "high in all the people's hearts". It is also the conspiracy's fatal miscalculation. Brutus's countenance will indeed turn the deed to virtue — for about an hour, in the Forum, until Antony speaks and the people's hearts, which Casca thought were fixed, turn.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD.** Brutus's reputation for integrity was real and was the conspirators' chief asset; Plutarch says the people trusted him and that his name lent the plot its appearance of justice. Shakespeare's 'alchemy' is his own metaphor, drawn from the London of 1599 where alchemical practice and alchemical fraud were both familiar.

**Literary devices:** Simile ("like richest alchemy"); metaphor of transformation (offence into virtue); the pun on "countenance" (face / sanction); antithesis of "offence" and "virtue"; the tricolon of Cassius's reply, "Him and his worth and our great need of him", which puts the man, his value and their lack in one line; the closing rhyme-like insistence of "ere day" / "be sure of him".

**Effect:** The scene ends on the word "him" — Brutus — spoken twice in two lines. The audience leaves the storm looking towards a house and a sleeping man.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the thematic key to the whole recruitment of Brutus and the reason the forged letters exist. It tells the audience how to understand everything the conspirators do to Brutus in Act 2: they are mining his reputation.

**Foreshadowing:** The alchemy works and then fails. Brutus's speech in the Forum turns the murder into virtue in the people's eyes; Antony's speech turns it back. Casca was right about Brutus's gold and wrong about how long the people would believe in it.

**Scene connection:** Cassius's "base matter" that illuminates "So vile a thing as Caesar" and Casca's "richest alchemy" that gilds the conspiracy are the same vocabulary used in opposite directions. Rome is base metal that makes Caesar shine; Brutus is the elixir that makes murder shine. The scene's two alchemies belong in one paragraph of any good essay.

**ICSE keywords:** alchemy, reputation, legitimacy, appearance versus reality, pun, antithesis, public opinion.

**Exam application:** On why the conspirators need Brutus: 'Casca puts it in a single simile — Brutus's "countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness" what would otherwise "appear offence" — and Cassius confirms "our great need of him". The conspiracy does not seek Brutus's judgement but his reputation, and that is why it is willing to deceive him to get it.'

::: revision ONE-MINUTE REVISION — Act 1 Scene 3
**5 must-remember events**
1. A night of thunder and "a tempest dropping fire": Casca, sword drawn, reports four prodigies to Cicero (burning hand, lion at the Capitol, women who saw men on fire, owl at noon).
2. Cicero warns that men construe things "after their fashion", fixes the date by asking whether Caesar comes to the Capitol tomorrow, and goes home.
3. Cassius, bare-chested in the lightning, reinterprets the storm as a warning against a man "Most like this dreadful night"; Casca reports the rumour that the senators will crown Caesar tomorrow.
4. Cassius vows suicide before slavery, blames Rome's sheep for Caesar's wolf, tests Casca ("Before a willing bondman"), and Casca gives his hand and his oath.
5. Cinna arrives; Cassius sends him with forged letters for Brutus's chair, window and ancestor's statue; the conspirators plan to visit Brutus before dawn.

**5 keywords** — portent (omen) · construe (interpret) · bondage / bondman · factious / redress · alchemy (transformation of appearance).

**3 quotations**
- "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves" (Cicero).
- "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" (Cassius).
- "His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness" (Casca, of Brutus).

**3 character insights**
- Casca: the sneer of Scene 2 was armour; under it is a frightened believer who is recruited through fear and shame and will strike first.
- Cassius: he does not believe or disbelieve the omens — he uses them; yet by the scene's end he reads the sky as his own omen, and his control twice slips.
- Cicero: the only cool head, given the truest line and removed before it can interfere — reason's fate in a time of faction.

**3 themes** — Superstition and interpretation (three readings of one storm) · Liberty versus bondage (the dagger as freedom) · Honour and deception (forged letters to win an honourable man).

**2 literary devices** — Extended simile/metaphor (Caesar as the storm; wolf and sheep) · Dramatic irony ("A very pleasing night to honest men"; the letters the audience knows are forged).

**2 historical points** — Plutarch supplies the prodigies, the rumour of kingship outside Italy and the anonymous messages to Brutus; Shakespeare adds the lion, makes the messages Cassius's forgeries, and moves the killing from the Curia of Pompey to the Capitol · The historical Cassius was an Epicurean who did not believe omens were divine messages — which colours his use of them here.

**3 connections** — Backwards: the letters execute the plan of Cassius's Scene 2 soliloquy · Forwards: the storm runs on into Brutus's orchard and Caesar's house (Act 2), and "opens graves" prepares Caesar's Ghost · Fulfilment: the dagger named here kills Cassius at Philippi; the hand given here strikes first at the Capitol.

**1 central idea** — The storm decides nothing; the interpreter does. Scene 3 is about how fear is given a meaning and a target, and how a conspiracy that calls itself honourable begins with a forgery.
