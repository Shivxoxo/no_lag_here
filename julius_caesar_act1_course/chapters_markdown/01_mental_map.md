# The Mental Map: What You Must Understand Before Scene 1

*Before you read a single line of Scene 1 you need a map. Students who struggle with Shakespeare rarely struggle because the language is impossible; they struggle because they arrive at the first speech not knowing who is talking, what Rome is, why anyone is angry, or what is at stake. This chapter supplies that missing map: what kind of play this is, the questions Shakespeare is asking, the people you must recognise on sight, the political situation, the history Act 1 silently assumes, and three graded summaries of the whole act. Read it slowly, twice if necessary. Every later chapter in this book stands on it.*

## A. What Julius Caesar is about

Ask a hundred students what *Julius Caesar* is about and most will answer: a Roman general is murdered by his friends. That is true, and it is almost useless. It describes an event, not a play. A play is built around a question, and the question this play asks has never been settled by any society in history: **what should good people do when one man grows too powerful for the system that made him?**

Here is the situation in plain terms. Rome in 44 BC is a republic. For nearly five centuries it has been governed not by a king but by elected officers and a Senate, and Romans are brought up to loathe the very word *rex* (king). Yet after years of civil war one man, Julius Caesar, has defeated every rival and now holds power that no republican office was ever designed to contain. He is adored by the common people, feared by a section of the nobility, and surrounded by men who cannot decide whether he is Rome's saviour or its destroyer. Act 1 is the act in which that indecision hardens into a plan.

What Shakespeare does with this situation is the real subject. He is not interested in simply staging a famous killing; the killing does not arrive until Act 3. He is interested in how a decision to kill is *made*: how one man, Cassius, persuades another, Brutus, that murder can be honourable; how a crowd can be turned in an afternoon; how omens, rumours, forged letters and flattery all become instruments of persuasion; how the public face of a great man differs from the private body that shakes with fever and cannot hear in one ear. Act 1 contains no violence at all. It contains talk, and the talk *is* the drama, because in this play words are the weapons that go before daggers.

So when you read Act 1, do not sit waiting for something to 'happen'. Something is happening in every line: Rome is being argued into a murder. The plot of Act 1 can be told in a paragraph (two officials scold a crowd; Caesar brushes aside a warning; Cassius works on Brutus; Casca reports a crown refused; a storm rages; conspirators gather). The *meaning* of Act 1 needs a book. That is why this one exists.

One more orientation point. The whole play compresses about two and a half years of history, from the Lupercalia of February 44 BC to the battle of Philippi in October 42 BC, into five acts. Act 1 covers the shortest stretch of that time and yet carries the heaviest load, because every motive that drives Acts 2 to 5 is planted here. If you understand Act 1 properly, the rest of the play unfolds like consequences from a cause. If you do not, the later acts feel like a series of unexplained shocks. That is the practical reason this book devotes an entire course to a single act.

## B. What kind of play it is

Three labels are used for *Julius Caesar*, and each tells you something about how to read it.

### A tragedy

The First Folio of 1623, the first collected edition of Shakespeare's plays, prints it among the Tragedies under the title *The Tragedie of Julius Caesar*. 'Tragedy' does not simply mean 'a sad story'. In the tradition that descends from the Greek philosopher Aristotle, a tragedy shows a person of high standing brought down through a combination of their own error and forces larger than themselves. Aristotle's word for the error is *hamartia*, which is often translated 'tragic flaw' but is closer to 'a mistake' or 'a missing of the mark'; scholars argue about the translation, and you should never write as if the word had one settled meaning. The fall arouses pity and fear, and it ends in death and a sober recognition of what has been lost.

Shakespeare's tragedies add something the Greeks did not stress: an intense interest in the *inner* life. His tragic figures talk to themselves, argue with themselves, hide from themselves. When Brutus describes himself in Scene 2 as "poor Brutus, with himself at war", he is announcing the subject of Shakespearean tragedy in five words. Keep that phrase near you throughout this course.

### A Roman play

*Julius Caesar* is also grouped with *Antony and Cleopatra* and *Coriolanus* (and sometimes the early *Titus Andronicus*) as one of Shakespeare's 'Roman plays'. The three mature Roman plays draw their stories from the Greek biographer Plutarch, whose *Parallel Lives* Shakespeare read in Sir Thomas North's English translation of 1579. The label matters because Roman plays share a distinctive atmosphere. Their world is *public*: streets, the Capitol, the marketplace, the Senate, rather than bedrooms and gardens. Their values are public too: honour, reputation, service to the state, courage in the face of death, and a Roman idea of manliness that the characters constantly invoke and police. When Cassius in Scene 3 hears a voice in the dark and asks who is there, Casca's whole answer is two words, "A Roman.", as if that were an identity complete in itself. Women are pushed to the margins of the public action: Calpurnia speaks three words in Act 1 and Portia none. There are no clowns of the kind you meet in the comedies, no fairies, no love plot. Even the Cobbler's jokes in Scene 1 are jokes about work.

### A political drama

Finally, *Julius Caesar* is a political play in the exact sense: it is about how power is gained, displayed, threatened and lost. It was written and first performed in 1599, almost certainly at the newly built Globe theatre, where a Swiss visitor named Thomas Platter recorded seeing a play about Julius Caesar on 21 September that year. In 1599 Queen Elizabeth I was sixty-six, unmarried, and had named no heir. Official Tudor teaching, preached from pulpits, held that rebellion against a ruler was a grave sin; at the same time, educated humanists across Europe debated whether killing a tyrant could ever be justified. Audiences *may* have felt that a play about one-man rule, succession and the legitimacy of resistance touched questions very close to home, and that ancient Rome was a safe distance from which to think about them. Be careful here: we cannot know what any Elizabethan felt, and you should never write as if we could. What we can say is that Shakespeare keeps the political debate genuinely open. No character in Act 1 is simply right.

### The tragic-hero debate: Caesar or Brutus?

**Literary interpretation.** Readers have argued for centuries about who the tragic hero of this play is, and you need to know the shape of the argument, not a 'correct answer'.

*The case for Caesar.* The play bears his name. Every other character defines himself by his relation to Caesar: for him, against him, afraid of him, in love with him. His fall is the event around which the whole structure turns, and even after his death his name and his memory continue to shape what happens. Act 1 already shows a classically tragic combination in him: enormous public greatness alongside a private pride that refuses warnings. "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." is the sentence of a man who cannot imagine being wrong.

*The case for Brutus.* Brutus has the largest part in the play and by far the richest inner life. He alone is shown genuinely torn; he alone acts from an ideal rather than from ambition or grievance; his error (the decision to join the conspiracy, and later to trust Antony) leads directly to his suffering and death; and the play's final tribute is paid over his body, by his enemy, praising his nobility. That is the full arc of a tragic protagonist, and Caesar, who dies before the play is half over, does not have it.

*A third view.* Some critics argue that the tragedy belongs to neither man but to Rome itself: that Shakespeare is dramatising the death of a republic, and that the individuals are instruments of a larger collapse. On this reading the crowd, the tribunes and the storm are not background but subject.

How should you hold these together? Notice what Act 1 does with each man. Caesar is *talked about* far more than he talks. Brutus is *worked upon*. Cassius, oddly, is the one who gets the act's only soliloquy. Shakespeare seems to be distributing the tragic weight rather than fixing it on a single head, and an answer that recognises this will always outscore one that flatly declares 'Brutus is the tragic hero'. In an examination you may take a position, but you must show that you know it is a position.

## C. What Shakespeare is examining

A great play is never 'about' a theme in the way a school essay is; it examines questions by making people live them. Six questions run through Act 1, and you should learn to see each scene as an experiment on one or more of them. The two Themes chapters later in this book develop each in depth; here you need the shape.

**Power: where does it actually sit?** Officially, power in Rome sits in the Senate and the elected magistrates. Act 1 shows that it now sits in a crowd's affection for one man. Caesar's power is visible only through its effects: a holiday declared, statues dressed with decorations, tribunes who object and are silenced. Flavius understands the mechanism perfectly when he describes Caesar's popularity as "growing feathers" that, if "plucked", will make him "fly an ordinary pitch" (pitch: the height to which a hawk soars). Power here is not a possession but a relationship, and relationships can be interfered with.

**Persuasion: how are minds changed?** This is the central activity of Act 1. The tribunes shame a crowd into silence. Cassius spends the whole middle of Scene 2 changing Brutus's mind, using a mirror image, two anecdotes, a comparison of names and an appeal to ancestry. Casca's report of the crown offering is itself an act of persuasion, colouring the event with contempt. Cassius ends the scene planning letters that will pretend to be the voice of the people. Even Caesar, when he swoons, manages his audience by blaming his infirmity. Watch every speech as a *move* and ask what the speaker wants it to do.

**Honour: what does it permit?** Brutus tells Cassius he loves "The name of honour more than I fear death", and Cassius instantly replies, "Well, honour is the subject of my story." The word is a lever, and Cassius has found it. The play will test whether honour can justify killing a friend, and whether a man who prizes the *name* of honour can be led by that very name.

**Public image and private self.** Caesar is called "immortal Caesar", "this god", and compared to a "Colossus", yet Cassius has seen him nearly drown, heard him groan with fever and cry out for drink "As a sick girl". Caesar declares "for always I am Caesar" and in the next breath asks Antony to come round to his good side because "this ear is deaf". Brutus admits he has "veiled" his look. Casca, according to Cassius, "puts on this tardy form" (tardy: slow, dull). Cassius presents himself as the honest friend while planning forgery. Everyone in Act 1 has a surface and a depth, and the play trains you to look through the surface.

**Fate and choice.** A Soothsayer knows the date of Caesar's death. A storm fills the sky with wonders. Yet Cassius insists that "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings." The same storm is read three ways in Scene 3: Casca sees the gods' warning, Cicero observes that "men may construe things after their fashion", and Cassius turns it into a portrait of Caesar and a call to arms. Shakespeare never tells you which reading is right.

**How republics die.** This is the largest question and the one most often missed. The Republic in Act 1 does not fall to an invading army. It is hollowed out from within: its officials cannot control its people, its people prefer a holiday to liberty, its nobles turn to secret conspiracy and forged letters, and the man who claims to defend freedom, Cassius, uses methods that mock it. **Literary interpretation:** one strong reading is that Shakespeare shows the Republic already dying before a single dagger is drawn, so that the assassination is not the cause of its death but a symptom.

Two people in that table deserve an extra word now, because students regularly confuse them. There are **two Brutuses** among the conspirators: Marcus Brutus, the hero of the play, and Decius Brutus, a different man entirely (historically Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus) who is merely named in Act 1. When Cassius or Cinna say 'Brutus' without a first name, they always mean Marcus. And there are **two Cinnas** in the play: Cinna the conspirator, who appears in Scene 3, and Cinna the poet, who does not appear until Act 3 and is torn apart by the mob for sharing a name. Keep them apart from the start.

::: history Where Shakespeare found his people
Nearly every named character in Act 1 comes from Plutarch's *Parallel Lives*, written in Greek around AD 100 and translated into English by Sir Thomas North in 1579. Shakespeare read North closely, sometimes lifting whole sentences into verse, and he drew chiefly on three of the Lives: those of Caesar, Brutus and Antony. But he also *changed* what he found. Plutarch's tribunes acted in January 44 BC, weeks before the Lupercalia; Shakespeare puts their protest on the same day as the triumph and the festival. Plutarch's diadem was offered twice; Shakespeare's Casca says three times, which makes the ritual feel more deliberate. Plutarch records that anonymous messages were left for Brutus urging him to act; Shakespeare gives the forgery to Cassius, so that the conspiracy's noblest recruit is won by a lie. Caesar's epilepsy, his "falling sickness", is in Plutarch and Suetonius; his deafness in one ear is not in any ancient source and appears to be Shakespeare's own invention. Whenever you meet a vivid detail in Act 1, it is worth asking: did Shakespeare find this, or make it? The additions are where his purposes show most clearly.
:::

## E. What political situation Rome is in

To understand why two city officials are so angry at a crowd in the first scene, you need a working picture of the Roman Republic: not a history lesson, but enough to see what is being threatened.

**Historical record.** Rome had expelled its last king, Tarquin the Proud, in the year traditionally given as 509 BC, and had governed itself for nearly five centuries since as a *res publica*, 'the public thing', a state that belonged to its citizens rather than to a ruler. Its institutions were designed above all to prevent any one man from holding supreme power for long. Two **consuls**, elected each year, shared the highest office and could each veto the other. The **Senate**, a council of several hundred former magistrates drawn from the wealthy families, debated policy and in practice directed the state. The **tribunes of the people** (there were ten by this period) were officers whose job was to protect the ordinary citizens, the plebeians, against the aristocracy; their persons were sacred and they could veto almost any official act. The office of **dictator** existed for emergencies only and was traditionally limited to six months. Over all of this hung a single shared emotion: a loathing of the title *rex*, king, so deep that a Roman politician could destroy a rival simply by suggesting that he wanted it.

The slogan of this system was *SPQR*, *Senatus Populusque Romanus*, 'the Senate and People of Rome', and its proudest word was *libertas*, liberty, by which Romans meant not personal freedom in our sense but freedom from being ruled by a master. When Cassius in Scene 2 says "I was born free as Caesar", and when he speaks in Scene 3 of delivering himself "from bondage", he is using this vocabulary. The conspirators will call themselves liberators. You need to hear the Roman weight in that word.

Now set against this picture what the opening of the play actually shows. The people are not in the Forum voting; they are in the streets cheering a general. The tribunes, whose office exists to speak for those very people, cannot get them to obey and have to resort to insult. Caesar's statues are being dressed with "ceremonies" (decorations, probably scarves or ribbons) as if he were a god or a king. The tribunes tear the decorations down, and by the next scene, we are told, they have been removed from office for it. The Senate, we hear in Scene 3, is rumoured to be about to make Caesar a king everywhere but in Italy. Every institution built to prevent one-man rule is shown either failing or bending.

**Literary interpretation.** It is tempting to say that Caesar has 'seized' power. Shakespeare is more subtle and more disturbing. Nobody seizes anything in Act 1. Power has flowed to Caesar because the crowd loves him, because the Senate is frightened or flattering, and because the republican nobles, for all their talk, have no idea what to do except whisper. The Republic has not been conquered; it has been emptied. The street scene with which the play opens is the political situation of Rome in miniature: an official shouting at citizens who are not listening.

::: history The late Republic was already breaking
Caesar did not invent the problem; he inherited it. For two generations before him, Rome's wars had been fought by armies loyal to their generals rather than to the state, and those generals had learned to use their soldiers in Roman politics. Marius had held the consulship an unprecedented seven times. Sulla had marched on Rome itself and made himself dictator in 82–81 BC, killing his enemies by published lists. Pompey had been granted extraordinary commands and celebrated three triumphs. In 60 BC Caesar, Pompey and Crassus formed a private alliance, now called the First Triumvirate, that effectively ran the state for several years. By the time Caesar crossed the Rubicon in January 49 BC, the republican constitution had already been bent repeatedly by strongmen. Shakespeare does not stage any of this, but Marullus's reverence for Pompey and Cassius's bitterness that "Rome ... hast lost the breed of noble bloods" only make sense in a Rome that has been losing its republican nerve for decades.
:::

## F. What happened before the play begins

Act 1 opens on a day of celebration, and the first thing to understand is *what* is being celebrated. The Cobbler says the people have made holiday "to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph". A triumph was the greatest honour the Republic could grant a general: a procession through the city to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline hill, with the victor in a chariot, captives in chains, spoils on display and soldiers marching behind. But a triumph was supposed to be for victory over *foreign* enemies. Marullus's furious question, "What conquest brings he home? / What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?" (tributaries: conquered peoples who pay tribute), is not rhetorical decoration. He is pointing out that this triumph has no foreign captives, because the enemy Caesar has just defeated was Roman.

Here is the history Act 1 needs, kept to its bones.

**Historical record.** In 49 BC the alliance between Caesar and Pompey had collapsed, and Caesar, ordered by the Senate to give up his army, instead led it across the Rubicon river into Italy, beginning a civil war. Pompey and most of the Senate fled to Greece. On 9 August 48 BC Caesar destroyed Pompey's army at Pharsalus. Pompey escaped to Egypt and was murdered on arrival, on 28 September 48 BC, by men hoping to please Caesar. Both Brutus and Cassius fought on Pompey's side and were pardoned by Caesar afterwards; this is the uncomfortable fact beneath Cassius's remark in Scene 2 that "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus". The war did not end with Pompey's death. His sons raised new armies, and Caesar had to fight on in Africa and then in Spain. The final battle was at Munda, in southern Spain, on 17 March 45 BC, where Pompey's elder son Gnaeus was killed and the younger, Sextus, escaped. In October 45 BC Caesar celebrated a triumph for this Spanish victory, and Plutarch records that Romans resented it because it honoured the killing of fellow citizens, the sons of a great Roman, rather than the defeat of a foreign enemy. That resentment is Marullus's speech.

Meanwhile Caesar's constitutional position had become something Rome had never seen. He had been named dictator in 49 BC, again in 48–47, dictator for ten years in 46 BC, and in February 44 BC dictator *perpetuo*, for life. The emergency office with a six-month limit had become permanent. He was also consul for 44 BC, with Antony as his colleague. He had not taken the title of king, and he publicly refused a crown, but he held every power a king would have held, and his statues were being treated as a king's.

**Shakespeare's text compresses all of this.** The triumph of October 45 BC and the feast of Lupercalia on 15 February 44 BC are made into a single day in Scenes 1 and 2. Scene 3 takes place on the night before the Ides of March; a month passes in silence between the second scene and the third, and the only clue is Cicero's question about whether Caesar comes "to the Capitol tomorrow". You do not need to memorise the dates. You need to understand the sequence, because the sequence is the emotional logic of the opening: a civil war has been won; the victor is being honoured as though he had conquered foreigners; the old republican families, many of whom fought on the losing side and owe their lives to his mercy, are watching the celebrations with a mixture of gratitude, humiliation and fear.

Pompey's presence in Act 1 is worth a moment more. He is dead, but Shakespeare keeps him in the air. Marullus remembers the crowds that once waited "The livelong day, with patient expectation, / To see great Pompey pass the streets of Rome" and accuses them of strewing flowers before the man "That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood". Cassius's conspirators, in Scene 3, gather at "Pompey's Porch", the colonnade of the great theatre Pompey built. **Historical record:** Caesar was in fact murdered in the Senate meeting-hall attached to that theatre, beneath Pompey's statue, though Shakespeare moves the assassination to the Capitol. **Literary interpretation:** the dead rival haunts the play as a reminder of what the Republic used to look like and how quickly a crowd forgets.

## G. Why Caesar is so important

Students sometimes complain that Caesar is a disappointing character: he speaks little, he is pompous, he dies early. This is to mistake the man for the part he plays. Caesar's importance in Act 1 does not lie in what he says. It lies in the fact that every other person on stage is defined by his relationship to Caesar, and that the play's whole political question is embodied in his person.

Consider how Shakespeare manages his first appearance. The entire first scene passes without him. We hear his name, we hear of his triumph and his statues, we watch the tribunes undress his images, and he never arrives. When he does enter, at the start of Scene 2, his first word is his wife's name and his first concern is her fertility: he instructs Antony to touch Calpurnia during the Lupercalia race, "for our elders say / The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse." This is a man who speaks in public about his private business as if the whole city were his household, and who believes, or wishes to be seen believing, in an old fertility ritual. Antony's reply tells you how he is regarded: "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed." A sentence from Caesar is already a command.

Then comes the Soothsayer. A voice from the crowd, "shriller than all the music", calls his name, and Caesar stops the procession to hear it: "Speak. Caesar is turned to hear." Notice that he refers to himself in the third person, as though 'Caesar' were an institution rather than a man. The warning, "Beware the ides of March", is delivered twice. Caesar dismisses it in nine words: "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." That is the whole exchange, and it is one of the most important moments in the act, because it shows the great man's method of dealing with anything he does not wish to hear: he names it, diminishes it and moves on.

His second appearance is just as brief and just as revealing. Returning from the games, he studies Cassius and delivers a character analysis of extraordinary accuracy: Cassius "reads much", is "a great observer", "loves no plays", "hears no music", "Seldom he smiles", and "Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". Every word of this is true, and the audience knows it, having just watched Cassius at work. Yet Caesar ends by refusing to act on his own insight: "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar." And then, in the very next line, the god asks for help: "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf."

**Literary interpretation.** That is why Caesar matters. Shakespeare has built him as a living contradiction: supremely perceptive and wilfully blind; a public colossus and a private body subject to fever, epilepsy and deafness; a man who refuses a crown three times while, in Casca's view, longing for it. The debate about whether he is a tyrant or a great leader cannot be settled from Act 1, because Shakespeare has deliberately given you evidence for both. His importance is that he *is* the question. Whatever Rome decides to do about Caesar will decide what Rome becomes.

::: insight Why Caesar is kept off-stage in Scene 1
A lesser dramatist would have opened with the triumph: trumpets, chariot, the great man waving. Shakespeare opens with two angry officials and a cobbler, and Caesar is not seen. This is a calculated choice with at least three effects. First, it makes Caesar an *idea* before he is a person. We meet his power through its traces, a holiday, decorated statues, a crowd that has forgotten Pompey, and so we learn to think of him as a force rather than a character, which is exactly how Rome thinks of him. Second, it sets up a comparison that will govern the whole play: the Caesar of reputation against the Caesar of the flesh. The tribunes speak of a man who "would soar above the view of men"; the man who then walks on asks a priest to cure his wife's barrenness and cannot hear on one side. The gap is the point, and it could only be opened by letting us hear the legend first. Third, delaying the entrance teaches the audience how to watch the play. By the time Caesar speaks, we have already been trained to listen for what people *say about* power, and to notice who is absent when it is discussed. Act 1 is, in a sense, a play about a man who is hardly there, and that absence is Shakespeare's first comment on what one-man rule does to a city: it fills every conversation with a person nobody is actually talking to.
:::

## H. Why some Romans fear Caesar

If Caesar is so beloved, why is anyone afraid of him? Act 1 gives at least four distinct answers, spoken by different people, and a good reader keeps them separate rather than mashing them into 'they feared his ambition'.

**The tribunes' fear is constitutional.** Flavius and Marullus do not accuse Caesar of any crime. Their anxiety is about what his popularity is doing to the Republic's structure. Flavius puts it in the image of a hawk whose feathers must be plucked so that it will "fly an ordinary pitch" instead of soaring "above the view of men" to "keep us all in servile fearfulness" (servile: slave-like). The fear is that Rome will come to live in the attitude of servants, looking up. Their reaction is to attack the *symbols* of Caesar's elevation, the decorated statues, because symbols are how a republic turns into a monarchy without anyone voting for it.

**Brutus's fear is for the Republic's principle.** When he hears shouting, his instant reaction is "I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." He says in the same breath that he loves Caesar. His fear is not of Caesar the man but of the office of king, which his family's whole identity is built on hating. This is why Cassius's reminder of his ancestor, "There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king" (brooked: tolerated), lands so heavily.

**Cassius's fear is personal and comparative.** Listen to the terms he uses. Caesar "doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs". Cassius "must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him". He had "as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself" (lief: willingly). What Cassius cannot bear is not tyranny in the abstract but the experience of being made small by an equal. His two anecdotes, the swimming race in the Tiber and the fever in Spain, are both designed to prove that Caesar's body is no better than his own; the question that torments him is "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great?"

**Casca's fear is political gossip made concrete.** In Scene 3 he reports that "the Senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king, / And he shall wear his crown by sea and land / In every place save here in Italy." Whether or not this rumour is true (the text never confirms it), it gives the conspiracy a deadline. Fear in Act 1 moves from the general to the specific: from a hawk flying too high, to a shout in the distance, to a rumour with a date attached.

**Literary interpretation.** Notice what is *not* in Act 1: a single act of tyranny by Caesar. He silences two tribunes, which is harsh but, in the Roman context, within the reach of his legal power. He refuses a crown. He pardons his enemies and promotes them. Shakespeare has given his Romans fears that are entirely reasonable and entirely unproven, and this is the moral trap of the play. One strong reading is that the conspirators act not against what Caesar has done but against what he *might* do, and that the tragedy lies in killing a man for his future.

## I. Why Brutus matters

Brutus speaks far fewer lines in Act 1 than Cassius, and the ones he does speak are mostly guarded and brief. Yet everyone in the act, including Caesar's enemies, treats him as the key to Rome. Why?

**His name.** Brutus carries the most republican surname in the city. The ancestor Cassius invokes, Lucius Junius Brutus, led the expulsion of the last king and founded the Republic. In Scene 3 Cassius sends letters to be fixed "Upon old Brutus' statue". A Brutus against Caesar would make the conspiracy look like history repeating itself in the right direction.

**His reputation.** Casca says of him, late in Scene 3, "O, he sits high in all the people's hearts, / And that which would appear offence in us / His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness." (alchemy: the medieval dream of turning base metal into gold.) The conspirators know that their act will look like murder unless a man of Brutus's known integrity stands in front of it. This is a cold political calculation, and Shakespeare lets Casca state it with complete frankness.

**His inner conflict.** Here is what distinguishes him as a dramatic creation. Before Cassius says a word of politics, Brutus has already confessed that he is "Vexèd ... / Of late with passions of some difference" and "with himself at war". The seed is in him before Cassius plants anything. When he hears the crowd shout, he admits both halves of his dilemma in one line: "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well." He then hands Cassius the exact lever to move him: "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently; / For let the gods so speed me as I love / The name of honour more than I fear death." Watch the word *name*. Brutus loves the *name* of honour, and the whole play will ask whether he can tell the name from the thing.

**His vulnerability.** The tragedy of Brutus is that his virtues are the handles by which he is lifted. His idealism makes him unable to imagine that Cassius's letters are forged. His modesty, "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection", makes him accept Cassius as his mirror. His seriousness, "I am not gamesome", keeps him away from the games and alone with the one man who wants to use him. By the end of Scene 2 he has promised to think it over and to meet Cassius tomorrow, and he has given Cassius his motto: "Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions". Cassius, in his soliloquy, is in no doubt: "Three parts of him / Is ours already". Brutus matters because the play's moral weight rests on whether a good man can do a terrible thing for good reasons, and Act 1 is where we watch him begin to believe he can.

## J. Why Cassius matters

If Brutus is the conscience of the conspiracy, Cassius is its engine. He speaks more lines than anyone else in Act 1, he gets the act's only soliloquy, and nearly everything that happens after the Soothsayer's warning happens because Cassius makes it happen. Three things make him indispensable to your understanding.

**He is the master persuader.** The long dialogue in Scene 2 is a lesson in how to change a mind without appearing to try. He begins with a complaint about friendship, not politics: Brutus has grown cold. He offers a mirror: Brutus cannot see his own worth, so Cassius, "I, your glass", will "modestly discover" it to him. He waits for the crowd's shout to do his work for him, and only then asks, "Ay, do you fear it?" He tells stories that humiliate Caesar's body while seeming merely to recall shared adventures. He plays with names: "'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar.'" He appeals to ancestry, to Rome, to shame: "Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" And when Brutus asks for time, he gracefully accepts, having got exactly the promise he needed. The chapter *Cassius: The Manipulation Masterclass* will take this apart move by move; for now, understand that Shakespeare has given you, in Cassius, a complete anatomy of political seduction.

**He tells the truth and lies at the same time.** This is what makes him more interesting than a simple villain. Almost everything Cassius says about Caesar's body is, within the play, true. Caesar does have the falling sickness; Caesar is deaf in one ear; Caesar is vain. Cassius's complaint that "we petty men / Walk under his huge legs" describes a real political situation. Yet his motive is envy as much as principle, as Caesar sees ("Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves"), and his method, the forged letters "As if they came from several citizens", is a fraud. He is a man using true facts for a partly false purpose, and the play never lets you rest in a simple judgement of him.

**His soliloquy tells you what the act has been.** Alone at the end of Scene 2, Cassius drops the mask: "Well, Brutus, thou art noble. Yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed." (mettle: temperament, with a pun on metal that can be worked; wrought: shaped.) Then the question that is really a confession: "For who so firm that cannot be seduced?" He admits that Caesar "loves Brutus" and that he would not himself be so used: "If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me." This is the only moment in Act 1 when a character speaks with no listener to manage, and what we hear is a planner congratulating himself. Cassius matters because he shows you that the conspiracy, from its first hour, rests on a mixture of real grievance and deliberate deceit.

**Historical record, briefly.** The real Cassius had reason for resentment: ancient sources record that Caesar gave Brutus the more prestigious praetorship in 44 BC and Cassius the lesser one, and Plutarch says Caesar distrusted him. Cassius followed the Epicurean philosophy, which taught that the gods do not interfere in human affairs; it is a nice irony, and perhaps a deliberate one, that in Scene 3 Shakespeare's Cassius reads the storm as a divine message when it suits his argument.

## K. Why Antony matters

Antony speaks only a handful of lines in Act 1, and a careless reader will forget him. Do not. Shakespeare is laying a fuse.

**Shakespeare's text.** In Scene 2 Antony appears first as an athlete, stripped "for the course", the ritual race of the Lupercalia. Caesar gives him an order and he answers with perfect obedience: "I shall remember. / When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed." Later, when Caesar confides his suspicion of Cassius, Antony is relaxed and reassuring: "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given." (well given: well disposed, of good character.) Caesar's description of him is affectionate and slightly dismissive: Antony "loves ... plays" and music, and is the sort of man Caesar prefers to have around, "fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights". We also learn from Casca that it was Antony who offered Caesar the crown, three times, in the marketplace.

**Literary interpretation.** Put those facts together and you have a portrait that the rest of the play will overturn. Antony is presented through other men's eyes as a pleasure-lover, a loyal subordinate, and an instrument, the hand that holds out a crown so that Caesar can refuse it. The conspirators will take this picture at face value, and Brutus will later argue that Antony is harmless once Caesar is gone. The audience that knows the play knows how wrong this is; the audience watching for the first time is given only one hint, Antony's own absolute formula of loyalty, which makes clear that if anything happens to Caesar, there is a man whose entire identity is bound up in him. Antony matters in Act 1 precisely because he is underestimated in it. Shakespeare is showing you how a political misjudgement is made: by looking at the surface of a man and assuming it is the whole.

**Historical record.** The real Antony was no idle companion. He had served under Caesar in Gaul from 54 BC, had been tribune in 49 BC (his vetoes on Caesar's behalf and his flight to Caesar's camp gave Caesar a pretext for crossing the Rubicon), had commanded a wing of Caesar's army at Pharsalus, and in 44 BC was consul alongside Caesar. His reputation for drinking and revelry was also real, which is partly why his enemies misjudged him. When you reach his funeral oration in Act 3 you will see what that misjudgement cost; Act 1 is where the mistake is quietly made.

## L. Why the Roman crowd matters

Shakespeare gives the Roman people the first words of the action, or rather, he gives the first words to the men trying to control them. The play opens with Flavius shouting "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!" and the whole of Scene 1 is a struggle between officials and citizens over whether the people may cheer whom they please. This is not merely a lively opening. The crowd is one of the play's major characters, and its behaviour in Act 1 is a prophecy.

**Shakespeare's text.** The commoners we meet are not a faceless mob. The Carpenter is briefly spoken; the Cobbler is witty, confident and entirely unafraid of authority. He teases the tribunes with puns ("a mender of bad soles"; "if you be out, sir, I can mend you"), describes himself grandly as "a surgeon to old shoes", and gives a perfectly reasonable account of why he is in the street: "we make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph." Marullus's answer is the long speech of reproach that reminds them how they once waited all day to see Pompey and now strew flowers before the man who comes "in triumph over Pompey's blood". The result, Flavius observes, is that "They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness." A speech has turned a crowd.

In Scene 2 the crowd is heard, not seen: three shouts offstage, each of which Brutus and Cassius interpret with anxiety. Casca then describes the people in the marketplace in language of pure contempt, "the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps", and notes that they behave towards Caesar exactly as audiences behave towards actors, clapping and hissing "according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre". Cassius, in Scene 3, is still harsher: Caesar would not be a wolf "But that he sees the Romans are but sheep", he "were no lion, were not Romans hinds" (hinds: female deer, and also a word for servants), and Rome itself is nothing but "What trash is Rome, / What rubbish, and what offal", base matter fit only to light a fire for Caesar.

**Literary interpretation.** Three things are being established. First, the crowd is *fickle*: it loved Pompey and now loves Caesar, and it can be shamed into silence by one speech and roused by another. Second, the crowd is *powerful*: the tribunes' fear, Brutus's fear and Cassius's bitterness are all, at bottom, fears of what the people's affection can do. Caesar is great because they cheer him. Third, the aristocrats on both sides *despise* the people even while depending on them, and the play will punish that contempt. Casca's comparison of politics to theatre is more than a joke: it tells you that in this Rome power is a performance and the crowd is the audience whose applause decides who rules. When you reach Act 3, and watch the same crowd swing from Brutus to Antony in the space of two speeches, you will recognise what Act 1 has already shown you in miniature.

::: insight Why the play opens with the crowd
Shakespeare could have opened in the Senate, or with Caesar, or with the conspirators. He opened with a cobbler making jokes. Consider what this does. The audience at the Globe in 1599 was itself a mixed crowd, with tradesmen and apprentices standing in the yard, and the first thing it saw was a stage full of tradesmen being told to go home by their betters. The comedy of the Cobbler's puns (soles and souls, mending shoes and mending men) invites the audience to side with the commoners against the humourless tribunes; then Marullus's great speech about Pompey swings the sympathy back, and we see the crowd as faithless. In the space of eighty lines Shakespeare has made the audience *feel* the crowd's fickleness from the inside, by making them fickle too. He has also made a structural promise. The play will begin and end with the question of whom the people love, and the Lupercalia shouts of Scene 2, the mob of Act 3 and the battles of Act 5 are all consequences of the fact established here: that in this Rome the people's affection is the real crown, and it can be put on and taken off in an afternoon. The opening is not a warm-up before the serious action. It is the thesis of the play, performed as a street argument.
:::
## Three graded summaries, and a warning about them

What follows are three summaries of Act 1 at three different lengths: a single connected page, a ten-minute structured walkthrough, and a one-minute paragraph with a chain diagram. Use them in this order the first time, then use whichever one you need before a revision session or an examination. But understand clearly what they are for. **They do not replace the detailed teaching in the scene chapters, the character chapters and the theme chapters that follow.** A summary tells you what happened; the chapters tell you what it means, why Shakespeare built it that way, how it connects to the rest of the play, and how to write about it. A student who learns only the summaries will write answers that retell the plot, and plot-retelling is the single most common reason for low marks in literature papers. Treat these pages as the skeleton on which the rest of the book hangs flesh.

## ACT 1 IN ONE PAGE

It is a holiday in Rome. Caesar has returned victorious from Spain, where he destroyed the last army of Pompey's sons, and the city has turned out to celebrate his triumph; by Shakespeare's compression it is also the feast of Lupercalia. Two tribunes, Flavius and Marullus, find the streets full of tradesmen in their best clothes and are furious. A quick-witted Cobbler fences with them in puns before admitting that the people have come "to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph". Marullus explodes. Have they forgotten Pompey, for whom they once waited all day on rooftops? Now they scatter flowers before the man "That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood". The crowd slinks off in shame, and the tribunes split up to strip the decorations from Caesar's statues, Flavius arguing that if Caesar's "growing feathers" are plucked he will fly at "an ordinary pitch" instead of soaring above them all.

Caesar enters in procession with his wife Calpurnia, Antony dressed for the ritual race, Brutus, Cassius, Casca, Cicero and others. He publicly asks Antony to touch Calpurnia during the race, since tradition says this cures barrenness. A Soothsayer calls from the crowd, "Beware the ides of March." Caesar has him brought forward, hears the warning again, and dismisses him: "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass."

The procession moves on to the games, but Brutus stays behind, saying he is "not gamesome", and Cassius stays with him. Cassius complains that Brutus has grown cold towards him; Brutus confesses that he is troubled by private "passions of some difference" and "with himself at war". Cassius offers to be Brutus's mirror and show him the worth he cannot see in himself. A shout is heard from the games, and Brutus blurts out his fear that the people "Choose Caesar for their king", adding at once that he loves Caesar well. Brutus declares he values honour above death; Cassius seizes the word and makes "honour ... the subject of my story". He tells how Caesar once challenged him to swim the Tiber and had to be rescued, crying "Help me, Cassius, or I sink!", and how Caesar shook with fever in Spain and begged for drink "As a sick girl". A second shout. Cassius pictures Caesar as a "Colossus" under whose legs "we petty men" creep, insists that "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves", weighs the names 'Brutus' and 'Caesar' and finds them equal, and reminds Brutus of the ancestor who would have endured the devil in Rome sooner than a king. Brutus, moved but cautious, promises to consider all this and to meet again, adding that he would rather be a villager than a Roman under such conditions.

Caesar returns from the games looking angry, his followers "like a chidden train". He tells Antony he distrusts "lean and hungry" men like Cassius, who think too much, then insists he fears nothing, "for always I am Caesar", and asks Antony to come to his right side because his left ear is deaf. Brutus and Cassius detain Casca, who reports in sour prose that Antony offered Caesar a crown three times, that Caesar refused it each time, though "he would fain have had it", that the stinking crowd cheered the refusals, and that Caesar then fainted in the marketplace, "foamed at mouth and was speechless". Cicero spoke Greek, "it was Greek to me", and the tribunes Marullus and Flavius have been "put to silence" for stripping the statues. Casca agrees to dine with Cassius. Alone, Cassius gloats that Brutus's "honourable mettle may be wrought" and plans to throw forged letters, as if from citizens, through Brutus's windows that night.

A month later, on the night before the Ides of March, a storm of impossible violence breaks over Rome. Casca, sword drawn, tells Cicero of wonders: a slave's hand burning unharmed, a lion by the Capitol, women who saw men of fire in the streets, an owl shrieking at noon. Cicero remarks drily that "men may construe things after their fashion" and goes home. Cassius arrives, boasting that he has bared his chest to the lightning. He reads the storm as heaven's warning about a man "Most like this dreadful night", and Casca names Caesar. Casca reports a rumour that the Senate will crown Caesar king tomorrow. Cassius declares he will free himself with his dagger first, scorns the Romans, who make Caesar a wolf by being "but sheep", and Casca gives his hand to the cause. Cassius reveals that other noble Romans wait at Pompey's Porch. Cinna hurries in, longing for "the noble Brutus" to join them; Cassius gives him the forged letters to plant in Brutus's chair, window and ancestral statue. Casca observes that Brutus's reputation will turn their crime into virtue, and the two set off, after midnight, to secure Brutus before dawn.

## ACT 1 IN TEN MINUTES

This walkthrough follows the act scene by scene and, for each movement, tells you what happens and what to notice. 'Notice' points are where the marks live.

### Scene 1 — A street in Rome: the tribunes and the crowd

**What happens.** Flavius and Marullus, tribunes of the people, discover the streets full of working men who have taken an unofficial holiday. They demand to know the men's trades. A Carpenter answers plainly; a Cobbler answers in riddling puns about soles, mending and awls, and finally explains that everyone has come out to cheer Caesar's triumph. Marullus delivers a long, furious speech reminding them of their old devotion to Pompey and condemning their ingratitude; he sends them home to pray that the gods will "intermit the plague" (intermit: hold back) that such ingratitude deserves. The commoners leave in silence. The tribunes resolve to go through the city removing the "ceremonies" from Caesar's statues, and Flavius closes the scene with the image of Caesar as a hawk whose feathers must be plucked.

**What to notice.**
- The commoners speak **prose**; the tribunes speak **verse**. Shakespeare marks class and tone through the form of the lines before you have understood a word.
- The Cobbler is cleverer than his questioners. His puns ("a mender of bad soles"; "I can mend you") are small acts of insolence, and the tribunes' irritation shows that their authority is already weak.
- Marullus's speech works by **contrast** and **rhetorical question**: then (Pompey) against now (Caesar), and a chain of "And do you now ... ?" questions that pile up guilt. Learn its shape; it is the first great persuasive speech in a play built of them.
- The crowd that was cheering a minute ago leaves "tongue-tied in their guiltiness". This is the first demonstration of the people's fickleness, and you will refer back to it from Act 3.
- Caesar never appears. The scene is *about* him, and his absence is deliberate.

### Scene 2, first movement — The procession and the Soothsayer

**What happens.** Caesar enters with his train. He tells Antony, who is about to run the Lupercalia race, to touch Calpurnia so that she may "Shake off their sterile curse". Antony promises obedience. A Soothsayer cries out from the crowd; Caesar stops, has him brought forward, hears "Beware the ides of March" twice, and dismisses him as "a dreamer".

**What to notice.**
- Caesar's first words are about a private matter, his wife's childlessness, spoken as public business. Watch how he treats the city as an extension of himself.
- He refers to himself in the **third person**: "Caesar is turned to hear." This habit is a key to his character.
- The warning is given, repeated and refused. The Soothsayer is the voice of a future the audience already knows; Caesar's dismissal is the first of many refusals to listen.
- Casca is the one calling for silence when Caesar speaks. Note that the man who will strike first is here acting as Caesar's usher.

### Scene 2, second movement — Cassius works on Brutus

**What happens.** Everyone leaves for the games except Brutus and Cassius. In a long dialogue, interrupted twice by offstage shouts, Cassius moves Brutus step by step from friendly puzzlement to a cautious promise. The steps are: a complaint about Brutus's coldness; Brutus's admission of inner conflict; the mirror image; the first shout and Brutus's fear of a king; Brutus's declaration about honour; Cassius's two anecdotes of Caesar's physical weakness (the Tiber, the fever); the second shout; the Colossus image and "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars"; the comparison of names; the appeal to Brutus's ancestor; Brutus's measured reply and his promise to meet again.

**What to notice.**
- Brutus is troubled *before* Cassius begins. His phrase "with himself at war" is the condition Cassius exploits, not something Cassius creates.
- Every one of Cassius's arguments attacks the *gap* between Caesar's reputation and his body. He never argues that Caesar has done wrong; he argues that Caesar is no better than they are.
- The **offstage shouts** are a stage device that does Cassius's work for him. Each cheer from the crowd makes Brutus more anxious and more receptive.
- Brutus says very little, but what he says is decisive: "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well", and the lines on honour and death. An examiner will expect you to know that Brutus handed Cassius the key.
- The scene ends not with a yes but with a promise to think. That restraint is Brutus's character in one gesture.

### Scene 2, third movement — Caesar returns; Casca's report; Cassius's soliloquy

**What happens.** Caesar comes back from the games visibly angry, his train "chidden", Calpurnia pale, Cicero red-eyed. Caesar tells Antony he distrusts Cassius's "lean and hungry look", analyses him with precision, denies any fear, and asks Antony to speak into his good ear. When the procession passes, Brutus and Cassius pull Casca aside. In blunt prose Casca describes how Antony offered Caesar a crown ("one of these coronets") three times, how Caesar refused each time with growing reluctance, how the crowd cheered the refusals, and how Caesar then fainted, foaming at the mouth. He adds that Caesar, before falling, offered the crowd his throat to cut, that Cicero spoke Greek, and that Marullus and Flavius have been silenced. Casca leaves; Brutus departs after arranging to meet Cassius tomorrow; Cassius, alone, reveals his plan to forge letters in several hands and throw them in at Brutus's windows.

**What to notice.**
- Caesar's analysis of Cassius is **correct in every detail** and is then overridden by his vanity. This pattern, insight cancelled by pride, is the Caesar you must be able to describe.
- "for always I am Caesar" followed immediately by "this ear is deaf" is one of the sharpest juxtapositions in the play: the public name and the private body in two consecutive lines.
- The crown scene is **reported, not shown**. Shakespeare lets us see it only through Casca's contemptuous eyes, so that we never know whether Caesar truly wanted the crown. Casca says "to my thinking, he would fain have had it"; that is an opinion, and you must present it as one.
- Casca's prose is a mask. Cassius calls his "rudeness ... a sauce to his good wit"; Brutus remembers him as "quick mettle" at school. The blunt manner hides a sharp observer.
- The soliloquy is the only time in Act 1 that a character speaks alone. It tells you the conspiracy will be built on forgery, and it should colour every later claim the conspirators make about honour.

### Scene 3 — The storm: Casca, Cicero, Cassius, Cinna

**What happens.** A month has passed; it is the night before the Ides of March. Thunder and lightning. Casca, sword drawn and breathless, tells Cicero of supernatural sights. Cicero answers that men interpret things to suit themselves, asks whether Caesar will go to the Capitol tomorrow (he will), and leaves. Cassius enters, exhilarated by the storm, and rebukes Casca for fearing it. He explains the portents as heaven's warning against a man grown "prodigious" like the night itself; Casca guesses Caesar. Casca reports the rumour that the Senate will make Caesar king tomorrow. Cassius vows that his dagger will free him from bondage, and launches into a bitter attack on Romans who make Caesar great because "the Romans are but sheep". Casca offers his hand. Cassius reveals that conspirators wait at Pompey's Porch. Cinna arrives, asks whether they can win "the noble Brutus", and is sent to plant the forged letters. Cassius and Casca go to visit Brutus before dawn, Casca noting that Brutus's good name will turn their offence into virtue.

**What to notice.**
- The storm is **read three ways**: as divine anger (Casca), as nothing certain (Cicero), as a political portrait of Caesar and a call to action (Cassius). The scene is a lesson in interpretation itself.
- Cassius, who told Brutus that fate is "not in our stars", now uses the heavens as evidence. He is not a consistent philosopher; he is a persuader who uses whatever lies to hand.
- The rumour of a crown "In every place save here in Italy" gives the plot a deadline. Act 1 ends with the clock running.
- Cassius recruits Casca by a different method from the one he used on Brutus: shame about manliness ("womanish"), contempt for the crowd, and a theatrical display of courage. Compare the two methods; the comparison is the heart of the Cassius chapter.
- Everyone agrees that the conspiracy needs Brutus. The act closes with the conspirators going to collect him, and the forged letters already on their way.

## ACT 1 IN ONE MINUTE

Rome celebrates Caesar's victory over Pompey's sons, but two tribunes shame the fickle crowd and strip Caesar's statues. At the Lupercalia a Soothsayer warns Caesar to "Beware the ides of March"; Caesar dismisses him as "a dreamer". Cassius works on the troubled Brutus, using Caesar's physical weaknesses, the equality of their names and the memory of Brutus's king-expelling ancestor to argue that "The fault ... is not in our stars, / But in ourselves". Casca reports that Caesar thrice refused a crown and then fainted; the tribunes have been silenced. Alone, Cassius plans forged letters. A month later, in a night of portents, Cassius recruits Casca, sends Cinna to plant the letters, and sets off with the conspirators to secure Brutus before dawn.
