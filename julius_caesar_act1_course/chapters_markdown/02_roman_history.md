# Roman History Before Act 1: Only What You Need, Taught Properly

*This chapter gives you the Roman background without which Act 1 looks like a group of grown men panicking over a hat. It teaches only what the three scenes of Act 1 actually depend on: how the Republic worked, what Romans valued and feared, how Caesar climbed to the top, and what two public ceremonies, the triumph and the Lupercalia, meant to the people watching them. Every item is tied back to the play through the same four-step chain, so that history never floats free of the text. At the end, a timeline and a section on what Shakespeare deliberately changed will make you the rare student who can tell the historical record from Shakespeare's invention, which is exactly the habit an examiner rewards.*

## Why a Literature Student Needs Any of This

Let me be direct about a problem you may have felt since Class 9. When you read Act 1 Scene 1, two officials shout at some workmen for taking a holiday. In Scene 2, you are told that a man has been offered a crown three times and has refused it three times, and the whole city seems to lose its mind. In Scene 3, respectable senators stand in a thunderstorm plotting murder because of a rumour about a crown. If you do not know Rome, all of this looks like over-reaction. If you do know Rome, every one of these moments becomes tense, logical and frightening. That is the difference this chapter makes.

You do not need a history degree. You need roughly twenty ideas, taught properly and always connected to the words Shakespeare actually wrote. For each idea I will use the same four-step chain:

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → what actually happened or existed in Rome (the historical record)
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → what the idea meant to a Roman of 44 BC, the world the characters live in
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → where it appears in Act 1 and what he does with it (Shakespeare's text, plus literary interpretation where I say so)
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → how the knowledge turns into marks (exam-oriented advice)
```

Keep one distinction alive throughout: **HISTORICAL RECORD** is what the ancient sources and modern historians tell us; **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** is what the play says; **LITERARY INTERPRETATION** is how critics read the gap between the two. Examiners are impressed by a student who knows which of the three they are talking about. They are unimpressed by a student who writes 'Caesar was killed in the Capitol' as history, because he was not, as you will see.

::: icse How this chapter earns marks honestly
Act 1 is the Class IX portion of the ICSE syllabus; the Class X board paper sets its drama extracts from Acts III, IV and V. So you will not be asked to write an essay on the Roman Senate. But the Class X questions constantly assume Act 1 knowledge: what motivated Brutus, why Cassius hates Caesar, why the crowd matters, what 'the work the ides of March begun' refers to. Every historical idea in this chapter is chosen because it feeds an Act 3, 4 or 5 answer, and I will say so each time. Treat the history as a tool for reading, not as a separate subject to memorise.

## The Machinery of the Republic

### The Roman Republic

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Rome was, according to its own tradition, ruled by kings for its first two and a half centuries. In 509 BC the last king was driven out, and the Romans built a system designed to make sure that no single person could ever hold that kind of power again. They called it the *res publica*, literally 'the public thing' or 'the public business', the origin of our word 'republic'. The idea was that the state belonged to the citizens collectively, not to one family. Power was divided among many elected officials (magistrates), held for short terms (usually one year), and always shared between colleagues who could block one another. The Senate, a council of senior men, advised; the assemblies of citizens voted; and the whole structure ran for over four centuries before it collapsed in the lifetime of the men you meet in Act 1.

**WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE PLAY.** Everything Cassius and Brutus say about 'Rome' assumes that Rome is a Republic and that a Republic is the only honourable form of government for a free people. When Cassius cries "Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!", he is not lamenting a lack of aristocrats. He is saying that the Republic has stopped producing men who will defend it. When Brutus says he "had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us", the 'hard conditions' are the death of the Republic. A 'villager' here means a nobody from a country village; Brutus would rather be a nobody in a free village than a famous man in an unfree Rome.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → From 509 BC Rome was a republic: power divided among annually elected magistrates, advised by the Senate, with laws voted by citizen assemblies; no king.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → The Republic was not just a system but an identity. To be Roman was to be a citizen of a free state; a Roman who bowed to a single master had stopped being fully Roman.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → The word 'Rome' in Act 1 always means 'the free Republic'. Cassius's "Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" and Brutus's "Brutus had rather be a villager" only make sense if losing the Republic means losing Rome itself.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → When an Act 3 or 4 question asks why Brutus joined the conspiracy, the correct core answer is not 'jealousy' but 'to preserve the Republic'. Act 1 is where the play establishes that Rome and the Republic are the same thing in Brutus's mind.
```

### The Senate

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The Senate was the Republic's council of elders, several hundred men who had held public office. Senators were not elected to the Senate; they entered it by winning election to a magistracy and then stayed for life unless expelled. Technically the Senate only 'advised', but in practice its advice controlled money, foreign policy, the assignment of armies and the running of the provinces. It sat in a building called the *Curia* (the Senate house), and it could also meet in any consecrated space, including a temple. One detail you will need later: the old Senate house had burned down in a riot in 52 BC and its replacement was still unfinished in 44 BC, so the Senate was meeting in borrowed halls, among them a hall attached to Pompey's theatre. That is where Caesar actually died. The Senate's authority (*auctoritas*, a word we will return to) rested on tradition and prestige rather than on formal law, which is exactly why a man with a private army could ignore it.

Caesar enlarged the Senate with his own supporters, which the old families resented as a dilution of their dignity. By 44 BC the Senate was full of men who owed Caesar their seats, yet it was also the body through which the conspirators would have to work, and the place where they finally killed him.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Act 1 refers to senators several times. Brutus describes Cicero looking "with such ferret and such fiery eyes / As we have seen him in the Capitol, / Being crossed in conference by some senators": a 'ferret' has small red angry eyes, so Cicero looks furious; 'crossed in conference' means contradicted in debate. In Act 1 Scene 3 Casca reports the rumour that "the Senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king, / And he shall wear his crown by sea and land / In every place save here in Italy." Notice the odd detail: a king everywhere except Italy. This reflects a real ancient rumour that Caesar might be made king over the provinces while remaining a mere dictator at home; whatever its truth, Shakespeare uses it to show that the Senate, the guardian of the Republic, is itself feared to be about to betray it.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → The Senate was a lifelong council of ex-magistrates; it advised rather than commanded, but its prestige made it the real centre of government. Caesar packed it with his own men.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → A Senate that voted honours to one man was surrendering the Republic. A rumour that senators would crown Caesar would sound like the guardians handing over the keys.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → Casca's rumour that "the Senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king" is the fuse that lights Cassius's dagger speech. Shakespeare never confirms the rumour; the conspirators act on fear, not fact.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → The Senate meeting on the Ides (Act 3 Scene 1) is the climax of the play. Knowing that the senators were expected to crown Caesar that day explains why the murder happens there and then, and why the conspirators believe they are acting for the Senate's own honour.
```

### Consuls

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** At the top of the yearly ladder of offices stood two consuls. Two, never one: each could veto the other, and each served for exactly one year, after which he could not normally hold the office again for a decade. The consuls commanded armies, presided over the Senate and gave their names to the year. The whole design is a machine for preventing kingship: split the top job in half and make it expire every twelve months.

Caesar was consul in 59 BC, and again in 48, 46, 45 and 44 BC, the last time with Mark Antony as his colleague. The historical Antony who runs in the Lupercalia in Act 1 Scene 2 is therefore not a young playboy but the serving consul of Rome, one of the two highest officials in the state, stripped for a religious footrace and, according to Plutarch, offering a crown to his colleague. A Roman would have found the sight both comic and alarming.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Shakespeare never uses the word 'consul' in Act 1, and does not tell you Antony holds the office. Instead he shows you the relationship. When Caesar gives an order, Antony replies, "I shall remember. / When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed." Historically the two men were equal in rank; in the play one commands and the other obeys. That is the point. The Republic's most carefully designed safeguard, two equal consuls, has silently collapsed into master and servant.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Two consuls, elected annually, each able to block the other; the office existed to make one-man rule impossible. In 44 BC the consuls were Caesar and Antony.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → A consul was the embodiment of shared power. A consul who behaved as another man's servant showed that the safeguard had failed.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → Antony's "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed" turns a constitutional equal into an instrument. Shakespeare shows the collapse of the system through tone, not through a history lesson.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → Antony's absolute loyalty here is the seed of his revenge in Act 3 and his rise in Acts 4 and 5. If a question asks how Antony is presented before the assassination, this single line is your evidence.
```

### Tribunes of the Plebs: Flavius and Marullus

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The tribunes of the plebs were the strangest and, for our purposes, the most important office in Rome. They were created after the plebeians, the ordinary citizens, walked out of the city in protest in 494 BC (the 'first secession of the plebs'). To bring them back, the aristocracy agreed that the plebeians could elect their own officers, whose job was to protect them from abuse by magistrates. By the late Republic there were ten tribunes a year, and only a plebeian could hold the office.

Three features made the tribune unique. First, **sacrosanctity**: the person of a tribune was sacred, and anyone who laid hands on him could be killed without trial. Second, the **veto** (Latin *intercessio*, 'stepping between'): a single tribune could halt a law, an election or a decree of the Senate simply by saying no. Third, the **right of help** (*ius auxilii*): any citizen threatened by a magistrate could appeal to a tribune, who would physically interpose himself. The tribune was, in short, the people's shield against the powerful. Ambitious men later discovered that the office could be used for their own ends, and both Caesar and Pompey relied on friendly tribunes, but the original idea never faded: the tribune stood for the ordinary Roman against the great.

In 44 BC two tribunes, Gaius Epidius Marullus and Lucius Caesetius Flavus, removed a diadem (a white royal headband) that someone had placed on a statue of Caesar, and arrested men who hailed Caesar as *rex*, 'king'. Plutarch says the crowd cheered them and called them 'Brutuses', after the man who had expelled the last king. Caesar had them stripped of their office; Suetonius adds that they were removed from the Senate. Note the date: by most modern reconstructions this happened in January 44 BC, before the Lupercalia, though Plutarch narrates the two events together, which is what Shakespeare read.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Act 1 Scene 1 belongs to the tribunes. They open the play with a rebuke that is really a piece of Roman law: "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home! / Is this a holiday? What, know you not, / Being mechanical, you ought not walk / Upon a labouring day without the sign / Of your profession?" ('Mechanical' means a manual worker; 'the sign of your profession' means the tools and apron that identified a tradesman.) Two guardians of the people are scolding the very people they exist to protect, because those people are cheering a man who threatens the Republic. That is the bitter irony of the scene.

Their method is Roman to the core. Flavius orders: "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies." ('Images' are statues; 'ceremonies' here are the decorations, scarves and trophies hung on them.) Marullus hesitates, "May we do so? / You know it is the feast of Lupercal", and Flavius overrides him: "It is no matter. Let no images / Be hung with Caesar's trophies." This is precisely what the historical tribunes did: they stripped royal symbols from Caesar's statues. Their motive is stated in the closing image of the scene: "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch, / Who else would soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness." (A 'pitch' is the height to which a hawk soars; 'servile' means slave-like.) Pull out the feathers of popular support and Caesar will fly at an ordinary height like any other Roman.

Then Shakespeare delivers the consequence with terrible economy. In Scene 2, Casca mentions almost in passing that "Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence." The phrase is deliberately vague. Historically they lost their office; in the play the words could mean dismissal, exile or death, and Shakespeare leaves it open. What is not open is the meaning: the sacred, untouchable protectors of the people have been silenced by one man, and nobody in the play protests. The Republic's immune system has been switched off.

::: textnote The tribunes' names
The historical tribunes were Gaius Epidius Marullus and Lucius Caesetius Flavus. North's Plutarch gave Shakespeare 'Flavius' and 'Marullus'; the First Folio prints 'Murellus', which some modern editions keep. Your ICSE edition almost certainly prints 'Marullus', and that is the form used throughout this book. It is worth knowing the variant only so that a different spelling in another book does not confuse you.
:::

::: insight Why the tribunes are the Republic's immune system
A school guide will tell you that Flavius and Marullus 'represent opposition to Caesar'. That is true and shallow. The deeper point is what kind of opposition they represent. A tribune was sacrosanct: to harm him was sacrilege. His veto could stop the Senate itself. He existed for one purpose, to stand between the ordinary Roman and the powerful. If any office could resist a would-be king by legal means, it was this one. So when Shakespeare shows the tribunes acting exactly as the constitution intended, stripping royal decorations from statues and dispersing an illegal holiday, and then has them "put to silence" within a single scene, he is demonstrating that the Republic's legal defences have already failed before the play's third scene begins. This is why Cassius can argue in Scene 3 that the only remedy left is the dagger: "I know where I will wear this dagger then." Read this way, Scene 1 is not a comic warm-up; it is a proof that lawful resistance is dead. Many critics also note the irony that the people's protectors are shouting at the people. One strong reading is that Shakespeare uses this to introduce, in the first minute, the theme that dominates Act 3: the crowd cannot be relied upon to defend its own liberty, and those who try to defend it on the crowd's behalf are destroyed.
:::

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Tribunes of the plebs were sacrosanct officers with a veto and a duty to protect ordinary citizens. In 44 BC the tribunes Marullus and Flavus removed a diadem from Caesar's statue and were deposed by Caesar.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → Deposing a tribune was an assault on the sacred protection of the people; it showed that Caesar's power now stood above the law itself.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → Scene 1 shows the tribunes doing their constitutional duty ("Disrobe the images"); Scene 2 disposes of them in one line ("are put to silence"). Lawful resistance is shown to be useless, which drives the conspiracy toward violence.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → Class IX extract questions on Scene 1 often ask why the tribunes are angry and what they intend to do about the decorated statues. For Class X, the silencing of the tribunes is your earliest evidence that Caesar is a threat to liberty, which is what Brutus argues in Act 3 Scene 2.
```

### Patricians

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The patricians were the old aristocratic clans of Rome, families who claimed descent from the founding fathers of the city. In the early Republic they monopolised the priesthoods, the Senate and the consulship. Over two centuries the plebeians fought their way into every office, and by the late Republic patrician birth carried prestige rather than legal privilege. Caesar himself was a patrician of the ancient Julian clan, which claimed descent from the goddess Venus through Aeneas. Note the twist for the play: the man accused of wanting to be king was of the most ancient blood in Rome, while Brutus, Cassius and Antony, the men who surround him in the play, belonged to families that had risen from the plebeian side into the nobility.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT AND INTERPRETATION.** Shakespeare does not use the word 'patrician' in Act 1, but he plays on ancestry constantly. Cassius calls Aeneas "our great ancestor" (Aeneas was the Trojan hero who, in Virgil's *Aeneid*, carried his aged father Anchises out of burning Troy and became the legendary forefather of the Romans) when describing how he carried the exhausted Caesar out of the Tiber "as Aeneas, our great ancestor, / Did from the flames of Troy upon his shoulder / The old Anchises bear". The joke, if you know Rome, is sharp: Aeneas was Caesar's family's special ancestor, and Cassius casts himself as the hero and Caesar as the feeble old father being carried. Cassius steals Caesar's own pedigree.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Patricians were the ancient aristocratic clans; by 44 BC their status was prestige, not power. Caesar was a patrician whose family claimed descent from Venus and Aeneas.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → Noble birth gave a man standing, but the Republic expected him to earn his place through service; birth alone was not a licence to rule.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → Cassius's comparison of himself to "Aeneas, our great ancestor" quietly appropriates Caesar's own family legend and recasts Caesar as the helpless Anchises.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → In character questions on Cassius, the Tiber anecdote is standard evidence of his envy; knowing the Aeneas detail lets you add a genuinely analytical point about how he manipulates ancestry.
```

### Plebeians

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The plebeians were every Roman citizen who was not a patrician: rich merchants and poor labourers alike. In the early Republic they struggled for legal equality, winning the tribunate, access to the consulship and finally, in 287 BC, the rule that the decisions of the plebeian assembly bound the whole state. By the first century BC, however, 'the plebs' in everyday speech had come to mean the urban poor of Rome: the crowds in the Forum, dependent on the grain supply, entertained by games, and courted by ambitious politicians with money, food and spectacle. Caesar built his career on their support.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** The commoners of Act 1 Scene 1 are Shakespeare's plebeians. The Carpenter and the Cobbler speak prose while the tribunes speak verse, a class marker an Elizabethan audience heard instantly. The Cobbler's puns are cheerful and clever, and he is not stupid. He explains that they "make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph." Marullus's fury is aimed at their fickleness: the same people once cheered Pompey until "Tiber trembled underneath her banks", and now they "strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood". Casca's report in Scene 2 pushes the contempt further. He calls the crowd "the rabblement" (a mob), "the tag-rag people" (ragged riff-raff) and "the common herd", and says they clapped and hissed Caesar "as they use to do the players in the theatre". Note that all of this contempt comes from aristocratic mouths; Shakespeare lets you decide whether it is fair.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Plebeians were the non-patrician citizens; by the late Republic the word usually meant the urban poor, whose support Caesar had cultivated for years.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → The plebs were the sovereign people in theory and a manageable crowd in practice; whoever fed and entertained them could count on their cheers.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → The prose-speaking commoners of Scene 1, and Casca's "rabblement" and "tag-rag people" in Scene 2, establish a crowd that is loud, changeable and easily played to, long before Antony plays to it in Act 3.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → The theme of the fickle mob runs from Act 1 Scene 1 to the murder of Cinna the poet in Act 3 Scene 3. Marullus's speech is the first evidence for that theme and should be in your notes for any question on the crowd.
```

### Roman Citizenship

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** To be a Roman citizen (*civis Romanus*) was a legal status with real content: the right to vote in the assemblies, to stand for office, to make a legally valid marriage, to own property under Roman law, and, most cherished of all, protection against arbitrary punishment by a magistrate. A citizen could not lawfully be flogged or executed without trial; a slave could be. The gulf between citizen and slave was the deepest line in Roman society, and citizenship was jealously guarded, then gradually extended: to all Italians after a bitter war (the Social War of 91–88 BC), and by Caesar to the people of northern Italy in 49 BC.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Listen to how often Act 1 treats being Roman as a birthright of freedom. Cassius insists, "I was born free as Caesar; so were you". In Scene 3, challenged in the dark with "Who's there?", Casca answers simply, "A Roman." Cassius's whole rhetoric works by contrasting citizens with slaves: he describes Romans "groaning underneath this age's yoke" (a yoke is the wooden bar laid across the necks of oxen), says "Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish" ('sufferance' means patient endurance), and speaks of himself as being in "bondage". To a Roman, a citizen in bondage is a contradiction in terms; that contradiction is what Cassius wants Brutus and Casca to feel.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Roman citizenship guaranteed votes, legal marriage, property rights and protection from summary punishment; the citizen-slave divide was absolute.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → A free citizen who had to bow to one man had been reduced to something like a slave; the language of bondage was the strongest insult a Roman could feel.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → "I was born free as Caesar", "A Roman.", "this age's yoke" and "bondage": Cassius builds his case entirely on the shame of a citizen living like a slave.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → When Brutus tells the crowd in Act 3 Scene 2 that Caesar's death was the price of their freedom, he is using exactly the citizen-versus-slave logic Cassius planted in Act 1. Quote Act 1 to show the argument's origin.
```

## The Roman Mind: Values That Drive the Play

### Roman Political Culture: dignitas, gloria, auctoritas and the mos maiorum

Four Latin words explain more about the behaviour of Caesar, Brutus and Cassius than any plot summary. Learn them simply.

| Latin word | Simple meaning | How it drives Act 1 |
|---|---|---|
| *dignitas* | A man's standing and worth in the eyes of others; his rank made visible | Caesar went to war in 49 BC rather than lose his dignitas; Cassius cannot bear to "bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him" |
| *gloria* | Glory won by public deeds, especially in war, and remembered after death | The triumph in Scene 1 is gloria made into a parade; Cassius resents that Caesar should "bear the palm alone" |
| *auctoritas* | Moral authority; the weight a man's word carries without force | Brutus has auctoritas: Casca says his "countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness" (alchemy: the medieval dream of turning base metal into gold) |
| *mos maiorum* | 'The way of the ancestors': the unwritten code of how things are done | Cassius's appeal to "our fathers" and the first Brutus; Casca's belief that omens must be feared as "It is the part of men" |

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Roman politics was a competition for honour among a few hundred families. A Roman noble measured his life by *dignitas* and *gloria*, and the Republic worked because the competition was regulated: offices were shared, terms were short, and the *mos maiorum* set limits that no ambitious man was supposed to cross. Caesar himself wrote that his *dignitas* had always been dearer to him than life; that sentence explains the civil war. *Auctoritas* is subtler: it is the authority a man has because of who he is, so that people follow his lead without being ordered. The Senate had it as a body; a few individuals, such as Cicero, had it personally.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Notice how precisely Shakespeare distributes these qualities. Caesar has dignitas and gloria in excess. Cassius has wounded dignitas and no gloria of his own, which is why he keeps measuring himself against Caesar: "Weigh them, it is as heavy". Brutus has auctoritas, the one thing the conspiracy lacks, which is exactly why Cassius and Cinna need him: "O Cassius, if you could / But win the noble Brutus to our party". And the whole conspiracy justifies itself by the mos maiorum, the ancestors' hatred of kings. Once you see this, Act 1 becomes a study in the collision of four Roman values, not a set of personal grudges.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Roman nobles competed for dignitas (standing), gloria (glory) and auctoritas (moral authority) within the limits set by the mos maiorum (ancestral custom).
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → A man whose dignitas was slighted had a duty to defend it; a man whose gloria outgrew the Republic was a danger to everyone else's.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → Cassius's resentment ("must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him") is wounded dignitas; the conspirators' need for Brutus is a need for auctoritas; their appeal to "our fathers" is the mos maiorum.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → These four words give you an analytical vocabulary for character questions that most students lack. Use them sparingly and explain them in one phrase each; examiners reward precision, not Latin for its own sake.
```

### Republicanism

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Republicanism, in the Roman sense, is the conviction that the state must belong to its citizens and that no individual may stand above the law. Its practical rules were the ones you have met: shared offices, annual terms, the veto, the Senate's collective wisdom, the people's vote. Its emotional core was pride: Romans believed their Republic had conquered the Mediterranean precisely because it was free, while the kingdoms of the East had fallen because they depended on one man.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Brutus is the play's republican conscience, and Act 1 gives you his creed in one exchange. Hearing the crowd shout, he says, "I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." Cassius pounces: "Ay, do you fear it? / Then must I think you would not have it so." Brutus's reply is the moral centre of the whole play: "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well." A republican may love a man and still refuse to let him be king. Brutus then defines his standard: "If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently". ('Aught' means anything; 'the general good' means the public interest, which is the res publica itself put into English.)

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Roman republicanism held that the state belonged to the citizens and that no individual could stand above the law; Romans credited their freedom for their greatness.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → Preferring the public good to private affection was the highest Roman virtue; the Republic's founding legend (below) was built on exactly that choice.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well" and "If it be aught toward the general good" make Brutus's republicanism personal and painful: he must choose between a friend and a principle.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → Brutus's Act 3 Scene 2 argument that he loved Caesar but loved Rome more is the direct fulfilment of this Act 1 line. A strong Class X answer on Brutus's motives quotes both moments.
```

### Roman Ideas of Liberty: libertas

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** *Libertas* did not mean 'doing as you please'. For a Roman it meant, first, not being a slave, and second, not being subject to the arbitrary will of a master (*dominatio*). A free people lived under laws it had made, spoke its mind in the Senate and the assembly, and could not be punished at one man's whim. The symbol of libertas was the *pileus*, the soft cap given to a slave on the day he was freed. After the assassination, Brutus issued a coin showing that cap between two daggers with the legend EID MAR, 'the Ides of March': liberty, in the conspirators' own iconography, was a freed slave's cap won by the knife.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Shakespeare's Cassius thinks in exactly these terms. He calls himself a slave of Caesar's greatness, "A wretched creature", who must bow "If Caesar carelessly but nod on him". His dagger speech in Scene 3 defines liberty as the power to end one's own subjection: "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" and "That part of tyranny that I do bear / I can shake off at pleasure." Casca answers with the freed slave's logic: "So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity." (A 'bondman' is a slave; to 'cancel' a captivity is to annul it as one cancels a debt.) The words 'bondage', 'bondman', 'yoke' and 'tyranny' all belong to the Roman vocabulary of libertas.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Libertas meant freedom from a master's arbitrary will and from slavery; its symbol was the freed slave's cap, which Brutus later stamped on his coins beside two daggers.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → A citizen living under one man's nod had lost libertas whatever his wealth or rank; regaining it justified extreme action.
↓
```

### Fear of Monarchy: 509 BC and the First Brutus

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Roman tradition held that the seventh and last king, Tarquin the Proud (Tarquinius Superbus), was a tyrant whose son raped a noblewoman, Lucretia. Lucius Junius Brutus, the king's own nephew, led the uprising that expelled the Tarquins in 509 BC, swore the people never to allow a king in Rome again, and became one of the first two consuls. The story continues darkly: when Brutus's own sons plotted to restore the king, he condemned them to death and watched the execution. Whatever its historical truth, this legend was Rome's founding myth of freedom, and it taught two lessons: the word *rex* is poison, and a true Roman puts the Republic above his own family.

Marcus Brutus, Shakespeare's Brutus, claimed descent from this man, and the people of 44 BC expected him to live up to the name. Ancient writers already noticed a difficulty in the pedigree, since the first Brutus was said to have killed his sons, but the claim was made and believed. The graffiti Plutarch reports on Brutus's praetor's chair (a praetor was the second-highest annual magistrate, a senior judge; Brutus and Cassius both held the office in 44 BC) and his ancestor's statue, messages to the effect of 'Brutus, are you asleep?' and 'You are no true Brutus', show how heavily the name weighed on him.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Cassius ends his great persuasion in Scene 2 with the ancestor: "O, you and I have heard our fathers say / There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king." ('Brooked' means tolerated; 'keep his state' means hold court in majesty.) The first Brutus would as soon have tolerated the Devil himself ruling Rome as a king. In Scene 3 Cassius tells Cinna to set a forged letter "with wax / Upon old Brutus' statue": Shakespeare has taken Plutarch's anonymous graffiti and made Cassius their author. Meanwhile, in Cassius's mouth, 'king' and 'tyrant' are treated as one word: "And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then?"

::: history The word rex
Rome's hatred of the title *rex* was so deep that even when Rome had emperors, from Augustus onward, they never called themselves kings. They ruled as 'first citizen' (*princeps*) and 'commander' (*imperator*), preserving the forms of the Republic because the word 'king' remained unsayable. Caesar's fatal error, in the eyes of many ancient and modern writers, was not his power but his failure to disguise it: the perpetual dictatorship, the royal-looking honours and the rumours of a crown all touched the one nerve that could not be touched.
:::

::: insight Why the hatred of the word king is the engine of the whole play
Ask yourself what the conspirators actually accuse Caesar of in Act 1. Not cruelty: nobody claims he has killed or robbed anyone. Not incompetence: he has just won a war. Not even present tyranny: Brutus himself admits "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well", and Cassius has to strain to find grievances, ending with an embarrassing swimming race and a fever. The whole case against Caesar rests on a fear about a word. Brutus fears "the people / Choose Caesar for their king". Casca reports a rumour that the senators "Mean to establish Caesar as a king". Cassius invokes the ancestor who would not brook "a king". The word does the work that evidence cannot. This is Shakespeare's most profound use of Roman history: he has understood that in Rome the title itself was a crime, so that a man could be killed for what he might become rather than for what he had done. Every later event grows from this root. Brutus's soliloquy in Act 2, Antony's repeated refrain about Caesar refusing the crown in Act 3, and the tragic irony that killing Caesar produced not liberty but emperors, are all variations on a single theme announced in Act 1: a Republic so terrified of a king that it destroyed itself to prevent one. A literary interpretation, but one that most critics share in some form.
:::

::: level LEVEL 1 — What happened?
In Scene 2 Casca reports that Antony offered Caesar a crown three times at the Lupercalia; Caesar refused it three times, more reluctantly each time in Casca's opinion, and the crowd cheered each refusal. Caesar then fainted. Earlier, Brutus heard the shouts and feared the people were choosing Caesar as king.
:::

::: level LEVEL 2 — What does it mean?
The crowd cheered the refusals, not the offers. Roman citizens, however much they loved Caesar, would not accept a king. Caesar's reluctance to let go, "he was very loath to lay his fingers off it", suggests to Casca and Cassius that Caesar wants the crown and is testing the public mood. Whether Casca's cynical reading is fair, the text leaves open.
:::

::: level LEVEL 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way?
He keeps the offering offstage and lets a sour, sarcastic witness describe it. We never see Caesar's face; we see Casca's contempt. The audience is put in the conspirators' position of interpreting rumours. Shakespeare also enlarges Plutarch's two offerings to three, the fairy-tale number, so the refusal feels like a ritual performance rather than a spontaneous act.
:::

::: level LEVEL 4 — How does it connect to the whole play?
The crown refused in Act 1 becomes the crown Antony reminds the crowd of in Act 3 Scene 2, recalling that he offered it three times, to prove Caesar was not ambitious. The same crowd that cheered the refusal will cheer Brutus and then Antony. And the Republic's horror of a king, so evident here, is what Brutus will use to justify the killing.
:::

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
In a Class X question about Antony's funeral speech or Brutus's motives, one sentence of Act 1 context earns credit: Caesar had publicly refused a crown three times, so the conspirators' fear rested on interpretation of his reluctance, not on any act of kingship. Say 'Casca reports' rather than 'Caesar did', because you are quoting a biased witness, and the examiner will see that you understand the difference.
:::

### Roman Attitudes Toward Powerful Individuals

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Romans admired great men and feared them in equal measure. The Republic honoured its heroes lavishly, with triumphs, statues and titles, but it also invented safeguards against them: term limits, colleagues with vetoes, and the memory of the Tarquins. When a man's greatness grew beyond what the system could contain, Romans tended to see not a saviour but a threat, and their history offered a stock of warnings: the would-be tyrants of the early Republic supposedly struck down for reaching too high. By the first century BC that older suspicion was fighting a new reality, in which armies loved their generals and the poor loved their benefactors more than they loved the constitution.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Cassius's Colossus image is the play's picture of an individual grown too large for his state: "Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves." (The Colossus was the giant bronze statue of the sun-god at Rhodes, one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world; the picture of it straddling the harbour so that ships sailed between its legs is a medieval legend, and it is that legend Shakespeare's image relies on. 'Bestride' means to stand with legs apart over something; 'petty' means small.) He follows it with the crucial republican axiom: "Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man." A Rome with room for only one man is no longer Rome. Caesar's own words from the other side of the fence complete the picture: "Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". Caesar accepts, without embarrassment, that he is the greater; Shakespeare lets the audience judge the arrogance in "for always I am Caesar."

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Rome honoured great men with triumphs and statues while fencing them in with term limits, colleagues and the memory of the expelled kings.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → A single individual whose greatness outgrew the Republic's safeguards was a danger by definition, whatever his personal qualities.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → The Colossus image and "Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man" express the republican fear of the outsized individual; Caesar's "for always I am Caesar" shows he thinks of himself in the third person, as an institution.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → Questions on Caesar's character (arrogance, over-confidence) in Act 2 Scene 2 and Act 3 Scene 1 are best answered with his Act 1 self-description. His third-person habit begins here.
```

## The Century That Broke the Republic

You now know how the Republic was meant to work. Here is how it stopped working, told briefly and only as far as Act 1 needs it.

### Military Power and Political Power: Marius and Sulla

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** For most of the Republic's history, the army was the citizen body in arms: farmers who served a campaign and went home. In the late second century BC the general Gaius Marius is traditionally credited with opening the legions to the landless poor, who served for pay, loot and the promise of land, and who therefore looked to their general, not to the Senate, to reward them. Marius held the consulship an unheard-of seven times. His former officer Lucius Cornelius Sulla then did what no Roman had done: in 88 BC he marched his army on Rome itself, and in 82–81 BC he made himself dictator without a time limit, killed his enemies by published lists (the proscriptions), rewrote the constitution and, to general astonishment, resigned. Two lessons were now on the record. A general with a loyal army could take Rome. And a dictator could hold power for as long as he chose. Caesar, as a young man, was on Sulla's wrong side; Suetonius and Plutarch both report that Sulla warned there were many Mariuses in the boy.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Shakespeare never names Marius or Sulla, but their legacy is the unspoken fact behind Act 1: Caesar's power rests on soldiers, and the crowd in Scene 1 is cheering a military victory. When Marullus asks "What conquest brings he home?" the question exposes the real source of Caesar's standing. Cassius's line that Caesar is "A man no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action, yet prodigious grown" makes the same point in reverse: strip away the army and the aura, and he is only a man. ('Prodigious' here means monstrous, unnatural, like a prodigy or omen.)

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → From Marius onward, Roman soldiers served long terms for pay and land and gave their loyalty to their general; Sulla proved in 88–81 BC that such an army could seize Rome and keep a dictator in power.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → Every great general was now a potential Sulla; a returning victor with a devoted army was as frightening as he was glorious.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → The triumph in Scene 1 is a military victor's parade; Marullus's "What conquest brings he home?" and Cassius's "prodigious grown" both point to the army-based power behind Caesar's civilian mask.

WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → When Acts 4 and 5 turn into a war between the conspirators' legions and those of Antony and Octavius, you are watching the Marius-and-Sulla pattern repeat. Naming that pattern in one sentence lifts an answer above plot summary.
```

### Political Factions: Optimates, Caesarians and the Pardoned Pompeians

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Rome had no political parties in the modern sense, but historians use two convenient labels. The *optimates* ('the best men') were those senators who defended the traditional authority of the Senate and the old families. The *populares* ('the people's men') were politicians who bypassed the Senate by appealing directly to the popular assemblies and the tribunes. Caesar built his career as a popularis; Pompey, though he too had been a popular strongman, ended up as the Senate's champion against Caesar. When civil war came in 49 BC, the optimates and Pompey were on one side and the Caesarians on the other.

Two things about the conspiracy of 44 BC now become clear. First, Brutus and Cassius had fought for Pompey, been defeated at Pharsalus, and been pardoned by Caesar; they held their praetorships in 44 BC by his favour. Second, the conspiracy also included Caesar's own trusted officers, above all Decimus Brutus (Shakespeare's Decius Brutus) and Trebonius. The plot united former enemies and former friends, which is why it was so hard to detect and so lacking in a shared plan for afterwards.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Shakespeare drops the faction labels but keeps the human situation. Cassius admits "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus" ('bears me hard' means dislikes me, bears a grudge against me), an acknowledgement that the two pardoned men stand in different favour. The meeting place he names, "In Pompey's Porch", is the portico of the theatre built by Caesar's dead rival: the pardoned Pompeians gather, literally, under Pompey's roof. And Cinna's question in the dark, "Who's that? Metellus Cimber?", together with the roll-call of "Decius Brutus and Trebonius", quietly shows an audience that Caesar's own men are among the plotters.

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → Late-Republican politics divided into defenders of senatorial authority (optimates) and those who appealed to the people (populares); after 49 BC the practical split was Pompeians against Caesarians. Brutus and Cassius were pardoned Pompeians; Decimus Brutus and Trebonius were Caesarians.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → A pardoned enemy owed his life and his office to Caesar; a plot by such men would be seen either as the last stand of the Republic or as monstrous ingratitude.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus" and the meeting at "Pompey's Porch" keep the Pompeian past alive; the names Decius Brutus and Trebonius show the plot reaching into Caesar's own circle.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → Antony's charge of ingratitude in Act 3 Scene 2, and Caesar's shock at Brutus in Act 3 Scene 1, both depend on the fact that these men were forgiven enemies and trusted friends. Act 1 plants that fact.
```

### Caesar's Rise: the First Triumvirate, the Consulship and Gaul

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** In 60 BC Caesar formed a private alliance with the two most powerful men in Rome: Pompey, the greatest general of the age, and Crassus, the richest man in it. Historians call it the First Triumvirate, though it had no legal standing; it was a pact to share influence and get each other what they wanted. It got Caesar the consulship of 59 BC, and then a five-year command in Gaul (roughly modern France), later extended. From 58 to 50 BC Caesar conquered the whole of Gaul, defeated the German tribes on the Rhine, twice invaded Britain and broke the last great Gallic revolt at Alesia in 52 BC. He wrote up his campaigns himself, in plain, powerful Latin, so that Rome read his victories as he wished them read. By 50 BC he had a battle-hardened army, vast wealth and unmatched glory, and a Senate that wanted to prosecute him the moment he laid down his command.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Cassius sneers at the way Caesar's words were treated as scripture: "that tongue of his that bade the Romans / Mark him and write his speeches in their books". Whatever Shakespeare knew of Caesar's writings, the line catches a truth: Caesar controlled his own image. The Gallic glory hangs over Act 1 without ever being described; the shouting that twice interrupts Brutus and Cassius, and the "new honours that are heaped on Caesar" that Brutus guesses it means, are its consequence. (One caution: the "universal shout" Marullus remembers in Scene 1 was raised for Pompey's chariot, not Caesar's. Do not borrow that phrase for Caesar's popularity.)


### Caesar and Pompey: Alliance, Julia, Breakdown, the Rubicon, Pharsalus, Death

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Pompey the Great was Rome's hero before Caesar was. He had cleared the Mediterranean of pirates, conquered the East and celebrated three triumphs, the third and grandest in 61 BC. To bind their alliance, Caesar gave Pompey his only daughter, Julia, in marriage in 59 BC; by all accounts the two were devoted, and Julia's death in childbirth in 54 BC removed the personal bond between the two men. Crassus died in battle at Carrhae in 53 BC, and the triangle collapsed into two rivals. The Senate, fearing Caesar more, drew Pompey to its side and in January 49 BC ordered Caesar to give up his army. Two tribunes who vetoed the order, Antony among them, fled to Caesar, giving him a legal pretext: the sacred tribunes had been violated. Around 10 or 11 January 49 BC Caesar led a legion across the Rubicon, the little river that marked the boundary of Italy, which no commander might cross with troops. Civil war followed. Pompey abandoned Italy for Greece; Caesar chased him and crushed his larger army at Pharsalus on 9 August 48 BC. Pompey fled to Egypt, where on 28 September 48 BC he was murdered as he stepped ashore, on the orders of the boy-king's advisers, who hoped to please Caesar. Caesar is said to have wept when shown the head.

::: history The Rubicon
The Rubicon was a small stream in northern Italy. Its importance was entirely legal: it marked the line beyond which a provincial governor could not bring his army without committing treason. Crossing it was the moment Caesar chose civil war over prosecution. The phrase 'to cross the Rubicon' still means to take an irreversible step. Shakespeare's play never mentions it, but it stands behind Marullus's grief: the man in the chariot is the man who crossed that line against Pompey.
:::

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Pompey has been dead for nearly four years when the play begins, and yet Act 1 cannot stop talking about him. Marullus's whole speech is a lament for him: "Knew you not Pompey? Many a time and oft / Have you climbed up to walls and battlements, / To towers and windows, yea, to chimney tops, / Your infants in your arms, and there have sat / The livelong day, with patient expectation, / To see great Pompey pass the streets of Rome." The crowd's crime, in Marullus's eyes, is that they now cheer the man who "comes in triumph over Pompey's blood". The conspirators meet at "Pompey's Porch" and "Pompey's Theatre". Shakespeare's Rome is haunted by Pompey, and in Act 3 the haunting will be completed when Caesar falls at the foot of Pompey's statue.

### Caesar's Military Achievements

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Between 58 and 45 BC Caesar fought, almost without pause, in Gaul, Germany, Britain, Italy, Spain, Greece, Egypt, Asia Minor, Africa and Spain again, and never lost a war. His reputation for speed became legendary; his three-word dispatch after a lightning victory in Asia Minor in 47 BC, 'I came, I saw, I conquered', was later carried in his triumph. Ancient writers also stress his personal endurance: he swam, rode and marched with his men and shared their hardships. That is worth remembering when you read Cassius.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT AND INTERPRETATION.** Cassius's strategy in Scene 2 is to attack the one thing Caesar's reputation rested on, his physical toughness. So he tells the Tiber story, in which Caesar "cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!'", and the fever story from Spain: "He had a fever when he was in Spain, / And when the fit was on him, I did mark / How he did shake." He sums up in disbelief that "A man of such a feeble temper should / So get the start of the majestic world / And bear the palm alone." ('Temper' means constitution or physical make-up; 'get the start of' means outstrip in a race; 'bear the palm' means take the victor's prize.) The Tiber race has no source in Plutarch and appears to be Shakespeare's invention. The Spanish fever may grow from Plutarch's remark that Caesar's epilepsy first attacked him at Corduba in Spain, but the shaking, groaning invalid crying out like "a sick girl" is Cassius's picture, not the historian's. Either way, the point is that Cassius must belittle the body of a man whose achievements he cannot deny. Casca's report of the fainting fit, and Brutus's diagnosis "he hath the falling sickness" (epilepsy, which Plutarch does record), serve the same purpose. Set against the historical Caesar's record, Cassius's belittling looks like envy; that is one strong reading, though some critics answer that his underlying point, a mortal man being worshipped as a god, is fair. The text lets you weigh both.

### Caesar's Return to Rome

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** After Pharsalus Caesar spent years finishing the war: Egypt and Cleopatra (48–47 BC), the victory at Zela (47 BC), the defeat of the Pompeians in Africa at Thapsus (46 BC), where Cato killed himself rather than accept pardon, and finally the crushing of Pompey's sons at Munda in Spain on 17 March 45 BC. He returned to Rome in the autumn of 45 BC and celebrated a triumph for Spain in October. He then set about reforming the state at breathtaking speed: a new calendar (the Julian calendar, ancestor of ours), debt relief, colonies for veterans and the poor, an enlarged Senate. The Senate, for its part, heaped honours on him that looked increasingly royal: his statue placed among those of the old kings, his image on coins during his lifetime (a first for a living Roman), a golden chair, the right to wear triumphal dress permanently. Which honours he sought and which were forced on him by flatterers is a question the ancient sources already argued about.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Shakespeare compresses the return into a single day of celebration and stitches it to the Lupercalia. Brutus hears the shouting and guesses "these applauses are / For some new honours that are heaped on Caesar." That word 'heaped' is exactly right for the winter of 45–44 BC. Caesar's own entrance, "Set on and leave no ceremony out", shows a man at ease with public ritual arranged around himself.

### Caesar's Dictatorship: 49 → 46 → February 44 BC

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The dictatorship was an ancient emergency office. In a crisis the Republic could appoint one man with supreme power, but for six months at most, after which he stepped down; the legend of Cincinnatus, who left his plough, saved Rome and returned to his plough within sixteen days, taught every Roman child what a dictator should be. Sulla had bent the rule; Caesar broke it. He was named dictator briefly in 49 BC to hold elections, again in 48–47 BC for a year, then in 46 BC for ten years, and in February 44 BC *dictator perpetuo*, dictator for life. A perpetual dictator was a king without the name. The Lupercalia fell on 15 February 44 BC, within days or weeks of that appointment, which is why the crown offered at the festival looked to many like the next logical step.

::: history The six-month rule
Why did six months matter so much? Because the dictatorship was the one office in which the Republic's safeguards were suspended: no colleague of equal rank, no effective veto, and in the old tradition no appeal to the people. The time limit was the only protection left. A dictator for life had removed the last brake. Cicero's speeches and letters from these months show educated Romans grasping precisely this point.
:::

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Shakespeare never uses the word 'dictator' in Act 1, and he does not date the events. Yet the reality shows through in Casca's report that Caesar "put it by" but "would fain have had it" ('fain' means gladly), and in Cassius's cry "Age, thou art shamed!" A generation that has accepted a perpetual dictator has, in Cassius's eyes, disgraced itself. The vagueness suits Shakespeare's purpose: his Caesar is not a specific constitutional office but a man on the edge of becoming something Rome has no name for except the forbidden one.

## Two Ceremonies the Play Is Built On

### The Roman Triumph

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** A triumph was the highest honour the Republic could give a general, and the Senate granted it only for a major victory over a foreign enemy. The procession began outside the city on the Campus Martius and wound through the streets to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline hill. First came the senators and magistrates, then wagons of captured treasure and painted boards showing the battles, then the animals for sacrifice, then the chief captives in chains, and finally the general himself in a chariot drawn by four horses, wearing a purple toga embroidered with gold, a laurel wreath on his head and, by tradition, his face painted red like the statue of Jupiter. A slave stood behind him holding a golden crown over his head and, according to a later source, murmuring that he should look behind him and remember he was only a man. His soldiers marched after, singing; the crowd wore holiday clothes and scattered flowers. At the temple the general sacrificed white bulls and laid his laurels in the god's lap.

The rule about a foreign enemy is the key to Act 1. Romans regarded a triumph over fellow Romans as indecent: one did not parade citizens in chains or celebrate the slaughter of one's own people. Caesar's Spanish triumph of October 45 BC was exactly that, a celebration of Munda, where the enemy had been Pompey's sons and their Roman legions. Plutarch records that this triumph displeased Romans more than anything else Caesar had done, because he had destroyed the children and the family of the greatest of Romans, and it was not thought fitting for him to triumph over the misfortunes of his country. (I paraphrase Plutarch; those are not Shakespeare's words.)

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Now reread Marullus with a Roman's eyes: "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? / What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?" ('Wherefore' means why; 'tributaries' are conquered peoples who must pay tribute; 'captive bonds' are the chains of prisoners.) Marullus is asking the three questions any Roman would ask about a triumph. What foreign conquest? None. What captive kings walk in chains before the chariot? None, because the defeated were Romans. The triumph, in other words, is a fraud, and the crowd should know it. The answer to his rhetorical questions is the unspoken word 'Pompey', which he then speaks: "Knew you not Pompey?" Everything in the speech turns on the historical rule that you do not triumph over your own countrymen: the man "That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood" has broken a taboo, and the crowd's flowers make them complicit. Marullus therefore tells them to "Pray to the gods to intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude" ('intermit' means to suspend or hold off): a triumph that offends the gods invites divine punishment.

::: quote The fraudulent triumph
> **Marullus:** Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? / What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?

Three questions that a Roman would recognise as the checklist for a legitimate triumph, each answered by silence.
:::

### Lupercalia

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The Lupercalia was celebrated every year on 15 February, one of Rome's oldest festivals, so old that the Romans themselves were unsure what it meant. It centred on the Lupercal, the cave at the foot of the Palatine hill where the she-wolf was said to have suckled Romulus and Remus. Priests called Luperci sacrificed goats there, cut the skins into strips, and ran through the city wearing little more than goatskin girdles, striking anyone they met with the thongs. Women deliberately stood in their way, because a blow from the goat-thong was believed to cure barrenness and ease childbirth. The festival was at once a purification of the city (the month name February comes from *februa*, the purifying thongs) and a fertility rite. In 44 BC Caesar had a third college of Luperci, the Juliani, created in his honour, and Mark Antony, the serving consul, ran as one of them.

At that festival, according to Plutarch, Caesar sat on the speakers' platform in the Forum on a golden chair, dressed in triumphal robes. Antony ran up carrying a diadem, the white ribbon that Greek kings wore round the head, twined with laurel, and held it out to Caesar. A few people, apparently placed for the purpose, applauded; when Caesar pushed it away, the whole crowd applauded. Antony offered it again; the same thing happened. Plutarch has two offerings. Caesar then ordered the diadem to be carried to the Capitol and dedicated to Jupiter, as though to say that only the god was king in Rome.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Shakespeare keeps the festival and moves everything else into it. Caesar's first full speech in the play, once he has called his wife's name, is an instruction about the fertility rite: "Stand you directly in Antonius' way / When he doth run his course", and "Forget not in your speed, Antonius, / To touch Calpurnia, for our elders say / The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse." ('Barren' means childless; 'sterile curse' is the curse of infertility.) This is exactly the ancient belief, and Shakespeare uses it to tell us, without a word of explanation, that the master of the world has no heir. Antony's reply, "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed", makes the consul-priest a servant. Then comes the offering, reported by Casca: "there was a crown offered him; and, being offered him, he put it by with the back of his hand, thus, and then the people fell a-shouting." Casca even corrects himself on the object, "'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets" (a small crown), which is Shakespeare's way of rendering Plutarch's diadem, a ribbon rather than a crown, in terms an English audience would grasp. And when the crowd delights in the refusal, Casca says Caesar "plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut" ('ope' means open; a 'doublet' is a close-fitting jacket, an Elizabethan garment Shakespeare puts on a Roman), a theatrical gesture of surrender. Whether that gesture was sincere or staged is the question Casca's sarcasm plants in Brutus.

::: insight Why Shakespeare joins the triumph to the Lupercalia
Historically these two events were four months apart; Shakespeare makes them one day. The gain is enormous. The triumph gives him a cheering crowd, decorated statues and an angry pair of tribunes: the public face of Caesar's power. The Lupercalia gives him a religious festival, a childless ruler asking a priest to touch his wife, a soothsayer in the crowd and a crown offered in public: the private and superstitious face of the same power. By welding them together he can show, in two scenes, Caesar as conqueror, Caesar as would-be father, Caesar as nearly-king, and Caesar as a fainting epileptic, all before the audience has had time to settle on an opinion. He also buys a structural symmetry: the play opens with Caesar refusing a crown before a crowd and moves, in Act 3, to Antony using that refusal before another crowd. Compression is not carelessness; it is the dramatist choosing density over accuracy. This is literary interpretation, but it is the kind that follows directly from comparing the sources with the text.
:::

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → The Lupercalia (15 February) was an ancient purification and fertility festival; the Luperci ran through Rome striking women with goat-hide thongs. ::: history What the Ides actually were
```
The Roman month had three fixed marker days: the Kalends (the 1st), the Nones (the 5th, or the 7th in March, May, July and October) and the Ides (the 15th in those same four months, the 13th in all the others). The Ides were sacred to Jupiter. So "Beware the ides of March" would have sounded to a Roman exactly like 'beware the fifteenth of March': a date, not a riddle. The eeriness is Shakespeare's; to Caesar it is a calendar entry, which is partly why he can dismiss the speaker as "a dreamer". There is a historical root. Plutarch and Suetonius record that a seer named Spurinna warned Caesar of danger coming no later than the Ides of March; Shakespeare strips away the name and gives the warning to a nameless Soothsayer in the crowd. The ICSE 2025 board paper asked candidates what 'the work the ides of March begun' refers to: a student who knows that the Ides is simply 15 March, the day of the assassination, answers in one clean sentence.
:::

## WHAT SHAKESPEARE CHANGED

This section is the one that will most distinguish you from other students. Shakespeare read history carefully and then altered it deliberately. Below, each change is set out as **HISTORICAL RECORD** against **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT**, followed by the likely reason (**LITERARY INTERPRETATION**). Keep these labels in your own notes; confusing the two is the most common error in student writing about this play.

### Three events compressed into one day

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The Spanish triumph took place in October 45 BC. The tribunes' removal of the diadem from Caesar's statue is placed by most modern reconstructions in January 44 BC. The Lupercalia was 15 February 44 BC. Three separate events, spread over four months.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** In Act 1 Scenes 1 and 2 they all happen on a single day. The commoners "make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph"; Marullus says "it is the feast of Lupercal"; the tribunes strip the statues; Antony runs his course; the crown is offered; and by the end of Scene 2 the tribunes "are put to silence".

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Compression gives the opening of the play a single, overwhelming day of Caesar's glory, and it lets cause and consequence sit side by side: the tribunes act in Scene 1 and are destroyed in Scene 2. It also means Shakespeare can show the fickle crowd, the childless ruler, the refused crown and the first stirrings of conspiracy within the first forty minutes of stage time.

### The silent month before Scene 3

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** From the Lupercalia (15 February) to the Ides (15 March) is one month.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Scene 2 ends with Cassius planning to throw letters "this night"; Scene 3 opens in a storm, and Cicero asks "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" Casca replies that Caesar "did bid Antonius / Send word to you he would be there tomorrow." Cassius says "it is after midnight". So Scene 3 is the night of 14 March. A whole month has passed between two consecutive scenes, and nothing in the dialogue marks it. Some editors and readers feel that Scene 3 follows Scene 2 on the same night; the text supports the month-long gap through Cicero's question, but leaves the sensation of continuity intact.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Shakespeare wants the conspiracy to feel like a single accelerating movement from the first shout to the first blow. A month of quiet plotting would dissipate that energy. He simply omits it, trusting that an audience feels time as pressure, not as a calendar.

### The Capitol versus the Curia of Pompey

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Caesar was killed on 15 March 44 BC in the Curia of Pompey, a meeting hall attached to Pompey's theatre complex, beneath a statue of Pompey. He was not on the Capitoline hill.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Act 1 repeatedly places the coming Senate meeting at the Capitol: Cicero asks "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?"; Casca has met a lion "Against the Capitol"; and the assassination in Act 3 takes place there. At the same time, the conspirators meet at "Pompey's Porch" and "Pompey's Theatre", so the real location survives in the play as the plotters' base rather than the murder site.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Elizabethan writers commonly imagined the Capitol as Rome's seat of government, much as we say 'Westminster' for Parliament. Shakespeare follows that habit, and gains a symbolic setting: the hill of Jupiter, where triumphs ended, becomes the place where the triumphant man is cut down.

### Caesar's deafness

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** No ancient source says Caesar was deaf in one ear. Plutarch and Suetonius do record epileptic seizures, the "falling sickness" Brutus diagnoses.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Caesar tells Antony, "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him."

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** The deafness appears to be Shakespeare's invention, and it arrives at a perfectly chosen moment: immediately after Caesar's most penetrating speech about how well he reads men. The man who "looks / Quite through the deeds of men" cannot hear on one side. Many critics read this as a small emblem of the whole character, magnificent in judgement and defective in perception, though the text does not label it so.

### Three crown offerings versus Plutarch's two

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** In Plutarch's account Antony offered the diadem twice, and both times the refusal drew louder applause than the offer.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Brutus asks Casca "Was the crown offered him thrice?" and Casca answers "Ay, marry, was 't, and he put it by thrice, every time gentler than other" ('marry' is a mild oath meaning 'indeed'; to 'put by' is to push aside). Cassius has already counted the offstage noise: "They shouted thrice."

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Three is the number of ritual and folk tale, and it lets Casca chart a decline: each refusal "gentler than other", each one, in his telling, more reluctant. Two refusals could be modesty; three graded refusals look like performance. Shakespeare also adds Casca's cynicism that Caesar "would fain have had it", which is nowhere in Plutarch, so the audience receives the event already interpreted by an enemy.

### The tribunes' timing and fate

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The tribunes removed a diadem from a statue and arrested men who hailed Caesar as king, most probably in January 44 BC. Caesar deposed them from office.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** The tribunes act on the day of the triumph and the Lupercalia, and Casca reports that they "for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence."

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** By moving their action onto the festival day and leaving their fate ambiguous, Shakespeare makes the punishment fall with shocking speed and lets the audience fear the worst. The phrase 'put to silence' is deliberately colder than 'removed from office'.

### Graffiti into forged letters

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Plutarch says that anonymous messages appeared on Brutus's praetor's chair and on the statue of his ancestor, urging him to act. Their authors are unknown.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Cassius plans the letters himself: "I will this night / In several hands in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens, / Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name", and in Scene 3 gives Cinna the papers for "the praetor's chair" and "old Brutus' statue".

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** This is the change that most alters the moral shape of the play. In history, Brutus may have been responding to real public feeling; in Shakespeare, he is deceived by a friend. One strong reading is that the forged letters compromise the conspiracy's claim to honour from the outset, and that the audience, unlike Brutus, knows it.

::: revision The changes in one glance
Triumph (October 45 BC) + tribunes (January 44 BC) + Lupercalia (15 February 44 BC) → one day. One silent month between Scenes 2 and 3. Curia of Pompey → the Capitol. Deafness → invented. Two offerings → three, plus Casca's cynicism. Tribunes deposed → 'put to silence'. Anonymous graffiti → Cassius's forgeries. Learn these seven and you will never again write history as if it were the play, or the play as if it were history.
:::

## Shakespeare's Source and the London of 1599

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** Shakespeare's main source was Sir Thomas North's English translation of Plutarch's *Parallel Lives*, published in 1579, itself translated from a French version. The Lives of Caesar, Brutus and Antony gave him nearly every incident in Act 1: the Lupercalia, the diadem, the tribunes, the omens, the soothsayer's warning, the graffiti, Caesar's remark that he feared lean and pale men rather than fat ones, and Caesar's epilepsy. Where a detail in the play is not in Plutarch, such as the deafness, the Tiber race and Casca's three offerings, you are looking at Shakespeare's own hand. The play was on the London stage by September 1599, when a Swiss visitor recorded seeing a tragedy of Julius Caesar at a Bankside theatre, almost certainly the newly built Globe.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** In 1599 Queen Elizabeth I was sixty-six, unmarried and without a named heir. The question of who would rule after her was the most dangerous topic in England, and it could not be discussed openly. Official teaching held that rebellion against a monarch was a grave sin; at the same time, educated people knew the classical arguments for killing a tyrant. A play about a Republic destroying itself over the fear of one man's rule, and about the chaos that followed, may well have spoken to those anxieties. Audiences may have felt the parallel; we cannot prove what they felt, and you should never write as though we can. The safe and accurate formulation for an answer is: 'Written in 1599, when Elizabeth was ageing without an heir, the play explores questions of succession and legitimate power that many in its first audience may have found uncomfortably close to home.'

```chain
HISTORICAL FACT → North's Plutarch (1579) supplied the incidents of Act 1; the play was performed by September 1599 in a London ruled by an elderly, heirless queen.
↓
WHAT A ROMAN AUDIENCE MAY HAVE UNDERSTOOD → Not applicable here; the audience that matters is Elizabethan, for whom republican Rome was both a classroom classic and a mirror they could not name.
↓
HOW SHAKESPEARE USES IT → He keeps Plutarch's facts, sharpens them (three offerings, forged letters) and adds small inventions (deafness) that turn history into character.
↓
WHY IT MATTERS FOR ICSE → A single well-placed sentence on the source or the 1599 context can earn credit in an evaluative sub-question, but only if phrased as interpretation. Never assert what Elizabethan audiences 'thought'.
```

::: revision The ten facts to carry into every Act 1 scene
1. Rome is a Republic founded (509 BC) on hatred of the word 'king'; Brutus is named for its founder.
2. Tribunes were sacrosanct protectors of the people; silencing them shows Caesar above the law.
3. Two consuls shared power; Antony, a consul, obeys Caesar like a servant.
4. Citizens were free by definition; Cassius's language of bondage is meant to shame.
5. Dignitas, gloria, auctoritas and the mos maiorum explain Cassius's envy, Caesar's pride, Brutus's value and the conspirators' appeal to the ancestors.
6. Armies loyal to generals (Marius, Sulla) are the real basis of Caesar's power.
7. Caesar and Pompey were allies, then enemies; Pompey's ghost haunts every scene.
8. A triumph over Romans was a scandal; Marullus's questions are a legal checklist.
9. The Lupercalia was a fertility festival; the diadem was offered twice in Plutarch, thrice in Shakespeare.
10. Caesar was dictator for life by February 44 BC; the crown was the name for what already existed.
