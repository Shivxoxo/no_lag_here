# Difficult Shakespearean English: The Act 1 Vocabulary System

*What this chapter does: it takes the single biggest obstacle between you and this play, the language itself, and turns it into a system you can operate. Part 1 teaches the grammar that makes Shakespeare sound foreign (thou, hath, wherefore, inverted word order, missing words) using only Act 1 examples. Part 2 is a scene-by-scene word bank of every genuinely difficult word or phrase in Act 1. Part 3 exposes the 'false friends', words that look modern but mean something else. Part 4 shows you, step by step, how a trained reader decodes three real passages, and then asks you to do it yourself. By the end, Shakespearean English should feel less like a wall and more like a lock you have the key to.*

## Before You Begin: Three Kinds of Difficulty, One Method

Let us be honest about what has been going wrong since Class 9. You open the play, you meet a line such as "What dost thou with thy best apparel on?", and something in your brain switches off. It is not because you are weak at English. It is because you are meeting three different problems at once and nobody has separated them for you.

**Problem one is grammar.** Shakespeare wrote in what scholars call Early Modern English, the English of roughly 1500 to 1700. It is *not* Old English (that is the language of *Beowulf*, and no untrained modern reader can follow a word of it). Early Modern English is our own language with a slightly different engine: a second set of pronouns (thou, thee, thy), extra verb endings (hath, doth, dost), question words that have since died (wherefore), and a freer word order. Once you know the engine, the sentences start moving.

**Problem two is vocabulary.** Some words have simply gone (awl, neat's leather, coronet). Others are still alive but have changed their meaning, and these are far more dangerous, because they do not look difficult. When Cassius tells Brutus "be not jealous on me", he is not talking about envy. When Casca says the lion went by "Without annoying me", he does not mean the lion was mildly irritating. Part 3 of this chapter is devoted entirely to these traps.

**Problem three is compression.** Shakespeare wrote most of this play in blank verse, ten-syllable lines with a regular beat. Verse forces a writer to pack meaning tightly: to leave out small words, to put the important word where the beat falls, and to use one metaphor where prose would use a paragraph. So even when you know every word, a line like "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings" asks you to unpack a whole theory of human responsibility from two lines.

**The method.** For the rest of this book, and for every Shakespeare passage you ever meet in an examination, read in three passes.

```chain
PASS 1 — Read to the punctuation, not to the line-end. Read aloud if you can. Get the shape of the sentence.
↓
PASS 2 — Find the main verb. Then ask 'who is doing this?' Rebuild the subject-verb-object order in your head.
↓
PASS 3 — Swap the old forms (thou → you, hath → has, wherefore → why) and unpack the metaphor. Only now translate.
```

Never translate word by word. Translate sentence by sentence. A word-by-word reader of "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other" produces nonsense; a sentence-by-sentence reader sees a man weighing two things at once and choosing to look at both without flinching.

One more encouragement before we begin. If you speak Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Marathi, Tamil or almost any other Indian language, you already possess an instinct that modern English has lost: you know that the word for 'you' changes depending on how close you are to the person and how much respect you owe them. Shakespeare's English had exactly that instinct. You are, in this one respect, better equipped to read him than a native speaker of modern English.

## Part 1: The Grammar of Shakespeare's English

### 1.1 Thou, thee, thy, thine, ye and you: the second person

Modern English has one word, *you*, for everybody: one person or twenty, a friend or a judge. Shakespeare's English had two sets, and the choice between them was meaningful.

| Form | Grammatical job | Modern equivalent | Act 1 example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **thou** | subject, one person | you | "Speak, what trade art thou?" (Flavius to the Carpenter) |
| **thee** | object, one person | you | "I rather tell thee what is to be feared" (Caesar to Antony) |
| **thy** | possessive, before a consonant | your | "Where is thy leather apron and thy rule?" (Marullus) |
| **thine** | possessive, before a vowel or standing alone | your, yours | Not used in Act 1. The same rule governs *mine*: Casca says "mine honest neighbours" and "mine own part" because the next word begins with a vowel or an *h*. |
| **thyself** | reflexive, one person | yourself | "A man no mightier than thyself or me" (Cassius to Casca) |
| **ye** | subject, several people (older form) | you (all) | "Ye gods, it doth amaze me" (Cassius; the Folger text prints "You gods") |
| **you** | several people, OR one person addressed politely | you | "Brutus, I do observe you now of late." (Cassius) |

**Original word:** thou / thee / thy. **Simple meaning:** you / you / your, addressed to one person. **Contextual meaning:** the *familiar* or *downward* form, used to a child, a servant, a close friend, a lover, a god in prayer, or an inferior one wishes to put in his place. **Modern equivalent:** the nearest living English equivalent is a first name or a nickname used where a title would be expected; in Hindi the parallel is *tu* or *tum* against *aap*. **Why it matters:** every time a Shakespearean character chooses *thou* rather than *you*, he is telling the audience what he thinks of the person in front of him. It is a stage direction hidden inside the grammar.

**Original word:** you / ye. **Simple meaning:** you, addressed to several people, or to one person with respect. **Contextual meaning:** the *polite* or *level* form between equals, and the form owed to superiors. **Modern equivalent:** plain modern *you*, but carrying the weight that *Sir* or *Madam* carries today. **Why it matters:** because it is the default among the Roman nobles, the moments when a noble drops into *thou* stand out sharply.

#### What the switching signals in Act 1

The distinction is not rigid. By 1599 it was already loosening, and Shakespeare uses it flexibly, sometimes for metre. But in Act 1 the pattern is consistent enough to teach, and it rewards attention.

**The tribunes and the workmen (Scene 1).** Flavius opens the play with a plural: "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!" That *you* is simply plural, aimed at the whole crowd. But the moment he singles out one man, he drops to *thou*: "Speak, what trade art thou?" Marullus does the same to the Carpenter, "Where is thy leather apron and thy rule?", and then, interestingly, begins with the polite form to the Cobbler: "You, sir, what trade are you?" Watch what happens as the Cobbler's puns test his patience. Marullus's next line is "But what trade art thou? Answer me directly." The pronoun has dropped from *you* to *thou* in the space of two speeches, and the insult follows at once: "What trade, thou knave? Thou naughty knave, what trade?" (the Folger text gives this to Flavius; some editions give it to Marullus) and "Mend me, thou saucy fellow?" The grammar tracks the temperature. The Cobbler, by contrast, never once says *thou* to a tribune. He says "Nay, I beseech you, sir, be not out with me." He knows his place, even while he mocks it.

**Cassius and Brutus (Scene 2).** Through their whole long conversation, Cassius addresses Brutus as *you*: "Brutus, I do observe you now of late", "Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?", "I know that virtue to be in you, Brutus". Brutus answers in kind: "Into what dangers would you lead me, Cassius". These are two senators, equals, speaking with the courtesy of their class. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the polite pronoun is part of Cassius's method. He is flattering a proud man, and flattery requires distance and deference. Then Brutus leaves, and Cassius is alone: "Well, Brutus, thou art noble. Yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed." Now, for the first time, Brutus is *thou* and *thy*. Two readings are available, and the text does not choose between them. One is that soliloquy conventionally uses *thou* for an absent person, as a poet addresses the moon. The other, sharper reading is that Cassius, with Brutus gone, drops the mask of deference and speaks of his friend as a thing to be handled, a piece of metal to be "wrought". A good answer can mention both.

**Caesar and everyone else (Scene 2).** Caesar says *thou* to the Soothsayer, "What sayst thou to me now?", which is a great man speaking down to a nobody. He says *thou* to Antony as well: "As thou dost, Antony", "I rather tell thee what is to be feared", "tell me truly what thou think'st of him". Here the downward pronoun means something different: intimacy from a superior, the tone of a commander to a trusted lieutenant. Antony, for his part, answers "Caesar, my lord" and never presumes to *thou* him back. Notice, too, that Caesar addresses his own wife with the formal pronoun, "Stand you directly in Antonius' way", which is a public order, not a private word. And in Cassius's remembered story of the Tiber, Caesar the young man says "Dar'st thou, Cassius, now / Leap in with me into this angry flood": the *thou* of a dare between young rivals, which Cassius, decades later, still resents.

**Cassius and Casca (Scene 3).** Cassius mostly says *you* to Casca, even when he is scolding him: "You are dull, Casca". But at the emotional peak of his speech, when he is about to name Caesar without naming him, he shifts: "Now could I, Casca, name to thee a man / Most like this dreadful night" and "A man no mightier than thyself or me". **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the pronoun closes the distance at exactly the moment Cassius needs Casca to feel like a fellow conspirator rather than an acquaintance. Once the bargain is struck he returns to the level form: "Now know you, Casca, I have moved already".

**Thou to the gods, to abstractions and to Rome.** Prayer and apostrophe (speaking to something that cannot answer) take *thou*. Cassius cries "Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" and, to his own grief, "But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me?" This is not disrespect; it is the intimacy of a man speaking to what he loves or fears.

::: insight The pronoun is a stage direction
A modern reader skims over *thou* and *you* as if they were the same word in two spellings. An Elizabethan audience heard them as we hear a change of tone. The nearest modern experience is a conversation in Hindi in which someone who has been calling you *aap* suddenly says *tu*: everyone in the room notices, and everyone understands that something has happened, whether it is affection, anger or contempt. Shakespeare had this instrument and used it constantly. In Act 1 it lets him do three things without adding a single word of dialogue. It ranks people: the tribunes are above the workmen, Caesar is above everyone, and the grammar says so before any insult is spoken. It measures anger: Marullus's slide from "You, sir" to "thou knave" is a temper rising in real time. And it exposes hypocrisy: Cassius says *you* to Brutus's face and *thou* to his back. When you write about Cassius's manipulation (see the chapter 'Cassius — The Manipulation Masterclass'), this grammatical detail is exactly the kind of textual evidence that separates a competent answer from an excellent one, because it shows you have read the words rather than the summary.
:::

### 1.2 Verb endings: art, hast, dost, hath, doth and the -est / -eth system

Shakespeare's verbs carried endings that modern English has dropped. The rule is simple once stated.

- After **thou**, the verb takes **-est** or **-st**: thou art, thou hast, thou dost, thou think'st, thou dar'st, thou mean'st, thou sayst, thou wilt, thou shalt.
- After **he / she / it**, the verb could take **-eth**: he hath, he doth. But by 1599 the modern **-s** ending had already won for almost every other verb. In Act 1 Caesar says of Cassius "He thinks too much", "He reads much", "He loves no plays", "he hears no music", "Seldom he smiles". Not one of these is *thinketh* or *readeth*. The old ending survives in Act 1 only in *hath* and *doth*, which were so common that they resisted change.

| Original word | Simple meaning | Contextual meaning (Act 1) | Modern equivalent | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **art** | are (with thou) | "Thou art a cobbler, art thou?"; "Age, thou art shamed!" | you are | Recognising *art* as a form of *to be* unlocks half the questions in Scene 1. |
| **hast** | have (with thou) | "Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!"; "Where hast thou led me?" | you have | Do not confuse it with *haste* ("in haste"), which sits two lines away in Scene 3. |
| **dost** | do (with thou) | "What dost thou with thy best apparel on?"; "As thou dost, Antony" | you do | Often a plain helping verb in questions: 'What are you doing with...?' |
| **sayst, think'st, dar'st, mean'st** | say, think, dare, mean (with thou) | "What sayst thou to me now?"; "what thou think'st of him"; "Dar'st thou, Cassius"; "What mean'st thou by that?" | you say, think, dare, mean | The apostrophe marks a swallowed *e*; the word is one syllable for the metre. |
| **wilt, shalt** | will, shall (with thou) | Not used in Act 1; common later in the play and throughout Shakespeare | you will, you shall | Learn them now so they do not ambush you in Acts II to V. |
| **hath** | has | "he hath the falling sickness"; "What hath proceeded worthy note today"; "heaven hath infused them with these spirits" | has | Six times in Act 1. Simply read *has* and move on. |
| **doth** | does | "When he doth run his course"; "he doth bestride the narrow world"; "Caesar doth bear me hard" | does | Eight times in Act 1. Often it is just a metrical helper: "he doth bestride" means 'he bestrides'. |
| **-eth** | third-person ending | Absent from Act 1 apart from *hath* and *doth* | -s | Proof that Shakespeare's grammar is closer to yours than it first appears. |

**The 'do' that means nothing.** Modern English uses *do* for questions, negatives and emphasis. Shakespeare also used it as a neutral filler to complete the metre. When Cassius says "Brutus, I do observe you now of late" or Brutus says "I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony" or "I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king", the *do* adds no emphasis at all. Read straight through it: *I observe*, *I lack*, *I fear*. Equally, Shakespeare could leave *do* out where we would need it: "the eye sees not itself" (we would say *does not see*), "Knew you not Pompey?" (*Did you not know*), "I know not what you mean" (*I do not know*), "Fear him not, Caesar" (*Do not fear him*). Once you stop expecting *do* where it is missing and stop hearing emphasis where it is present, a surprising number of lines become ordinary sentences.

### 1.3 Question words and direction words: wherefore, hence, hither, thither, whence, whither

Modern English keeps *where*, *here* and *there* and adds prepositions to them: *from where*, *to here*, *to there*. Shakespeare's English had single words for each of these jobs, and one of them, *wherefore*, is the most misread word in the whole of Shakespeare.

| Original word | Simple meaning | Contextual meaning (Act 1) | Modern equivalent | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **wherefore** | why, for what reason | "Wherefore rejoice?"; "But wherefore art not in thy shop today?"; "But wherefore do you hold me here so long?"; "But wherefore did you so much tempt the heavens?" | why | Four times in Act 1, and it never means *where*. Marullus's "Wherefore rejoice?" is not asking for a location but demanding a justification for the entire holiday. |
| **hence** | away from here; from this time or cause | "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!" | get away from here | The first word of the play is an order to disperse. The tribunes' whole project is to send the crowd *hence*; the whole play's tragedy is that crowds cannot be sent away for long. |
| **hither** | to here | Not used in Act 1 | (come) here | Common later in the play and elsewhere in Shakespeare. |
| **thither** | to there | Not used in Act 1 | (go) there | Pairs with *hither*. |
| **whence** | from where | Not used in Act 1 | where from | Often survives in the redundant modern phrase 'from whence'. |
| **whither** | to where | Not used in Act 1 | where to | Distinguish it from *whether*, which Flavius contracts to "whe'er". |
| **whereof, wherein, therein** | of which, in which, in that | "By means whereof this breast of mine hath buried / Thoughts of great value"; "wherein obscurely / Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at"; "Therein, you gods, you make the weak most strong" | of which, in which, in that respect | These compounds glue long sentences together. Split them: *where* + *of* = *of which*; *there* + *in* = *in that*. |
| **where** (meaning 'instances in which') | that, in cases where | "I have heard / Where many of the best respect in Rome ... Have wished that noble Brutus had his eyes" | I have heard that | An Elizabethan use that trips students who expect a place. |

### 1.4 Small words with big jobs

These are the words that make a Shakespearean sentence *sound* archaic. Individually each is trivial; together they are the accent of the play. Learn them once and they stop being noise.

| Original word | Simple meaning | Contextual meaning (Act 1) | Modern equivalent | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ere** | before | "But ere we could arrive the point proposed"; "ere day / We will awake him" | before | Never confuse with *e'er* (ever) or *err* (make a mistake). |
| **yon, yond, yonder** | that, over there | "Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look"; "swim to yonder point" | that (one) over there | Caesar's *yond* is a pointing finger: he is looking at Cassius from a distance and judging him by his shape. |
| **anon** | soon, at once | Not used in Act 1 | in a moment | Standard Shakespearean; learn it for later acts. |
| **nay** | no | "Nay, I beseech you, sir, be not out with me."; "Nay, an I tell you that, I'll ne'er look you i' th' face again." | no | Often opens a correction: 'no, listen...'. |
| **ay** | yes | "Ay, do you fear it?"; "Ay, marry, was 't"; "Ay, he spoke Greek." | yes | Pronounced like *I*. Cassius's "Ay, do you fear it?" seizes on Brutus's admission instantly. |
| **prithee, I pray you, I beseech you** | please (I pray thee) | *Prithee* is not used in Act 1. Its full forms are: "I pray you, do."; "But soft, I pray you."; "Nay, I beseech you, sir" | please; I beg you | *Beseech* is stronger and humbler than *pray*: the Cobbler begs, Cassius merely requests. |
| **'tis, 'twas** | it is, it was | "'Tis just."; "'Tis very like; he hath the falling sickness."; "'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets" | it is, it was | The apostrophe replaces the dropped *i*. Read straight through. |
| **o'er, e'er, ne'er** | over, ever, never | *O'er* and *e'er* are not used in Act 1; "I'll ne'er look you i' th' face again" | over, ever, never | One-syllable squeezes for the metre. |
| **whe'er** | whether | "See whe'er their basest mettle be not moved." | whether | The Folger contraction; the MIT text prints *whether*. Same word, same meaning. |
| **i' th', th'** | in the, the | "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other"; "Th' eternal devil" | in the, the | Swallowed vowels; the line still scans as ten syllables. |
| **'em** | them | "conjure with 'em" | them | Casual, as in modern speech. |
| **on 't, was 't** | of it, was it | "I am glad on 't."; "Ay, marry, was 't" | of it, was it | *On* often means *of* in Shakespeare ("jealous on me" = suspicious of me). |
| **ha'** | have | "I ha' not since put up my sword" | have | Casca, breathless, swallows the consonant. |
| **marry** | indeed, why yes (originally an oath 'by Mary') | "Ay, marry, was 't"; "Marry, before he fell down" | indeed; well, actually | A prose speaker's filler; Casca uses it twice. Nothing to do with weddings. |
| **an** | if | "An I had been a man of any occupation"; "Nay, an I tell you that" | if | Not the article. If *an* is followed by a clause, read *if*. |
| **aught** | anything | "If it be aught toward the general good" | anything | Opposite is *naught* (nothing). |
| **lief** | gladly, willingly | "I had as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself." | I would just as soon | 'I had as lief X as Y' = 'I would as willingly X as Y'. |
| **fain** | gladly | "he would fain have had it" | he would gladly have taken it | Casca's whole cynical reading of Caesar rests on this one word. |
| **durst** | dared | "I durst not laugh" | dared not | Past tense of *dare*. |
| **whiles** | while, as long as | "Whiles they behold a greater than themselves" | while | An extra *s*, nothing more. |
| **soft** | wait, hold on | "But soft, I pray you. What, did Caesar swoon?" | wait a moment | An interjection, not the adjective. |
| **yea** | yes, indeed, even | "yea, to chimney tops" | even, indeed | Marullus piles up the list: walls, battlements, towers, windows, *even* chimney tops. |
| **withal** | besides, moreover; with | "but withal I am indeed, sir, a surgeon to old shoes" | in addition | The Cobbler puns: *withal* sounds like *with awl*, the tool of his trade. |
| **save** | except | "In every place save here in Italy" | except | Not the verb *rescue*. |
| **but** | only, merely | "Where Brutus may but find it"; "If Caesar carelessly but nod on him" | just, only | When *but* sits before a verb, test *only* before *however*. |
| **still** | always, continually | "still as he refused it the rabblement hooted" | every time | Not *nevertheless* and not *motionless*. |
| **like** | likely | "'Tis very like"; "as this time / Is like to lay upon us" | likely | Brutus's "'Tis very like" means 'that is very probable'. |
| **wont, use** | accustomed | "as I was wont to have"; "as they use to do the players in the theatre" | used to | *Use* as a verb of habit is the origin of modern 'used to'. |

### 1.5 Inversion: when the words come in the wrong order

Modern English is strict about order: subject, verb, object. Elizabethan English was freer, partly because its grammar was closer to Latin, partly because verse needs flexibility to put the strong word on the strong beat. There are four patterns you need to recognise in Act 1.

**Pattern A: questions without 'do'.** The verb simply moves in front of the subject. "Knew you not Pompey?" is 'Did you not know Pompey?'. "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" is 'Is Caesar coming...?'. "Brought you Caesar home?" is 'Did you bring...?'. "Why, saw you anything more wonderful?" is 'Did you see...?'. "Are not you moved" is 'Are you not moved'. **Method:** find the verb at the front, put a modern *did* or *is* before the subject, and the question reappears.

**Pattern B: the emphatic word first.** Brutus says "Vexèd I am / Of late with passions of some difference." The modern order is 'I am vexed of late'; Shakespeare moves *vexed* to the front because the vexation is the news. Cassius says "Accoutred as I was, I plungèd in": the fact that he was fully dressed and armed comes first because it is the point of the boast. Marullus says "Many a time and oft / Have you climbed up to walls and battlements": the adverb of frequency is thrown to the front and the verb follows it before the subject, as still happens in formal English ('Never have I seen...'). Cassius's "Then must I think you would not have it so" works the same way: *then* comes first, so *must* leaps ahead of *I*. And Caesar's "And therefore are they very dangerous" puts *are* before *they* because *therefore* has taken the front seat.

**Pattern C: the verb pushed to the end.** This is the Latin habit, and Cassius, the most rhetorical speaker in Act 1, uses it at his grandest moment: "I, as Aeneas, our great ancestor, / Did from the flames of Troy upon his shoulder / The old Anchises bear". The verb *bear* arrives last, after the subject, the comparison, the place, the manner and the object have all been laid out. Then comes "so from the waves of Tiber / Did I the tired Caesar", where the verb is not even repeated: you are meant to supply *bear* from the line before. Similarly, in his soliloquy Cassius says "I will this night / In several hands in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens, / Writings", holding the object, *Writings*, back for two whole lines so that the listener feels the scheming before learning what is being thrown.

**Pattern D: the phrase 'this our Caesar'.** "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great?" Modern English would say 'this Caesar of ours'. Elizabethan English could stack a demonstrative and a possessive together. The effect is contemptuous familiarity: *this Caesar of ours*, as one might say *this little dictator of ours*.

```chain
STEP 1 — Underline every verb in the sentence (including hath, doth, art).
↓
STEP 2 — For each verb, ask 'who or what does this?' Mark the subject.
↓
STEP 3 — Ask 'to whom or to what?' Collect the objects and the prepositional phrases.
↓
STEP 4 — Write the sentence out in modern order: subject, verb, object, everything else.
↓
STEP 5 — Only now replace the old forms and unpack the metaphors.
```

Practise on "Did I the tired Caesar." Verb: *did* (with *bear* understood). Subject: *I*. Object: *the tired Caesar*. Modern order: 'I bore the tired Caesar.' The line is now transparent, and you can turn to the interesting question, which is why Cassius compares himself to Aeneas and Caesar to a helpless old man.

### 1.6 Omitted words and squeezed forms

Verse leaves things out. So does fast speech. Act 1 has both, and a reader who expects every word to be present will keep tripping.

**Ellipsis (words left out).** When Cassius asks Brutus to come and watch the race, Brutus answers "Not I." That is the whole sentence: 'I will not go' compressed to two words. When Brutus asks Casca what the second shout was for, Casca says "Why, for that too." (supply 'the shout was'). Cassius's "'Tis just." means 'that is exactly right'. When Cassius calls "Who's there?" in the dark, Casca answers "A Roman." and Cassius replies "Casca, by your voice.", meaning 'I know you are Casca by your voice'. Cicero asks "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" and Casca answers "He doth", which stands for 'he does come'. Cassius tells Casca that the Roman fire in him is missing "Or else you use not", where the object 'it' is understood. In "But ere we could arrive the point proposed", the preposition *at* is missing after *arrive*, because Elizabethan *arrive* could take a direct object. Brutus asks his friends not to "construe any further my neglect / Than that poor Brutus, with himself at war, / Forgets the shows of love to other men", which unpacks as 'do not interpret my neglect as anything more than the fact that I am at war with myself'.

**Loose agreement.** Elizabethan grammar allowed a singular verb with a plural subject when it felt natural to the speaker. Cassius says "Three parts of him / Is ours already" and asks Cinna "Is Decius Brutus and Trebonius there?"; Cinna says "There's two or three of us have seen strange sights" (also dropping *who* before *have*). These are not errors to correct; they are the living grammar of 1599. Do not lose confidence when a verb does not 'agree'.

**Squeezed forms.** The metre needs ten syllables, so vowels vanish. Here is the full Act 1 set in one place.

| Squeezed form | Full form | Act 1 example |
|---|---|---|
| 'tis, 'twas | it is, it was | "'Tis Caesar that you mean, is it not, Cassius?" |
| i' th', th' | in the, the | "death i' th' other"; "Th' ambitious ocean" |
| 'em | them | "conjure with 'em" |
| on 't, was 't | of it, was it | "I am glad on 't." |
| ha' | have | "I ha' not since put up my sword" |
| whe'er, ne'er | whether, never | "See whe'er their basest mettle be not moved." |
| think'st, dar'st, mean'st | thinkest, darest, meanest | "what thou think'st of him" |
| threat'ning | threatening (two syllables) | "the threat'ning clouds" |
| a-nights, a-shouting | at night, shouting | "such as sleep a-nights"; "the people fell a-shouting" |
| ope | open | "he plucked me ope his doublet" |

::: textnote The little accent marks: touchèd, Vexèd, glancèd
In the Folger text you will see words printed with a grave accent: "touchèd", "Vexèd", "plungèd", "glancèd", "unbracèd", "disposèd", "Transformèd", "disturbèd". This mark is an editor's signal, not Shakespeare's spelling and not a literary device. It tells the reader to pronounce the *-ed* as a separate syllable so that the line keeps its ten beats: *touch-èd*, *vex-èd*. Where the *-ed* is silent, the same editors print an apostrophe or nothing. The MIT/Globe text you may have met in an ICSE edition prints the plain form (touched, vexed) and leaves the pronunciation to you. Meaning is unaffected. Never write in an answer that 'the poet uses the device of the accented -ed'; there is no such device. If a question asks about rhythm, the honest point is that Shakespeare's line requires the extra syllable, and the accent merely records that fact.

## Part 2: Act 1 Word Bank by Scene

How to use these tables. Do not try to memorise them. Read each scene in your text with the relevant table open beside it, and tick the words you actually stumbled on. Those, and only those, go on your revision cards. The 'Why it matters' column is the one examiners care about: it tells you what a word is *doing* in the play, which is the difference between a dictionary and a study of literature. Words are listed in the order they occur in the scene. Quotation marks around a phrase mean it is Shakespeare's exact wording.

| **exalted** | raised high | "kiss the most exalted shores of all" | 'the highest banks' | Hyperbole: weep enough to flood the Tiber. |

| **tongue-tied** | silenced | "They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness." | 'speechless with guilt' | The witty Cobbler is silenced in a moment. The crowd shifts at a word: the play's central political fact. |

| **trophies** | marks of victory | "Let no images / Be hung with Caesar's trophies." | 'victory decorations' | Flavius overrides religious scruple with political urgency. |
| **the vulgar** | the common people | "drive away the vulgar from the streets" | 'the commoners' | A noun, not the adjective *rude*. The tribunes' contempt is in the grammar. |
| **thick** | crowded together | "where you perceive them thick" | 'wherever they cluster' | The people are always a mass, never individuals. |
| **growing feathers ... fly an ordinary pitch** | new plumage ... fly at a normal height | "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch" | 'clip his wings and he will stay at our level' | Falconry: *pitch* is the height a hawk climbs to before its dive. | **servile fearfulness** | slavish fear | "And keep us all in servile fearfulness." | 'cowering like slaves' | The republican nightmare in two words; it becomes Cassius's *bondage* in Scene 3. |

The text leaves the exact punishment open. |

| **blunt; quick mettle** | dull, plain-spoken; lively spirit | "What a blunt fellow is this grown to be! / He was quick mettle when he went to school." | 'how dull he's become; he was full of life at school' | Brutus misreads Casca; Cassius corrects him. |
| **execution; tardy form** | performance, carrying out; sluggish manner | "in execution / Of any bold or noble enterprise, / However he puts on this tardy form" | 'when it comes to actually doing a bold deed, whatever this slow act of his' | *Execution* has no hint yet of killing; it will. |
| **rudeness; sauce; stomach** | roughness; relish; appetite | "This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit, / Which gives men stomach to digest his words / With better appetite." | 'his bluntness is the seasoning on his intelligence; it makes people swallow what he says' | A whole meal metaphor in three lines; *rudeness* is a false friend (Part 3). |
| **think of the world** | consider the state of things | "Till then, think of the world." | 'meanwhile, think about the state we're in' | Cassius's parting instruction plants the seed he will water with forged letters. |
| **honourable mettle ... wrought ... disposed** | noble temperament ... worked, shaped ... inclined | "Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed." | 'your noble nature can be bent away from its natural inclination' | Metal-working metaphor: Brutus is iron to be forged. The MIT text prints *metal*. |
| **keep ever with their likes; seduced** | always associate with their equals; led astray | "noble minds keep ever with their likes; / For who so firm that cannot be seduced?" | 'the noble should stick to their own kind, because anyone can be corrupted' | Cassius admits, alone, that he is corrupting Brutus. |
| **bear me hard** | bears a grudge against me | "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus." | 'Caesar has it in for me' | Personal resentment beneath republican principle. |
| **humour** | influence by indulging one's mood; cajole | "He should not humour me." | 'he wouldn't be able to win me round' | False friend (Part 3): nothing to do with comedy. |
| **several hands** | different handwritings | "In several hands in at his windows throw" | 'in various handwritings' | *Several* = separate (Part 3). The forgery is planned in detail. |
| **tending; obscurely; glancèd at** | pointing towards; indirectly; hinted at | "Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name, wherein obscurely / Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at" | 'letters all suggesting how highly Rome thinks of him, with veiled hints at Caesar's ambition' | The method of manipulation is indirectness: never accuse, only hint. |
| **seat him sure** | make his position secure | "let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure" | 'let Caesar sit tight; we'll shake him off, or suffer for it' | A rider's metaphor; the scene ends on a rhyme, the audible click of a decision. |

### Scene 3: the storm

Scene 3 is the hardest of the three for vocabulary, and for a reason worth knowing before you start. Scene 1 is mostly prose and insult; Scene 2 is persuasion between friends. Scene 3 is verse spoken in a thunderstorm by frightened or exalted men, and its words are drawn from three specialised registers at once: the language of omens (prodigies, portentous, heralds, tokens), the language of nature breaking its own laws (ordinance, preformèd faculties, quality and kind) and the language of slavery and freedom (bondage, bondman, yoke, sufferance, captivity). Once you see the three registers, the scene stops being a fog of hard words and becomes three vocabularies arguing with one another.

| **sway of earth** | the whole swaying frame of the world; also 'rule, government' | "all the sway of earth / Shakes like a thing unfirm" | 'the solid earth itself' | *Sway* carries a second sense, 'dominion'. The ground is shaking and so, the audience suspects, is the state. |

| **ghastly** | ghost-like, deathly pale with terror | "a hundred ghastly women" | 'women white as ghosts' | Not 'awful' in the modern loose sense; the word still contained *ghost*. |
| **Transformèd with their fear** | changed in appearance by terror | "Transformèd with their fear, who swore they saw / Men all in fire" | 'their faces changed by fear' | Fear alters bodies in this scene: hands, faces, Cassius's bared chest. |
| **bird of night** | the owl | "the bird of night did sit / Even at noonday upon the marketplace" | 'an owl' | An owl by day in the busiest public space: the natural order inverted exactly where the political order is decided. |
| **prodigies** | unnatural, ominous events | "When these prodigies / Do so conjointly meet" | 'these freak occurrences' | The key noun of the scene. Latin *prodigium* = a sign sent by the gods. |
| **conjointly** | all together, at the same time | same line | 'all at once' | Casca's argument: one marvel might be chance; several together cannot be. |
| **portentous** | foreboding, full of omen | "they are portentous things / Unto the climate that they point upon" | 'they are omens' | From *portent*. A portent points at something: the preposition *upon* keeps the pointing finger visible. |
| **climate** | region, country | same line | 'the land they point at' | Not weather. The omens are addressed to a particular country, Rome. |
| **strange-disposèd** | strangely inclined, abnormal | "it is a strange-disposèd time" | 'a strangely arranged time' | *Disposed* = arranged by some disposing power; Cicero hints that something is arranging events without saying what. |
| **construe ... after their fashion; Clean from the purpose** | interpret ... in their own way; entirely away from the real meaning | "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves" | 'people read things however they like, quite missing what the things actually mean' | *Clean* = completely. The play's most important sentence about interpretation, and the word *construe* carries it, as it carried Brutus in Scene 2. |
| **did bid** | ordered, instructed | "he did bid Antonius / Send word to you" | 'he told Antony to send word' | *Bid* is a strong verb of command; Caesar does not ask. |
| **disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in** | a troubled sky is no weather to be out in | "This disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in." | 'this is no night to be out' | Cicero removes himself from the plot by removing himself from the storm. |
| **menace** | threaten | "Who ever knew the heavens menace so?" | 'when did the sky ever threaten like this?' | A verb here. Casca's question is answered by Cassius with a theory, not a fear. |
| **so full of faults** | so riddled with wrongs | "Those that have known the Earth so full of faults" | 'anyone who knows how corrupt the world is' | *Faults* means moral failings; Cassius reads the storm as a mirror of political rot. |
| **Submitting me unto** | exposing myself to | "Submitting me unto the perilous night" | 'putting myself at the mercy of the night' | The reflexive *me* where we say *myself*. |
| **unbracèd** | with doublet unfastened, chest bare | "And thus unbracèd, Casca, as you see, / Have bared my bosom to the thunder-stone" | 'with my shirt open to the lightning' | Elizabethan dress again. | **thunder-stone** | thunderbolt | same line | 'the lightning bolt' | **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Elizabethans believed a stone fell with the lightning. The word is a fossil of that belief. |
| **cross blue lightning** | forked, zigzag lightning | "when the cross blue lightning seemed to open / The breast of heaven" | 'forked lightning' | *Cross* = crossing, zigzag. Heaven has a breast as Cassius has a bosom: the sky and the man face each other. |
| **tempt the heavens** | provoke, test the gods | "But wherefore did you so much tempt the heavens?" | 'why did you dare the gods like that?' | *Tempt* = put to the test (as in 'tempt fate'). Casca is pious; Cassius is not. |
| **the part of men** | the proper role of human beings | "It is the part of men to fear and tremble" | 'it's only right for men to be afraid' | *Part* = role, as in a play. Cassius will shortly redefine what the part of a Roman man is. |
| **tokens; heralds; astonish** | signs; messengers who announce; stun as if by thunder | "When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us" | 'when the gods send such dreadful messengers to stun us' | *Astonish* comes from a word meaning 'to strike with thunder': here the metaphor is literal. |
| **dull** | slow of feeling, insensible | "You are dull, Casca" | 'you're slow, Casca' | Not boring. Cassius accuses Casca of failing to feel what a Roman should feel. |
| **want; use not** | lack; do not exercise | "those sparks of life / That should be in a Roman you do want, / Or else you use not" | 'you either lack a Roman's fire or you don't use it' | *Want* = lack (Part 3). The insult is framed as a diagnosis so that Casca will want to prove it wrong. |
| **put on fear; cast yourself in wonder** | assume a frightened look; throw yourself into amazement | "You look pale, and gaze, / And put on fear, and cast yourself in wonder" | 'you go pale and stare and act terrified' | *Put on* implies fear is a costume. Cassius treats emotion as something chosen. |
| **impatience** | inability to endure; agitation | "the strange impatience of the heavens" | 'the sky's wild agitation' | Heaven cannot *bear* something. Cassius is building towards what it cannot bear. |
| **from quality and kind** | away from their natures and species | "Why birds and beasts from quality and kind" | 'why animals act against their natures' | *Kind* = nature (as in *mankind*). Behaviour against kind is the Elizabethan definition of the unnatural. |
| **calculate** | prophesy, cast horoscopes | "Why old men, fools, and children calculate" | 'why old men, idiots and children are all making prophecies' | Those least qualified to reason are reasoning about the future: order has flipped. |
| **ordinance** | the ordained natural order | "Why all these things change from their ordinance" | 'depart from the laws laid down for them' | From *ordain*. Not weapons (that is *ordnance*). |
| **preformèd faculties** | inborn powers, natures fixed in advance | "Their natures, and preformèd faculties, / To monstrous quality" | 'their built-in natures, into something unnatural' | The most abstract phrase in Act 1. Nature has a programme, and it has been overwritten. |
| **monstrous quality; monstrous state** | unnatural character; abnormal condition (and, punningly, a deformed commonwealth) | "To make them instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state" | 'to warn of some monstrous situation' | *State* means condition and also the political state. | **infused** | poured in, filled | "heaven hath infused them with these spirits" | 'heaven has filled them with these powers' | Literal sense of pouring: the omens are vessels filled with meaning. |
| **prodigious grown; fearful; eruptions** | grown into a portent; terrifying; outbreaks, disturbances | "yet prodigious grown, / And fearful, as these strange eruptions are" | 'grown into a monstrous omen, terrifying like these outbreaks' | *Fearful* = causing fear, not feeling it (Part 3). Caesar is described in the vocabulary of the storm so that the two become one thing. |
| **thews** | muscles, sinews | "Have thews and limbs like to their ancestors" | 'the same muscles and limbs as their ancestors' | Bodies are unchanged; only minds have shrunk. The whole speech rests on this contrast. |

| **retentive to** | able to hold back, to imprison | "Can be retentive to the strength of spirit" | 'can hold the spirit prisoner' | A Latinate adjective where we would use a verb: 'cannot retain'. Decoded in Part 4. |

| **factious; redress; griefs** | active in forming a faction; remedy; grievances | "Be factious for redress of all these griefs" | 'form a party to put right all these wrongs' | *Factious* is nearly always a word of blame in Shakespeare; Casca uses it as praise. *Griefs* = grievances, not sorrows. |

| **undergo; honourable-dangerous** | undertake; honourable and dangerous at once | "To undergo with me an enterprise / Of honourable-dangerous consequence" | 'to take on with me a venture that is both honourable and dangerous' | *Undergo* = undertake, not suffer. | **stay for me** | wait for me | "they stay for me / In Pompey's Porch" | 'they're waiting for me' | *Stay* = wait. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the portico of Pompey's theatre, beside the hall where Caesar was in fact killed. |
| **stir** | movement, activity | "There is no stir or walking in the streets" | 'nobody is about' | The streets that were thronged in Scene 1 are empty: the people have withdrawn, and the plotters have the city. |
| **complexion of the element / In favour 's like** | the appearance of the sky resembles | "the complexion of the element / In favour 's like the work we have in hand" | 'the look of the sky is like the deed we're planning' | *Element* = sky; *favour* = face, appearance. Cassius reads the storm as a portrait of the conspiracy. |
| **Stand close** | keep hidden | "Stand close awhile, for here comes one in haste." | 'hide a moment' | *Close* = concealed. Conspirators learn the vocabulary of hiding. |
| **gait** | manner of walking | "'Tis Cinna; I do know him by his gait." | 'I know his walk' | In the dark, men are known by voice and walk: Casca "by your voice", Cinna by his gait. Identity is uncertain all night. |

| **Repair to; hie; bestow** | go to; hurry; place, deposit | "Repair to Pompey's Porch, where you shall find us."; "Well, I will hie / And so bestow these papers as you bade me." | 'go to the porch'; 'I'll hurry and plant these papers as you told me' | *Repair* has no sense of mending here; *bestow* means 'put in place', not 'give'. Three archaic verbs in three lines. |
| **Three parts of him / Is ours** | three quarters of him belong to us | "Three parts of him / Is ours already, and the man entire / Upon the next encounter yields him ours." | 'he's three-quarters ours; one more meeting and he's completely ours' | Brutus as a fraction being filled: the arithmetic of seduction. |
| **sits high in all the people's hearts** | is greatly loved by the people | "O, he sits high in all the people's hearts" | 'the people adore him' | Casca, who despised the people an hour ago, now wants their good opinion. The conspirators need the crowd they scorn. |
| **appear offence; countenance** | look like a crime; approval, backing (and 'face') | "that which would appear offence in us / His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness." | 'what would look like a crime if we did it, his support will turn into virtue' | *Countenance* = face and sanction at once. Brutus's face is the alchemy that will gild the murder. |
| **alchemy** | the art of turning base metal to gold | same line | 'a magic that turns lead into gold' | Answers Cassius's "base matter". Rome is base metal; Brutus is the philosopher's stone. |
| **conceited** | conceived, understood, expressed | "You have right well conceited." | 'you've put that exactly right' | Not vanity (Part 3). Cassius praises Casca's image because it is his own thought in better words. |
| **ere day** | before daybreak | "and ere day / We will awake him and be sure of him" | 'before dawn we'll wake him and make sure of him' | *Be sure of* = secure, pin down. The scene ends with Brutus as a prize to be collected before morning. |

## Part 3: False Friends

A false friend is a word you recognise and therefore do not look up. That is precisely what makes it dangerous. When you meet *tributaries* or *thews* you know you are in difficulty and you reach for a glossary. When you meet *jealous*, *annoying* or *proper*, you sail on with a modern meaning in your head, and the sentence quietly turns into nonsense without telling you. Every word in this section still exists in modern English, and every one of them meant something different, or something wider, in 1599. Most of the shifts follow one of three patterns, and knowing the pattern helps you guess the next false friend you meet.

- **Narrowing.** The old word was broad; the modern word keeps one slice. *Jealous* once meant any kind of suspicion or watchful anxiety; we keep only the romantic slice. *Sad* meant serious or settled; we keep only the unhappy slice. *Quick* meant alive; we keep only the speed.
- **Weakening.** The old word was strong; the modern word is mild. *Annoying* meant to harm; now it means to irritate. *Naughty* meant wicked; now it describes a toddler. *Saucy* meant insolent; now it means cheekily charming.
- **Drifting.** The word has slid to a neighbouring sense. *Humour* moved from a bodily fluid, to the mood it produced, to indulging that mood, and only afterwards to comedy. *Conceit* moved from a concept to the specific concept of one's own excellence. *Nice* drifted so far that it no longer means anything much at all (it does not occur in Act 1, but it haunts the rest of Shakespeare, where it usually means 'fussy' or 'precise').

### The Act 1 false-friend table

| Word | What you think it means | What it means in Act 1 (with the line) | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **jealous** | envious, possessive in love | *suspicious, doubtful, mistrustful*. Cassius: "And be not jealous on me, gentle Brutus." Brutus: "That you do love me, I am nothing jealous." | A student who reads envy here makes Cassius ask Brutus not to envy him, which is absurd. Cassius is asking not to be *suspected*; Brutus replies that he has no *doubt* of Cassius's love. The whole exchange is about trust, and the false friend hides it. Note also *on* for *of*. |
| **humour** | comedy, funniness | *to indulge, cajole, win round by playing to someone's mood*. Cassius, alone: "He should not humour me." | Cassius means that if their positions were reversed, Brutus could not talk him round. Read as 'amuse', the line becomes a complaint that Brutus is not funny, which destroys the soliloquy's logic. The Folger prints *humor*. |
| **mettle / metal** | courage, spirit (mettle); iron, gold (metal) | *both at once*. In 1599 they were one word with one spelling. Flavius: "See whe'er their basest mettle be not moved." Cassius: "Thy honourable mettle may be wrought"; "He was quick mettle when he went to school." | Modern spelling forces a choice Shakespeare did not make. In each line the sense of temperament *and* the sense of a workable metal are present: the crowd is base metal to be stirred, Brutus is honourable metal to be forged ("wrought"), Casca was lively metal at school. The MIT text prints *metal* in Cassius's soliloquy; both are right. |
| **quick** | fast | *alive, lively, full of life*. Brutus: "that quick spirit that is in Antony". Cassius: "He was quick mettle when he went to school." | Brutus is not saying Antony is speedy; he is saying Antony has an aliveness that Brutus lacks. The old sense survives in 'the quick and the dead' and in a fingernail's *quick*. |
| **want** | desire | *lack, be without*. Cassius to Casca: "those sparks of life / That should be in a Roman you do want". | Read as desire, the line says Casca *wants* Roman fire, which is nearly the opposite of the insult. Cassius says Casca *lacks* it. In Shakespeare, test *lack* before *wish for*; the old sense survives in 'for want of'. |
| **rude / rudeness** | impolite | *rough, unpolished, uncultivated*. Brutus: "This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit". | Brutus is not calling Casca bad-mannered; he is calling his style rough-hewn, and saying the roughness makes his intelligence more palatable. The modern reading turns a compliment into a complaint. |
| **saucy** | cheeky, playfully impertinent | *insolent, presumptuous towards a superior*. Flavius: "Mend me, thou saucy fellow?" Casca: "the world, too saucy with the gods". | In 1599 this was a serious charge: it accused a social inferior of forgetting his place. Flavius is furious, not amused. Casca's phrase means mankind has become *insolent* towards heaven, an offence worth a storm. |
| **nice** | pleasant, kind | *not used in Act 1*. Elsewhere in Shakespeare: fastidious, precise, trivial. | Listed so that it does not ambush you in the later acts or in another play. If you meet it, never read 'pleasant'. |
| **conceit / conceited** | vanity / vain | *idea, conception / conceived, expressed*. Cassius to Casca: "You have right well conceited." | Cassius is not calling Casca arrogant. He is saying Casca has *understood and put into words* exactly the right idea. *Conceit* was simply the noun from *conceive*. |
| **indifferent / indifferently** | mediocre; carelessly | *impartial, of no particular concern; impartially, with equal weight*. Brutus: "I will look on both indifferently". Cassius: "And dangers are to me indifferent." | Brutus does not promise to look at honour and death carelessly; he promises to weigh them *equally*, without preferring his own life. Cassius is not saying danger is mediocre; he is saying it does not matter to him either way. |
| **several** | many, more than two | *separate, different, distinct*. Cassius: "In several hands in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens". | 'Several hands' is not 'many hands' but 'different handwritings': Cassius will disguise his writing so that each forged letter looks like a different man's. The old sense survives in the legal phrase 'jointly and severally'. |
| **sensible** | reasonable, practical | *capable of feeling, able to sense*. Casca: "Not sensible of fire, remained unscorched." | The slave's hand is not 'reasonable about fire'; it *feels* no fire. The old word belongs to *sense* and *sensation*, not to good sense. |
| **annoying** | mildly irritating | *harming, injuring, molesting*. Casca: "Without annoying me." | The lion walked past *without hurting him*. If you read irritation, the marvel evaporates and so does the fear. The French cousin *ennuyer* kept a different slice of the same old word. |
| **proper** | correct, respectable | *handsome, fine* (of people); *one's own, belonging to oneself* (of things). Cobbler: "As proper men as ever trod upon neat's leather". Brutus: "Conceptions only proper to myself". | Two old senses, neither modern. The Cobbler's customers are *fine-looking* men; Brutus's thoughts are *his own*. The second sense survives in 'property' and in 'proper noun' (a name that belongs to one thing). |
| **naughty** | mischievous (of a child) | *wicked, worthless, good for nothing*. Flavius: "Thou naughty knave". | From *naught*, nothing: a *naughty* man is a man of no worth. The tribune is not scolding a child; he is dismissing a man as rubbish, and the Cobbler's reply treats the insult with the seriousness it was meant to carry. |
| **idle** | lazy | *lazy, but also empty, trivial, unemployed*. Flavius: "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!" | The modern sense is close enough that you will not misread the line, but the old word was stronger and wider: the workmen are *useless*, *doing nothing of value*, *not at work*. It is a class insult, not a comment on energy. |
| **wonderful** | very good, delightful | *causing wonder, astonishing (and often frightening)*. Cicero: "Why, saw you anything more wonderful?" | Cicero is asking whether Casca saw anything *more astonishing* than the storm; nothing in the word is pleasant. The modern praise-sense did not yet exist. |

::: icse How false friends cost marks, and how they earn them
**EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE.** The most common vocabulary error in extract answers is not failing to know a hard word; it is confidently mistranslating an easy one. A student asked to explain "be not jealous on me" who writes about envy has not merely lost the vocabulary mark; the examiner now doubts everything else in the answer, because the student has shown that he or she did not understand what the two men were talking about. The reverse is also true. If a Class 10 extract from Act 3 or 4 contains a word you recognise from this table, and you translate it correctly in your own words ('here *several* means separate', 'here *want* means lack'), you demonstrate exactly the understanding of meaning that CISCE's stated skills reward, in two lines, at no risk. Be honest with yourself about the syllabus: Act 1 is examined in Class 9, and the board paper sets Acts 3 to 5. But the false friends do not change between acts. These are not Act 1 peculiarities; they are ordinary Shakespearean usage, and you will meet the same traps in the examined acts and in every other Shakespeare text you ever read. The student who learned them here reads those acts with an advantage the summary-readers never acquire.

## Part 4: Reading Practice

Everything above is equipment. This section shows the equipment in use. Three passages follow, one from each scene, chosen because each presents a different kind of difficulty: the first is hard because of grammar and compression, the second because of metaphor, the third because of register and Latinate vocabulary. For each, I will do exactly what I asked you to do at the start of this chapter: three passes, no word-by-word translation, and only then the literary question. Read the passage aloud first. Then cover my working and attempt your own before reading on.

### Passage 1 (Scene 1): Marullus turns on the crowd

> **Marullus:** Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? / What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels? / You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things! / O you hard hearts, you cruel men of Rome, / Knew you not Pompey?

**Pass 1, the shape.** Read to the punctuation. There are six sentences in six lines, and five of them are questions. Nobody is expected to answer; a tribune does not ask a crowd for information. So the shape tells you the mode before you understand a word: this is a man demanding, not asking. The sixth sentence is not a question but a list of names: "You blocks, you stones". A list of insults in the middle of a list of questions is the sound of someone losing his temper.

**Pass 2, the verbs and their subjects.** *Rejoice* has no subject written: it is 'why do *you* rejoice?' with the *do you* omitted (section 1.6). *Brings* has the subject *he*, inverted after the question word: 'what conquest does *he* bring home?' *Follow* has the subject *tributaries*, which comes after *what*, so the order is 'what tributaries follow him?' *Grace* is an infinitive of purpose: 'in order to decorate'. *Knew* is inverted with *you*: 'did you not know Pompey?' Once each verb has its owner, every question stands up straight.

**Pass 3, swap and unpack.** *Wherefore* is *why*, not *where* (section 1.3). *Tributaries* are conquered peoples who pay tribute; *captive bonds* are the chains of prisoners; to *grace* is to adorn; *senseless* means without senses, not foolish. Now the metaphors. The crowd are called *blocks* and *stones*: not merely stupid but inert, material that has no will and takes whatever shape it is given. Then "worse than senseless things": a stone cannot be blamed for feeling nothing, but a man can. Then the paradox *hard hearts*: hearts that have turned to the stone just named.

**Plain English:** 'Why are you celebrating? What territory has he conquered? Which conquered nations are following him to Rome in chains to decorate his chariot? You lumps, you stones, you things with less feeling than objects! You hard-hearted, cruel Romans, did you not know Pompey?'

**What a trained reader notices.** Three things, each worth a sentence in an answer. First, the questions have a logic: *conquest* and *tributaries* are the two things a triumph is supposed to display, and Marullus names them in order to show that both are missing. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** a Roman triumph was for victory over foreign enemies, and Caesar's Spanish victory was over Pompey's sons, fellow Romans. Second, *Knew you not Pompey?* is placed last and is the only question that reaches into the past; it introduces the long memory speech that follows. Third, the inversion *Knew you* instead of 'did you know' is not decoration. It puts the verb of knowing first, as if to accuse the crowd of forgetting before the sentence has even named what they forgot.

### Passage 2 (Scene 2): Cassius on the Colossus and the stars

> **Cassius:** Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves.

> **Cassius:** Men at some time are masters of their fates. / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.

**Pass 1, the shape.** The first block is one sentence running over four lines; the second is two sentences, each a complete statement. So the speech moves from a long picture to short conclusions. That movement, image then argument, is the rhythm of Cassius's whole method of persuasion, and recognising it is the first step to analysing him rather than merely understanding him.

**Pass 2, the verbs and their subjects.** *Doth bestride*: subject *he* (Caesar); the *doth* is a metrical helper and means nothing (section 1.2), so read 'he bestrides'. *Walk* and *peep*: subject *we petty men*. *To find* is purpose: we peep about *in order to* find. In the second block, *are masters*: subject *men*; *is not*: subject *the fault*. Nothing is inverted here; the difficulty is not grammar.

**Pass 3, swap and unpack.** *Bestride* is to stand with one leg on each side. The *Colossus* was the giant statue at Rhodes; **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the picture of it straddling the harbour so that ships sailed between its legs is a medieval legend, not ancient fact, and Shakespeare uses the legend. The world is *narrow* not because it has shrunk but because Caesar is so large that it fits between his feet. *Petty* means small and insignificant; *peep about* means to look timidly here and there; *dishonourable graves* are graves of men who did nothing worth remembering. In the second block, *fates* and *stars* belong to astrology, the belief that the heavens fix a man's destiny at birth. *Underlings* are inferiors, subordinates.

**Plain English:** 'Why, man, he straddles the narrow world like a giant statue, and we little men walk between his huge legs and look timidly about, searching for graves to lie in without honour. Men are sometimes in charge of their own destinies. The fault, dear Brutus, is not in the stars we were born under but in ourselves, because we allow ourselves to be inferiors.'

**What a trained reader notices.** The two blocks contradict each other, and the contradiction is the point. In the first, Caesar is a statue: enormous, fixed, apparently permanent, while the men below are tiny and mobile. In the second, Cassius says that nothing about this is fixed at all: the sky did not make Caesar great, and Brutus and Cassius made themselves small. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many critics read "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars" as the play's clearest statement of free will against fate. One strong reading is that Cassius means it sincerely and that the storm scene, where he reads omens as eagerly as Casca, shows how inconsistent his position is. The text leaves open whether he believes the sentence or merely finds it useful. Notice, finally, the one word of tenderness in the whole speech, *dear*, placed exactly where the accusation lands: 'the fault, *dear* Brutus, is in ourselves'. A manipulator softens the blow at the moment of delivering it.

### Passage 3 (Scene 3): Cassius on the prison of the world

> **Cassius:** Nor stony tower, nor walls of beaten brass, / Nor airless dungeon, nor strong links of iron, / Can be retentive to the strength of spirit; / But life, being weary of these worldly bars, / Never lacks power to dismiss itself.

**Pass 1, the shape.** Two sentences joined by *But*. The first takes three lines to arrive at its verb: four things are listed, each introduced by *nor*, before the sentence tells you what they cannot do. This is Pattern C from section 1.5, the Latin habit of holding the verb back, and it means you must hold four nouns in your head and wait. Reading aloud helps: the four *nor*s fall like hammer blows, and the ear expects a verb at the end.

**Pass 2, the verbs and their subjects.** *Can be retentive*: the subject is the whole list, *tower, walls, dungeon, links*. The *nor ... nor ... nor ... nor* means 'neither this, nor this, nor this, nor this'; modern English would begin with *neither* and we would expect only two items. *Lacks*: subject *life*. *Being weary* is a participle describing *life*: 'life, when it is weary'. *To dismiss itself* is what life has the power to do.

**Pass 3, swap and unpack.** *Retentive to* is the Latinate difficulty: it means 'able to retain, to hold in'. A dungeon that is *retentive to the strength of spirit* is one that can imprison a strong spirit. *Beaten brass* is brass hammered into plates; *links of iron* are chains. *Worldly bars* are the bars of the prison that is this world: the body, circumstance, tyranny. *Dismiss itself* is the cold heart of the passage: life can *release itself from service*, which is to say a man can kill himself. Cassius never uses the word suicide. The whole speech is built so that the audience arrives at the idea without hearing it named.

**Plain English:** 'No stone tower, no walls of hammered brass, no airless dungeon, no strong iron chains can imprison a strong spirit; because a life that is tired of these worldly prison bars always has the power to end itself.'

**What a trained reader notices.** The register has risen as high as Act 1 goes. Where the Cobbler punned on *soles* and Casca grumbled about *sweaty nightcaps*, Cassius is now speaking the language of Roman Stoic philosophy, in which the freedom to die was the last freedom no tyrant could remove. **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the real Cassius was an Epicurean, not a Stoic; the text gives him Stoic sentiments because they suit the moment, and you should not claim this is a portrait of his historical philosophy. Two further points. First, the speech is a hinge: Cassius says he will free *himself* by dying, but the dagger he has just mentioned ("I know where I will wear this dagger then") will in the event be turned on Caesar, not on himself, until Philippi, when the sentence comes true after all. Second, the piled-up negatives (*nor, nor, nor, nor, never*) make a positive. Four prisons are denied so that one power can be affirmed. That is how rhetoric works, and if you can describe the mechanism in your own words, you have moved from translation to criticism.

::: revision Now you: a passage to decode alone
Try the method on Cicero's reply to Casca, which is only two lines long and contains no rare words at all: "But men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves." **Pass 1:** one sentence; find the comma. **Pass 2:** the verb is *construe*, the subject is *men*; what is being construed? **Pass 3:** *construe* means interpret (you met it in Brutus's mouth in Scene 2); *after their fashion* means in their own manner; *clean* means completely. Now write the plain English in one sentence, and then answer the literary question: why does Shakespeare give the play's calmest statement about misreading to the one senator who then walks away from the plot? Check your plain English against the word bank for Scene 3. If you can do this unaided, you no longer need this chapter; you need only the play.

::: insight Why the hard words are hard for good reasons
It is tempting to believe that Shakespeare is difficult because he is old, and that if he had written in 2026 he would be easy. The Cobbler disproves this: his prose is four hundred years old and you can follow it with a glossary of six words. The real difficulty lies in three deliberate choices, and once you see them as choices, the difficulty becomes information. The first is compression. Blank verse gives a speaker ten syllables a line, and a character in a hurry, or in a rage, will pack those syllables until they strain: "Did I the tired Caesar" carries a whole sentence in five words, and the strain is Cassius's resentment made audible. When a line feels crammed, ask what pressure is cramming it. The second is metaphor. Shakespeare rarely says a thing when he can show it as another thing: the people are stones, Caesar is a Colossus and a wolf, Brutus is metal to be wrought, Rome is kindling. These are not ornaments laid over a plain meaning; they *are* the meaning, and a reader who strips them away to find the 'real' sense is left holding nothing. When a word seems to belong to the wrong subject, you have found a metaphor, and the question is what it reveals about the speaker. The third is register. Shakespeare gives each speaker a vocabulary that places him: *replication* and *concave shores* for a tribune straining after dignity, *sweaty nightcaps* for a cynic, *retentive* and *preformèd faculties* for a conspirator dressing murder as philosophy. The hard word tells you who is speaking and what he wants you to think of him. So the honest answer to 'why is this so hard?' is usually 'because the character is under pressure, is thinking in pictures, or is performing a role', and each of those is something an examiner will reward you for noticing. The difficulty is not between you and the play; it is in the play, where Shakespeare put it, for you to find.
