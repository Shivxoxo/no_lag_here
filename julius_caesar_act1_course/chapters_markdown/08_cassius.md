# Cassius — The Manipulation Masterclass

*What this chapter does: it takes the single most skilful piece of private persuasion in Act 1 — Cassius working on Brutus while the games are going on offstage — and slows it down until you can see every move. You will learn to read the seduction of Brutus as thirteen distinct stages, each with its own aim and technique; to name Cassius's tools with the precise vocabulary an examiner rewards; to watch him change his method completely when he turns to Casca in the storm; and to weigh, honestly and without oversimplifying, what actually drives him. By the end, you should be able to explain not just what Cassius says but why it works, why Shakespeare lets us see through it, and how this knowledge earns marks in Class 10 answers on Acts 3 to 5.*

## Why Cassius is the best teacher of persuasion you will ever meet

Most students meet Cassius as a label: 'the envious conspirator', 'the manipulator', 'the practical one'. Labels are the enemy of good literature answers. What makes Cassius worth several weeks of a scholar's attention is that Shakespeare shows us a persuader *at work*, in real time, on a man who is intelligent, suspicious and morally serious. Brutus is not a fool. He sees what Cassius is doing — he says so, in so many words, near the end of their talk. And yet Cassius still gets what he came for. That is the puzzle this chapter solves.

Hold on to one idea from the start. Cassius almost never *argues*. He does not produce evidence that Caesar has broken a law, ordered an execution or abolished an office. Instead he works on Brutus's picture of himself: his honour, his name, his ancestry, his fear of what Rome thinks of him. This is why the play's own word for what happens is not 'persuasion' but something darker. Alone on stage afterwards, Cassius asks, of Brutus, "who so firm that cannot be seduced?" Keep that word in mind; we shall return to it.

A note of honesty before we begin. Act 1 belongs to the Class IX portion of the ICSE syllabus, and your Class 10 board paper examines Acts 3, 4 and 5. But the paper constantly assumes you know how the conspiracy was born, why Brutus joined it, and what kind of man Cassius is when you meet him again quarrelling with Brutus in Act 4 or dying in Act 5. Everything you learn here is foundation, and the analytical vocabulary of persuasion you will build here is exactly what you need to analyse Antony's funeral oration in Act 3.

::: level LEVEL 1 — What happened?
After the Soothsayer's warning, Caesar's procession leaves for the games. Brutus and Cassius stay behind. Cassius complains that Brutus has been cold to him lately; Brutus explains that he is troubled by private thoughts. Cassius offers to be Brutus's 'mirror' and show him how highly Rome thinks of him. Two offstage shouts interrupt them; Brutus admits he fears the people are making Caesar king. Cassius then tells two stories against Caesar — a swimming race in the Tiber in which Caesar nearly drowned, and a fever in Spain during which Caesar shook and groaned — and argues that Caesar is no better a man than either of them. He reminds Brutus of his ancestor who drove out Rome's last king. Brutus promises to think it over and to meet again. Left alone, Cassius admits that he means to trick Brutus with forged letters. In Act 1 Scene 3, during the storm, Cassius recruits Casca by very different means and sends Cinna to plant the letters.
:::

::: level LEVEL 2 — What does it mean?
The conversation is a recruitment. Cassius is the instigator of a conspiracy that already exists (he tells Casca in Scene 3 that others are waiting at Pompey's Porch), and he needs Brutus because Brutus's reputation for honour will make the killing look like a public service rather than a private murder. Every stage of the talk is designed to lower Brutus's guard, raise his sense of his own importance, lower Caesar in his eyes, and make refusal feel like cowardice or betrayal of his family name. The soliloquy that closes the exchange tells us, in Cassius's own voice, that he knows he is bending an honourable man away from his natural disposition. Shakespeare therefore gives us a double view: we see the persuasion working on Brutus, and we see the persuader's private contempt for the very honour he is exploiting.
:::

## The seduction of Brutus in thirteen stages (Act 1 Scene 2)

**Setting the moment.** The procession has just swept off to the Lupercal running, taking Caesar, Antony, Calpurnia and the rest. Cassius makes a light social opening — "Will you go see the order of the course?" (the running of the race) — and Brutus declines: "I am not gamesome. I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony." He even tries to leave. Notice that Brutus has already separated himself from Antony's kind of man, the athlete and reveller, and from the festive crowd. He is a man who stays behind. Cassius, who "is a great observer" in Caesar's later phrase, has noticed this too, and it is precisely on the man who stays behind that he goes to work.

### Stage 1 — The complaint about lost gentleness

**What Cassius says:** He stops Brutus from leaving with a personal reproach: "Brutus, I do observe you now of late. / I have not from your eyes that gentleness / And show of love as I was wont to have. / You bear too stubborn and too strange a hand / Over your friend that loves you." ('Wont' means accustomed; 'gentleness' here is friendly warmth; the 'hand' is a rider's hand on the rein.)

**What Cassius means:** 'You have been avoiding me, and I am hurt. You are treating your friend like a horse held on a tight, unfamiliar rein.' The metaphor quietly accuses Brutus of coldness and control.

**What Cassius wants:** He wants Brutus to stop, to explain himself, and to feel that he owes his friend an account of his inner life. A man who has just apologised is a man whose guard is down. Cassius also wants information: what is really troubling Brutus?

**How Brutus receives it:** He immediately reassures and apologises. "Be not deceived. If I have veiled my look, / I turn the trouble of my countenance / Merely upon myself." He is "Vexèd I am / Of late with passions of some difference" (conflicting emotions) and "Conceptions only proper to myself" (private thoughts), and begs his friends not to "construe" (interpret) his neglect as anything more than "poor Brutus, with himself at war". He even counts Cassius among his "good friends".

**Rhetorical technique:** The reproach-as-invitation. Cassius takes the position of the injured party so that Brutus must come towards him to make amends. The equestrian (horse-riding) metaphor ('a stubborn and strange hand') is aristocratic and intimate at once — a nobleman's image, used between equals.

**Emotional effect:** Brutus is put on the defensive and made to confess that he is inwardly divided. He does not say what about; he does not need to. Cassius, who has observed him "now of late", can guess.

**Political effect:** Before a single word about Caesar has been spoken, Cassius has learned the one thing he most needed to know: the ground is already disturbed. Brutus is not contented. A discontented Brutus can be worked.

**Important keywords:** gentleness, wont, stubborn and strange hand, countenance (face, expression), passions of some difference, construe, "with himself at war".

**Later consequence:** Brutus's admission of inner war is the seed of his great orchard soliloquy in Act 2, where that private conflict becomes the reasoning by which he talks himself into killing a man he loves. Cassius did not create the conflict; he found it and fed it.

### Stage 2 — The mirror: "I, your glass"

**What Cassius says:** He pretends to have misread Brutus, and to have therefore kept back "Thoughts of great value, worthy cogitations" (weighty reflections). Then comes the question that changes everything: "Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?" Brutus answers with a commonplace of the age — "No, Cassius, for the eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things." Cassius agrees, and laments that Brutus has "no such mirrors as will turn / Your hidden worthiness into your eye". He adds that "many of the best respect in Rome, / Except immortal Caesar", have been "groaning underneath this age's yoke" and have "wished that noble Brutus had his eyes". When Brutus asks into what "dangers" he is being led, Cassius answers: "I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of."

**What Cassius means:** 'Rome's best men are suffering under Caesar and wish you could see yourself as they see you — as the man who should act. I will be the mirror that shows you that.'

**What Cassius wants:** To make Brutus see himself not with his own eyes but with a reflection Cassius controls. Note the sly move from 'I think' to 'Rome thinks': Cassius's opinion becomes the opinion of "the best respect in Rome". He is testing whether Brutus is moved by reputation. He is.

**How Brutus receives it:** With a flash of alarm and modesty: "Into what dangers would you lead me, Cassius, / That you would have me seek into myself / For that which is not in me?" The word "dangers" shows he already senses where this leads. But he does not walk away.

**Rhetorical technique:** Flattery disguised as service; the extended metaphor of the mirror. There is also a teasing withholding of information ('I have thoughts of great value — but I misjudged you, so I kept them buried'), which makes Brutus curious. And the phrase "Except immortal Caesar" is a small masterpiece of mock-reverence: it excludes Caesar from the honourable Romans while appearing to honour him.

**Emotional effect:** Brutus is told that he is admired more than he knows and that good men are waiting on him. For a man who loves "the name of honour", as he will shortly confess, nothing could be more pleasant to hear or harder to dismiss.

**Political effect:** The conspiracy acquires its justification: it is what 'Rome' wants. This is the germ of the forged letters, which will literally manufacture the voice of Rome that Cassius here merely claims to have overheard ("I have heard").

**Important keywords:** glass (mirror), reflection, shadow (image), "hidden worthiness", "the best respect in Rome", yoke (a wooden bar laid on the necks of oxen — here, servitude), "modestly discover" (reveal).

**Later consequence:** A mirror shows a reversed image, and Cassius's mirror shows Brutus a flattering, distorted Rome. In Act 3 Brutus will address the real Roman crowd, expecting the gratitude Cassius promised; Antony will show him what Rome actually thinks. The mirror was a lie, and the play makes Brutus pay for believing it.

### Stage 3 — The protest of sincerity: "Were I a common laughter"

**What Cassius says:** Before revealing anything, he defends his own character: "And be not jealous on me, gentle Brutus. / Were I a common laughter, or did use / To stale with ordinary oaths my love / To every new protester; if you know / That I do fawn on men and hug them hard / And after scandal them, or if you know / That I profess myself in banqueting / To all the rout, then hold me dangerous." ('Jealous on' is suspicious of; a 'common laughter' is a joker or object of ridicule; to 'stale' is to cheapen; a 'protester' is someone who professes friendship; to 'scandal' is to slander; 'the rout' is the rabble.)

**What Cassius means:** 'If I were the kind of man who swears friendship to everybody, flatters people and then slanders them behind their backs, and shouts my loyalties at drunken parties, then you would be right to distrust me. I am not that man.'

**What Cassius wants:** To neutralise the suspicion Brutus has just voiced with the word "dangers". He must establish his credit before he spends it.

**How Brutus receives it:** He has no time to reply — the first offstage shout interrupts. But the disclaimer clearly works: from this point Brutus never again questions Cassius's motives, only his own readiness.

**Rhetorical technique:** The disclaimer, sometimes called apophasis — mentioning the very things you wish to deny. Cassius lists the behaviour of a manipulator in vivid detail (fawning, hugging, slandering) precisely so as to distance himself from it. Rhetoricians have always known that a speaker who begins by establishing his own honesty gains a hearing for everything that follows.

**Emotional effect:** Reassurance. Brutus is invited to feel that this is intimate, serious talk between two austere (severe, self-denying) men who despise the flatterers and the crowd — a shared aristocratic contempt for "the rout".

**Political effect:** Cassius has separated himself from the ordinary, noisy politics of the festival and positioned this conversation as something graver. He has also spoken the word "dangerous" of himself — a word Caesar will use of him within the hour.

**Important keywords:** jealous (suspicious), common laughter, stale, protester, fawn, scandal, rout, "hold me dangerous".

**Later consequence:** The irony is total. In his soliloquy Cassius will admit that he is doing exactly what he here denies — winning Brutus by pleasing him and working on his nature. And in Act 3 a far greater speaker, Antony, will use the same move in his funeral oration, protesting to the crowd that he is a plain, blunt man with no gift for stirring people — a claim he makes while doing exactly that. Shakespeare's most effective persuaders always begin by denying that they are persuaders at all.

::: textnote Laughter or laugher?
The Folger text prints "Were I a common laughter"; the MIT and Gutenberg texts, following older editors, print 'laugher'. 'Laughter' would mean a laughing-stock or a buffoon; 'laugher' would mean a man who laughs easily and cheaply. Either reading gives the same sense: 'were I a frivolous man'. Both are legitimate; do not lose sleep over which your edition prints, but quote whichever your edition gives.
:::

### Stage 4 — Exploiting the shout and Brutus's fear

**What Cassius says:** A flourish and a shout come from the offstage crowd. Brutus reacts first: "What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." Cassius pounces: "Ay, do you fear it? / Then must I think you would not have it so."

**What Cassius means:** 'You said *fear*. A man does not fear what he wants. So you are against Caesar being king — you have just told me so.'

**What Cassius wants:** A commitment from Brutus's own mouth, before any argument has been made. He wants Brutus to hear himself take a side.

**How Brutus receives it:** He concedes, with the qualification on which the whole tragedy will turn: "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well." Then impatience: "But wherefore do you hold me here so long? / What is it that you would impart to me?" Brutus wants the point, and wants it framed as public duty — he will listen only "If it be aught toward the general good" ('aught' is anything; the 'general good' is the public welfare).

**Rhetorical technique:** Seizing the interruption. Cassius could not have scripted the shout, but he uses it as a third speaker in the conversation, letting the crowd 'say' what he dare not. Then a forced inference — "Then must I think" — which dresses a leap as a logical necessity.

**Emotional effect:** Brutus's anxiety about Caesar's kingship, which he has been carrying privately, is now spoken aloud and shared. A fear shared with a sympathiser becomes a bond.

**Political effect:** Cassius has not yet made one open charge against Caesar, and already Brutus has declared himself against a crown. The persuader has made the target supply the conclusion.

**Important keywords:** fear, king, "the general good", aught, impart.

**Later consequence:** "yet I love him well" is the half-line Brutus never escapes. In Act 2 he reasons that he has no personal grievance against Caesar and must kill him for what he *might* become; in Act 3 he tells the crowd he loved Caesar; at Philippi he still speaks of him. Cassius heard the love in this line and worked around it rather than against it — a sign of real skill.

### Stage 5 — The honour pledge: "honour is the subject of my story"

**What Cassius says:** Brutus lays down his terms: "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently; / For let the gods so speed me as I love / The name of honour more than I fear death." ('Indifferently' is impartially; 'speed' is prosper.) Cassius answers: "I know that virtue to be in you, Brutus, / As well as I do know your outward favour. / Well, honour is the subject of my story." ('Favour' is face.)

**What Cassius means:** 'I know your inner character as well as I know your face — and honour is exactly what I want to talk about.'

**What Cassius wants:** To make his cause and Brutus's ruling value appear identical. Whatever Cassius was going to say, it is now going to be called 'honour'.

**How Brutus receives it:** He has been given what he asked for — a matter of honour — and settles in to listen. He speaks only once more before the ancestor passage.

**Rhetorical technique:** Vocabulary mirroring. Brutus uses the word honour twice in four lines; Cassius adopts it instantly and makes it his title. Persuaders who echo the target's own words seem to be agreeing with him even while leading him. Note also the last word of "honour is the subject of my story": Cassius announces a narrative, not an argument, and a story is far harder to refute than a claim.

**Emotional effect:** Brutus feels understood and respected. His most serious self has been addressed.

**Political effect:** The conspiracy is now labelled, in Brutus's hearing, as honourable. From this moment the word will cling to it — through Cassius's later phrase to Casca about an enterprise "Of honourable-dangerous consequence", to Brutus's speeches in Act 3.

**Important keywords:** honour, "the name of honour", virtue, favour, indifferently, story.

**Later consequence:** Look hard at what Brutus actually said: he loves "The *name* of honour". Not honour alone — its name, its reputation. Cassius has just discovered that Brutus can be reached through what people say about him. The forged letters, "all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name", are the direct application of this discovery.

### Stage 6 — Equality: "I was born free as Caesar"

**What Cassius says:** "I cannot tell what you and other men / Think of this life; but, for my single self, / I had as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself. / I was born free as Caesar; so were you; / We both have fed as well, and we can both / Endure the winter's cold as well as he." ('I had as lief not be' means 'I would as soon not exist'.)

**What Cassius means:** 'I would rather die than stand in awe of a man who is no more than I am. Caesar was born free, exactly as we were; he eats the same food and feels the same cold. What is he that we should bow?'

**What Cassius wants:** To remove Caesar's halo by placing him in the plain category of 'a man like us', and to make continued submission feel like a personal humiliation for Brutus too — "so were you".

**How Brutus receives it:** In silence. Shakespeare gives him no line for over forty verses. Silence on stage is not absence: Brutus is listening, and the actor must show the words landing.

**Rhetorical technique:** Levelling by a triad of shared human facts: birth, food, cold. Cassius also speaks in the first person — "for my single self" — so that the idea arrives as personal conviction rather than as a demand on Brutus, which would provoke resistance.

**Emotional effect:** Shame. The word that does the work is awe ("In awe of such a thing as I myself"): awe is what one feels before a god, and to feel it before an equal is degrading.

**Political effect:** This is the aristocratic republican creed in miniature. Rome's nobles were meant to be equals in a shared enterprise; one man "grown so great" that others feel awe is, by definition, a violation of the republic. Cassius's motive here may be pride as much as principle, but the principle is real and Brutus shares it.

**Important keywords:** free, awe, "for my single self", lief, equality.

**Later consequence:** 'I would as soon not exist as live in awe' is the first sounding of a note that grows louder: in Act 1 Scene 3 Cassius will say "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius", meaning suicide, and at Philippi he acts on it, ordering his bondman Pindarus to kill him. The freedom he preaches is finally the freedom to die.

### Stage 7 — The Tiber anecdote

**What Cassius says:** On "a raw and gusty day", with "The troubled Tiber chafing with her shores", Caesar challenged him: "Dar'st thou, Cassius, now / Leap in with me into this angry flood / And swim to yonder point?" Cassius leapt in "Accoutred as I was" (fully dressed, in his gear) "And bade him follow; so indeed he did." They fought the torrent "With lusty sinews", but "ere we could arrive the point proposed, / Caesar cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!'" Cassius carried him out as "Aeneas, our great ancestor" carried "The old Anchises" from burning Troy. Then the turn: "And this man / Is now become a god, and Cassius is / A wretched creature and must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him."

**What Cassius means:** 'The man you are all cheering could not finish a swim he himself proposed. I saved his life. And now I have to bow if he so much as glances at me.'

**What Cassius wants:** To replace the public image of Caesar — the triumphant general in the chariot — with a private image of Caesar helpless and crying for help; and to make the present order (Cassius bowing, Caesar nodding) look absurd.

**How Brutus receives it:** Still in silence. Notice that the story is not about Brutus at all. Cassius has drifted from his target to himself.

**Rhetorical technique:** The exemplum, or illustrative anecdote — the oldest tool in the orator's kit, because a story is remembered when an argument is forgotten. Inside it sits an epic simile: Cassius as Aeneas, founder-hero of Rome, and Caesar as Anchises, the aged father who must be carried. The simile is flattering to the speaker and quietly ageist about Caesar. The line ends on antithesis (the setting of opposites side by side for contrast): "Is now become a god" against "A wretched creature".

**Emotional effect:** Intended to arouse contempt for Caesar's frailty. But there is a second, unintended effect on an alert listener: Cassius's resentment is showing. The "wretched creature" who "must bend his body" is a man burning with humiliation.

**Political effect:** Cassius has attacked not Caesar's policies but his body. This becomes the play's quietest argument against the deification (treating as a god) of any man: gods do not sink.

**Important keywords:** Tiber, "angry flood", accoutred, "hearts of controversy" (spirits eager to contend), Aeneas, Anchises, "become a god", "bend his body".

**Later consequence:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the swimming race appears nowhere in Plutarch; scholars generally treat it as Shakespeare's invention. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** that choice matters. Shakespeare could have given Cassius a documented grievance; instead he gives him a story that reveals the storyteller's vanity as much as Caesar's weakness. When Caesar, minutes later, describes Cassius as a man never "at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves", we have just heard the proof.

### Stage 8 — The fever: "this god did shake"

**What Cassius says:** "He had a fever when he was in Spain, / And when the fit was on him, I did mark / How he did shake. 'Tis true, this god did shake. / His coward lips did from their colour fly, / And that same eye whose bend doth awe the world / Did lose his lustre. I did hear him groan." Caesar's tongue, which "bade the Romans / Mark him and write his speeches in their books", cried "Give me some drink, Titinius" — "As a sick girl." Then the summing-up: "it doth amaze me / A man of such a feeble temper should / So get the start of the majestic world / And bear the palm alone." ('Fit' is seizure; 'bend' is glance; 'temper' is constitution; to 'get the start of' is to outstrip; the 'palm' is the victor's prize.)

**What Cassius means:** 'I watched this so-called god tremble, turn pale, groan and whimper for water like a sick little girl. How has such a weakling come to hold the whole world's prize by himself?'

**What Cassius wants:** To finish the demolition of Caesar's divinity begun in the Tiber, and to make Caesar's supremacy seem not merely unjust but unnatural — an inversion of the order in which the strong lead.

**How Brutus receives it:** Silence again, until the second shout.

**Rhetorical technique:** The body as argument. Each detail is physical — lips, eye, tongue, groan — and each is set against a claim of divinity. Look closely at "His coward lips did from their colour fly": the lips are cowards, and the blood leaving them is imagined as soldiers deserting their regimental colours. Caesar the general is betrayed by his own face. The simile "As a sick girl" is deliberate gender-shaming, and the mockery of a man who wanted Romans to "write his speeches in their books" turns Caesar's fame as a writer against him.

**Emotional effect:** Disgust and indignation. Brutus is meant to feel that awe of Caesar is a kind of category error.

**Political effect:** Cassius here fuses two ideas the play will keep apart and together: Caesar's physical frailty and Caesar's political monopoly. The republican objection is contained in the final word of "And bear the palm alone" — honour in Rome was meant to be shared among colleagues, not carried off by one man.

**Important keywords:** fever, fit, "this god did shake", "coward lips", colour, lustre, "As a sick girl", "feeble temper", "bear the palm alone".

**Later consequence:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Plutarch does say that Caesar suffered from epileptic seizures and that the illness first attacked him in Corduba, in Spain; Shakespeare's 'fever in Spain' has that seed, though making Cassius an eyewitness is the dramatist's addition. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT:** within minutes Casca will report that Caesar "swooned and fell down" in the marketplace and Brutus will diagnose "the falling sickness". Cassius's private story is publicly confirmed, which is why the audience leaves the scene half-believing him. The feminising of weakness also prepares his Act 1 Scene 3 taunt to Casca that Romans have become "womanish".

### Stage 9 — The Colossus and "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars"

**What Cassius says:** The second shout comes. Brutus: "Another general shout! / I do believe that these applauses are / For some new honours that are heaped on Caesar." Cassius: "Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves. / Men at some time are masters of their fates. / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings."

**What Cassius means:** 'Caesar stands over the world like a giant statue while we scurry between his legs looking for somewhere shameful to die. There are moments when men can control their destiny. Our slavery is not fate's doing — it is our own fault for accepting it.'

**What Cassius wants:** To turn shame into responsibility. If servitude were fate, Brutus could sigh and accept it; if it is a fault "in ourselves", inaction becomes a moral failure — Brutus's failure.

**How Brutus receives it:** Silence still, but the maxim is aimed at exactly the kind of mind Brutus has — philosophical, self-examining, hard on itself.

**Rhetorical technique:** A hyperbolic (deliberately exaggerated) image followed by a sententia (a compact moral maxim). The picture of the Colossus reduces the Roman nobility to insects, and the phrase "dishonourable graves" tells Brutus that even his death will be shameful if he does nothing. Then the register (the level and tone of the language) changes completely: from grotesque image to calm philosophy, and to the intimate "dear Brutus". Cassius's rhythm — heat, then cool reason — is itself persuasive.

**Emotional effect:** Humiliation ("underlings" is a word for servants), then a bracing sense of agency: "masters of their fates".

**Political effect:** The argument has moved from 'Caesar is weak' to 'we are guilty'. This is a call to action disguised as a philosophical observation.

**Important keywords:** Colossus, "petty men", "dishonourable graves", "masters of their fates", stars (astrological destiny), underlings.

**Later consequence:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the historical Cassius was an Epicurean, a school that denied that gods or stars direct human affairs, and this speech is the purest Epicurean moment in the play. Yet the same Cassius in Act 1 Scene 3 will read the storm as a warning from heaven, and at Philippi (Act 5) he will confess that he has begun to credit omens. The line about our stars is therefore also a trap Shakespeare sets for its speaker: the man who denies fate is the one fate destroys.

::: history The Colossus of Rhodes
The Colossus was a gigantic bronze statue of the sun-god Helios at the harbour of Rhodes, built around 280 BC and counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. The image of it straddling the harbour mouth, with ships sailing between its legs, is a medieval legend rather than ancient fact — but it is that legend Shakespeare's image relies on. For your purposes: the Colossus means overwhelming, monumental size, and the picture of men walking between its legs means the shrinking of everyone else.
:::

### Stage 10 — The weighing of names

**What Cassius says:** "'Brutus' and 'Caesar'—what should be in that 'Caesar'? / Why should that name be sounded more than yours? / Write them together, yours is as fair a name; / Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; / Weigh them, it is as heavy; conjure with 'em, / 'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar.'" Then: "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great? Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" and the bitter pun, "Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man."

**What Cassius means:** 'Take the two names and test them any way you like — written, spoken, weighed, used in magic — and Brutus is the equal of Caesar. So what has Caesar been eating to grow so monstrously? This age is disgraced; Rome has run out of noble men. Rome is only *room* — empty space — when it holds just one man.'

**What Cassius wants:** To empty Caesar's name of its magic and fill Brutus's with equal power; to make Brutus feel that his own name is a public resource he is failing to use.

**How Brutus receives it:** Silence, but this is the passage that most directly flatters the thing Brutus has admitted he loves: "The name of honour" — his name.

**Rhetorical technique:** A run of imperatives — write, sound, weigh, conjure — as if conducting a scientific demonstration. Rhetorical questions ("what should be in that 'Caesar'?"). Apostrophe (direct address to an abstraction) in "Age, thou art shamed!" and "Rome, thou hast lost". The food image, "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed", picks up "We both have fed as well" and makes Caesar's greatness look like an unnatural, monstrous growth — an idea Cassius will repeat in Act 1 Scene 3 with "prodigious grown". And a pun: in Elizabethan pronunciation *Rome* and *room* sounded alike.

**Emotional effect:** Pride. Brutus is told that his name can "start a spirit" — raise a ghost — as readily as Caesar's. Nothing in the scene is more seductive to a man who loves the name of honour.

**Political effect:** Cassius delegitimises Caesar's authority by treating it as a mere sound. If the name is empty, the power that rests on it is an illusion that only lasts as long as men consent to be awed.

**Important keywords:** name, sound, weigh, conjure, "start a spirit", meat, "grown so great", "breed of noble bloods", Rome/room.

**Later consequence:** This is the argument the play most cruelly overturns. After the Ides, Caesar's name proves mightier dead than alive: Antony conjures with it before the crowd, Caesar's spirit walks at Sardis and Philippi, and Brutus, dying, still speaks to Caesar. Cassius said the name was empty; the last two acts show that it was not.

### Stage 11 — The ancestor: "There was a Brutus once"

**What Cassius says:** "O, you and I have heard our fathers say / There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king." ('Brooked' is tolerated; to 'keep his state' is to hold court like a monarch.)

**What Cassius means:** 'Your ancestor would as soon have let the devil himself rule Rome as let a king. What would he say of you?'

**What Cassius wants:** To make the case personal in the deepest possible way: not 'Rome needs a hero' but 'your blood obliges you'. Refusal is now not caution but a betrayal of the family name.

**How Brutus receives it:** This finally draws him out, and his reply is remarkable for its self-awareness: "That you do love me, I am nothing jealous. / What you would work me to, I have some aim." ('Nothing jealous' is 'not at all doubtful'; 'aim' is a guess.) He knows he is being worked on. He asks for time — "What you have said / I will consider" — and then gives Cassius more than he asked for: "Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us." ('Repute himself' is 'consider himself'.)

**Rhetorical technique:** Appeal to lineage, using a historical exemplum. The hyperbole "Th' eternal devil" is an anachronism — the Christian devil in pagan Rome — but Shakespeare's audience would have felt its force: even Satan would be preferable to a king. The framing "you and I have heard our fathers say" makes the memory shared, so that Cassius seems to be reminding, not preaching.

**Emotional effect:** Brutus is made to stand in his ancestor's shadow. His answer — he would rather be a nobody from the countryside than a Roman under a king — shows how completely the appeal has landed, even as he insists on thinking further.

**Political effect:** The conspiracy acquires its founding legend. The republic began when a Brutus expelled a king; it can be saved when a Brutus stops one.

**Important keywords:** ancestor, brooked, "keep his state", "eternal devil", villager, "son of Rome", "work me to", aim.

**Later consequence:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Lucius Junius Brutus led the expulsion of Tarquin the Proud in 509 BC, and Marcus Brutus claimed descent from him; Plutarch records that anonymous messages — 'Brutus, art thou asleep?', 'Thou art not really Brutus' — were left on his praetor's tribunal (the raised platform from which he gave judgement). **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT:** Cassius will convert those messages into letters, one of which Cinna is to fix "Upon old Brutus' statue". The ancestor is not a passing flourish; it is the target Cassius keeps aiming at.

### Stage 12 — The retreat: "my weak words"

**What Cassius says:** "I am glad that my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus." Caesar's train returns; Cassius quietly hands over the next task: "pluck Casca by the sleeve, / And he will, after his sour fashion, tell you / What hath proceeded worthy note today." After Casca's report, it is Brutus who proposes the next meeting: "Tomorrow, if you please to speak with me, / I will come home to you; or, if you will, / Come home to me, and I will wait for you." Cassius accepts with a parting shot: "I will do so. Till then, think of the world."

**What Cassius means:** 'I have said little, and clumsily, and yet look how much fire it has struck from you — so the fire was already in you.' And at parting: 'Think about the state of things.'

**What Cassius wants:** To stop while ahead. Having drawn Brutus's declaration, he must not push into a refusal. He also wants Brutus to feel that any spark of rebellion is Brutus's own.

**How Brutus receives it:** The target now pursues the persuader. Brutus, who tried to leave at the start, is the one arranging the next conversation and offering to wait for Cassius at his house.

**Rhetorical technique:** False modesty — the old orator's trick of calling one's words weak so that the listener credits the ideas to himself. The image is flint and steel: "struck" fire from Brutus, whose "honourable mettle" (spirit, punning on metal) Cassius will name in the soliloquy. Then a deliberately vague parting line, "think of the world", which plants without pressing.

**Emotional effect:** Relief and respect. Brutus is not being hustled. He has been treated as the senior partner whose judgement is awaited.

**Political effect:** A second meeting is fixed and the recruit has volunteered for it. The conspiracy now has a calendar.

**Important keywords:** "weak words", fire, struck, "sour fashion", "think of the world".

**Later consequence:** The meeting takes place in Act 2, when the conspirators come to Brutus's house before dawn — and by then Cassius's letters have done their work, so that Brutus greets them already decided. Everything about this scene's ending is engineered to make that later scene possible.

### Stage 13 — The soliloquy

**What Cassius says:** Alone: "Well, Brutus, thou art noble. Yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed. Therefore it is meet / That noble minds keep ever with their likes; / For who so firm that cannot be seduced? / Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus. / If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me." Then the plan: "I will this night / In several hands in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens, / Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name, wherein obscurely / Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at." And the couplet: "And after this, let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure."

**What Cassius means:** 'Brutus is noble, but his noble nature can be worked away from its natural bent, the way metal is worked. Noble men should keep to their own kind — because anyone can be seduced. Caesar dislikes me but loves Brutus. Tonight I will throw letters through his windows in different handwritings, as if from many citizens, all about how highly Rome regards him, and hinting indirectly at Caesar's ambition. Let Caesar make himself secure — we will shake him, or suffer worse.'

**What Cassius wants:** Now the honest answer: Brutus, by any means. Where Rome's voice does not exist, Cassius will manufacture it.

**How Brutus receives it:** He is not there — that is the point. This is the one moment in the scene when we hear Cassius unperformed.

**Rhetorical technique:** The soliloquy convention: alone on stage, a character tells the audience the truth. Cassius speaks in maxims ("who so firm that cannot be seduced?") and in a plan, and he closes on a rhyming couplet whose verb — ‘shake’ — echoes his own "this god did shake". The letters use indirection ("obscurely", "glancèd at"): a hint works on a proud man where a demand would fail.

**Emotional effect:** On the audience, a complicated one: admiration for the skill, unease at the cynicism, and a new pity for Brutus, who has just walked off thinking himself the wiser man.

**Political effect:** The conspiracy that will call itself honourable is founded, at this instant, on forgery. Public opinion — the force that will decide everything in Act 3 — is here first faked.

**Important keywords:** mettle, wrought, disposed, meet, "keep ever with their likes", seduced, "bear me hard" (bear a grudge against), humour, "several hands", "great opinion", obscurely, glanced at.

**Later consequence:** In Act 1 Scene 3 Cassius hands the letters to Cinna with precise instructions; in Act 2 Brutus reads one and completes its hints himself. And the whole play is shaped by what Cassius says here about noble minds keeping "with their likes": he knows that Brutus's nobility is a different substance from his own, and the Act 4 quarrel is the sound of those two metals grinding.

::: textnote A genuine crux: "He should not humour me"
Editors disagree about who 'he' is in "If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me." Reading one: 'if I were in Brutus's place, loved by Caesar, Caesar's affection would not win me over' — 'he' is Caesar, and 'humour' means to indulge or please into compliance. Reading two: 'if our positions were swapped, Cassius would not be able to work on me as I am working on Brutus' — 'he' is the imagined Cassius. The first is the more common; the second makes Cassius admit that Brutus is easier to lead than he is. Both are defensible. In an answer, you may say 'the line is ambiguous, but on either reading Cassius is confessing that Brutus is being led'.
:::

::: level LEVEL 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way?
Shakespeare had a simpler option: Plutarch's anonymous messages could have done the recruiting, and Brutus could have agonised alone. Instead he stages a two-hander of some hundred and fifty lines in which one man works on another. Three reasons. First, drama needs pressure between people, and this scene lets us watch pressure being applied and absorbed, with the offstage shouts as a pulse. Second, by giving Cassius stories (the Tiber, the fever) rather than charges, Shakespeare makes the case against Caesar personal and uncheckable — which is exactly how real political persuasion works. Third, by ending with the soliloquy, he refuses to let the audience be seduced along with Brutus. We are made wiser than the tragic hero at the very moment he thinks himself wisest. That gap between what Brutus knows and what we know is where the tragedy lives.
:::

::: insight Why Cassius's honesty about his own dishonesty is dramatically brilliant
A lesser playwright would have kept Cassius's cynicism hidden, or revealed it late as a twist. Shakespeare reveals it at once, and the effect is far richer. First, it splits our response: we have just admired a superb performance, and now we are shown the performer counting the take. Admiration and distrust must live in us together, and that is what makes Cassius a character rather than a villain. Second, it makes Brutus's tragedy visible from the start. He leaves believing he has been asked, honourably, to reflect; we know he is about to be flooded with counterfeit letters. Every later scene in which Brutus insists on the conspiracy's purity is shadowed by our knowledge of how it began. Third — and this is the point most school notes miss — the soliloquy does not make Cassius's arguments false. Caesar *did* fall down in the marketplace; the Republic *was* in danger. Shakespeare separates the truth of a case from the honesty of the man who makes it, and leaves the audience holding both. Finally, the confession is itself a clue to Cassius's motives: the one grievance he names when no one is listening is not liberty but "Caesar doth bear me hard". Alone, he speaks of himself.
:::

## Cassius's toolkit: the techniques, named and analysed

The stages showed you the sequence. Now separate the tools, because an examiner's question — 'How does Cassius persuade Brutus?' — is asking you to name techniques and show them working. Each entry below gives a definition you can reuse, the best textual evidence, and one observation that lifts your answer above a list.

### Flattery

Flattery is praise offered to gain influence. Cassius's is of a superior kind: he never praises Brutus directly for long, because Brutus would recoil. Instead he *reports* praise — "many of the best respect in Rome" wish "that noble Brutus had his eyes" — and offers to be the ‘glass’ in which Brutus can see his own "hidden worthiness". The flattery is attributed to others and framed as a service. Observation: the one time Cassius praises Brutus sincerely is when Brutus cannot hear it — "Well, Brutus, thou art noble" — and even that is the prelude to planning his corruption.

### Comparison

Comparison sets two things side by side so that one is diminished. Cassius compares Caesar to himself (the swimmer who sank against the swimmer who saved him), Caesar to Brutus (the two names written, sounded and weighed), Caesar to a god he plainly is not ("this god did shake"), and Caesar to a Colossus beside "petty men". Observation: notice that every comparison has the same hidden premise — that Caesar and other nobles are *the same kind of thing*, so that any elevation of him is theft from them. That premise is the aristocratic republican creed, and it is also the voice of a man who cannot bear to be second.

### Anecdote

An anecdote is a short story told to prove a point. Cassius's two — the Tiber and the fever — do what no argument could: they let Brutus *see* Caesar helpless. Their power is also their weakness. Both rest on Cassius's word; both cast Cassius as the strong man and Caesar as the weakling; and neither is told by Plutarch in this form — the Tiber race is absent from the ancient sources altogether, and the fever, though Plutarch places Caesar's first seizure in Spain, acquires its eyewitness only in Shakespeare — which suggests the dramatist wanted us to sense the teller shaping the tale. Observation: an anecdote is a form of selective truth. Even if every word were accurate, choosing to remember Caesar sinking rather than Caesar conquering Gaul is a choice about what Brutus should picture.

### Rhetorical questions

A rhetorical question is asked not for an answer but to make the hearer supply one. "Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?" opens the mirror; "Ay, do you fear it?" traps Brutus into a stance; "what should be in that 'Caesar'? / Why should that name be sounded more than yours?" invite Brutus to answer 'nothing' and 'it should not'; "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed?" makes greatness sound like gluttony. Observation: the questions cluster where Cassius wants Brutus to feel he has *reached* a conclusion rather than been given one. A conclusion you think you reached yourself is one you defend.

### Appeals to honour

Honour, for a Roman noble, meant public recognition of virtue. Cassius does not argue that resisting Caesar is honourable; he simply announces "honour is the subject of my story" the moment Brutus names honour as his price, then fills the story with things Brutus will find dishonourable — awe of an equal, "dishonourable graves", being "underlings". Observation: Cassius's appeal is to honour as *reputation*, which is what Brutus confessed to loving ("The name of honour"). Brutus's own idea of honour is more inward — conscience, consistency, the "general good". The gap between these two honours is invisible in Act 1 and enormous by Act 4.

### Appeals to Roman liberty

Liberty (Latin *libertas*) was the Republic's founding value: no Roman should be subject to another Roman's will. Cassius invokes it as "I was born free as Caesar", as "this age's yoke", and through the ancestor who would not tolerate a king. Observation: Cassius's liberty is aristocratic. It is the freedom of noblemen not to bow, not the freedom of the crowd, whom he despises as "the rout". When Brutus later speaks of liberty, he means something closer to the freedom of all Rome. Same word, different content — and the conspiracy never notices.

### Appeals to Brutus's reputation and ancestry

These are the most personal weapons in the scene, and Cassius saves them for late. Reputation: "the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name" is, by his own admission, the lever of the forged letters. Ancestry: "There was a Brutus once". Observation: both appeals work by making Brutus answerable to people who are not present — dead ancestors and imagined citizens — so that the actual man present, Cassius, seems merely to be their messenger. That is why Brutus never treats Cassius as the source of pressure: the pressure appears to come from Rome and from his own blood.

### Caesar's weaknesses — the body as argument

Cassius's most original tactic is to argue against Caesar's *body*: the near-drowning, the shaking, the pale "coward lips", the groan, the cry for drink "As a sick girl". This works because the case for Caesar's supremacy rests on his being more than a man — "immortal Caesar", "this god". Every physical failure is a refutation. Observation: Shakespeare confirms and complicates the tactic within the same scene. Casca reports the swoon in the marketplace; Caesar himself mentions his deaf ear. The body really is weak. But the play's later acts insist that Caesar's *spirit* is the thing that matters, and against a spirit Cassius's argument has no purchase.

### Shared Roman identity — "We both have fed as well"

Cassius keeps constructing a 'we' — "so were you", "We both have fed as well", "you and I have heard our fathers say", "we petty men", "we are underlings". Shared identity is the soil in which persuasion grows: one accepts from a fellow what one would reject from a stranger. Observation: the 'we' shifts its meaning as the scene goes on. At first it is 'we two friends'; then 'we equals of Caesar'; then 'we shamed Romans'; and finally, in the soliloquy, the 'we' of the couplet — "we will shake him" — is the conspiracy, which Brutus has not yet joined. Cassius has been speaking for a group all along and only reveals it when alone.

### Forged letters — the Scene 2 soliloquy and the Scene 3 instructions

In Scene 2 the plan is stated: letters "In several hands" (different handwritings) "As if they came from several citizens", praising Brutus and hinting "obscurely" at Caesar's ambition. In Scene 3 it is executed with care: Cinna is to "lay it in the Praetor's chair, / Where Brutus may but find it", to "throw this / In at his window", and to "set this up with wax / Upon old Brutus' statue". Each placement is chosen for meaning — the chair of Brutus's office, the privacy of his house, the statue of his ancestor — so that Rome seems to speak from every direction. Observation: the letters are the mirror stage made literal. In Scene 2 Cassius claimed to be a glass reflecting Rome's opinion; in Scene 3 he manufactures the reflections. Note the moral cost he accepts without a flicker: a conspiracy 'for honour' begins in fraud.

### Selective truth — what Cassius leaves out

Selective truth is the use of accurate facts arranged to mislead by omission. Consider what Brutus never hears in this scene. That Cassius fought against Caesar in the civil war and was pardoned by him (**HISTORICAL RECORD**: Cassius commanded Pompey's fleet, was forgiven after Pharsalus and made praetor by Caesar in 44 BC — as was Brutus). That Caesar's clemency (his mercy to defeated enemies) was itself famous. That Caesar's public conduct can be read in his favour — as Casca is about to report, Caesar has been refusing a crown while they talk, and when the report comes Cassius instantly turns the refusal into another charge, "we have the falling sickness". That a conspiracy already exists and men are waiting at "Pompey's Porch". That the 'voice of Rome' is about to be forged. Observation: so far as we can tell, Cassius tells Brutus no outright lie in Scene 2. He simply arranges true and half-true things so that only one conclusion is visible, and saves the outright fraud for paper.

### Emotional manipulation

All of the above are forms of it, but it is worth naming the emotional sequence as such, because it is the real architecture of the scene: guilt (the neglected friend) → curiosity (the buried thoughts) → reassurance (the disclaimer) → anxiety (the shouts) → pride (honour, the names) → contempt (Caesar's body) → shame (underlings) → obligation (the ancestor) → relief (the retreat). Each emotion opens the door for the next. Observation: nowhere does Cassius ask Brutus to *decide*. He works only on feeling and identity and leaves the decision to Brutus's own reasoning — which is why Brutus will always believe the decision was his.

::: insight Persuading and seducing: "who so firm that cannot be seduced?"
Persuasion, in the classical sense, addresses the reason: it offers grounds and invites judgement. Seduction addresses desire and self-image: it offers a flattering picture of who you are and lets the picture do the reasoning. Cassius uses the second word of himself, and the choice is exact. Look back at the thirteen stages and ask a simple question: at what point does Cassius give Brutus a *reason* to believe Caesar is a tyrant? He never does. No law broken, no citizen wronged, no office abolished. What Brutus receives instead is a series of pictures — Brutus admired, Brutus's name weighed equal, Caesar sinking, Caesar shaking, the ancestor watching — and a series of feelings attached to them. The word 'firm' matters too. Brutus's firmness — his constancy, his refusal to be moved by pressure — is his chief virtue, the thing the whole play admires in him. Cassius regards it as a lock to be picked. That is what seduction does: it treats a person's best quality as an obstacle. And here is the cruel joke of the line: Cassius's question assumes that *no one* is beyond seduction, himself included. He may be right about himself too: in Act 1 Scene 3 the sceptic either exploits a storm he does not believe in or is half-seduced by it — the section on his motives below weighs the two readings.
:::

## Second case study: the recruitment of Casca (Act 1 Scene 3)

A month has passed (Cicero asks whether Caesar comes to the Capitol "tomorrow" — the Ides, the 15th of March). It is night — "it is after midnight", Cassius will say before the two men leave — in a storm that drops fire. Shakespeare has just let Cicero say what may be the wisest thing in the play about omens — "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves" — and then sends Cicero home. What follows is a live demonstration of his warning: Cassius construing the heavens to his own purpose. Watch how completely his method changes when the material changes.

### Move one: the omen reinterpreted

Casca is terrified. He has his sword out; he has seen a slave's hand burn unharmed, a lion by the Capitol, women who swore they saw men on fire, an owl at noon. He believes "there is a civil strife in heaven" or that the world, "too saucy with the gods", has provoked them. Cassius enters, calls out "Who's there?", and gets the answer "A Roman" — Casca's fear has made him cling to his identity, and Cassius will use it. Then the first stroke: "A very pleasing night to honest men." Casca: "Who ever knew the heavens menace so?" Cassius: "Those that have known the Earth so full of faults." In two lines the storm has been turned from a threat into a verdict — heaven's anger at wrongdoing on earth.

Cassius then performs his own fearlessness: he has walked the streets "unbracèd" (doublet unfastened), "bared my bosom to the thunder-stone", and stood "Even in the aim and very flash of it". This is theatre, and it is meant to be; a frightened man is shamed by a fearless one. When Casca protests that "It is the part of men to fear and tremble" before the gods' "tokens", Cassius attacks: "You are dull, Casca, and those sparks of life / That should be in a Roman you do want" ('want' is lack). Then the reinterpretation proper: the prodigies (unnatural wonders, taken as warnings) are "instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state" — where 'state' means both 'condition' and 'government'. The omens are not about the gods; they are about Caesar. Cassius clinches it with a riddle: a man "Most like this dreadful night", who "thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars", yet is "no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action" — "prodigious grown". Casca names the answer: "'Tis Caesar that you mean, is it not, Cassius?" And Cassius, exactly as with Brutus, lets the target supply the conclusion, adding a layer of deniability: "Let it be who it is."

### Move two: the manliness taunt

"For Romans now / Have thews and limbs like to their ancestors. / But, woe the while, our fathers' minds are dead, / And we are governed with our mothers' spirits. / Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish." ('Thews' are muscles; 'sufferance' is patient endurance.) The ancestor argument returns, but where Brutus received a named forefather, Casca gets a collective shaming: Roman bodies are as strong as ever, Roman spirit has turned female. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** this is the crudest instrument in Cassius's kit, and he does not use it on Brutus. He judges, rightly, that Casca — ‘blunt’, in Brutus's word, and ‘sour’, in Cassius's own — can be moved by an insult to his manhood where Brutus would have been repelled. Casca's response shows the taunt working: he volunteers intelligence, that the senators "Mean to establish Caesar as a king" tomorrow. The recruit is now contributing.

### Move three: the suicide-as-freedom speech

"I know where I will wear this dagger then; / Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius." Notice the third-person self-naming: Cassius speaking of Cassius as if of a hero in a history. The gods, he says, "make the weak most strong" and "you tyrants do defeat" through this one power. Then the catalogue, driven by the repeated 'nor': "Nor stony tower, nor walls of beaten brass, / Nor airless dungeon, nor strong links of iron, / Can be retentive to the strength of spirit; / But life, being weary of these worldly bars, / Never lacks power to dismiss itself." ('Retentive' means able to hold in.) The idea — that the free man always holds the exit of death — was a Roman commonplace, and Cassius states it magnificently. Its function here is tactical: it sets a standard of fearlessness that Casca must match or be shamed. Casca matches it at once: "So can I. / So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity." Watch the follower echo the leader's rhythm and image — the surest sign that recruitment is working.

Cassius then converts the philosophy into politics: "And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then?" If death is always available, servitude is chosen; therefore Caesar is a tyrant only because Romans allow it — "he would not be a wolf / But that he sees the Romans are but sheep; / He were no lion, were not Romans hinds." ('Hinds' are female deer, and also servants.) This is the Brutus argument again — "The fault... is not in our stars, / But in ourselves" — in animal fur. He goes further, into contempt: Romans are "weak straws" for Caesar's fire, ‘trash’, "rubbish", ‘offal’ (butcher's waste), the "base matter" that makes "So vile a thing as Caesar" shine. Fire imagery links Caesar to the tempest "dropping fire": the man is the storm.

### Move four: the risk test — "Before a willing bondman"

Here is the most cunning moment in the scene. Cassius pretends to catch himself: "But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me? I perhaps speak this / Before a willing bondman; then, I know / My answer must be made. But I am armed, / And dangers are to me indifferent." ('My answer must be made' means 'I shall have to answer for it'.) The feigned slip does three things at once. It flatters Casca by suggesting Cassius was carried away by grief in his presence. It challenges him: are you a willing slave who will report me? And it displays Cassius's own readiness to die — "I am armed" — as the price of speaking freely. Casca has no honourable option but to prove he is not that man, and does: "You speak to Casca, and to such a man / That is no fleering telltale." ('Fleering' is sneering, grinning falsely.) The test was rigged, and Casca passes it gratefully.

### Move five: the handshake bargain

"Hold. My hand. / Be factious for redress of all these griefs, / And I will set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest." ('Factious' means active in forming a faction; 'redress' is remedy.) Cassius: "There's a bargain made." Weigh that word. Not 'oath', not 'faith' — a *bargain*, the language of the market. Only now, with the hand in his, does Cassius reveal what he withheld throughout: that a conspiracy already exists, that he has "moved already / Some certain of the noblest-minded Romans" to "an enterprise / Of honourable-dangerous consequence", and that they wait at "Pompey's Porch". Sequence is everything: information is released after commitment, never before. And in a final flourish he takes the very storm Casca feared and makes it the badge of the deed: "the complexion of the element" is "like the work we have in hand, / Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible." The omen he mocked ten minutes earlier is now his banner.

### HOW CASSIUS WORKS ON BRUTUS vs ON CASCA

| Dimension | On Brutus (Act 1 Scene 2) | On Casca (Act 1 Scene 3) |
|---|---|---|
| Setting he exploits | Daylight festival; offstage cheering for Caesar | Midnight storm; terrifying prodigies |
| Opening move | A hurt friend's reproach | A paradox: a "pleasing night to honest men" |
| His self-presentation | Modest "glass"; "my weak words" | Fearless hero who "bared my bosom to the thunder-stone" |
| Main emotional lever | Honour, reputation, ancestry, pride in a name | Fear, shame, manliness ("womanish") |
| Tone | Patient, gentle, indirect | Blunt, insulting ("You are dull, Casca") |
| How Caesar is attacked | Openly, through stories of his weak body | By riddle; Casca must name him |
| Handling of doubt | Reassurance (the disclaimer) | Challenge (the "willing bondman" test) |
| What he withholds | The existing conspiracy and the letters | The conspiracy — until the handshake |
| Result | "What you have said / I will consider"; a meeting fixed | "Hold. My hand." — immediate commitment |
| Why it fits the man | A philosopher must feel the decision is his own | A soldier answers to dare and shame |

The lesson for your analysis is this: Cassius has no single 'method'. He reads each man and fits the instrument to the material — which is precisely what Caesar means when he says Cassius "looks / Quite through the deeds of men". The one constant is that Cassius never lets the target hear the whole truth until the target has committed.

::: insight Why Cassius needs Brutus: Casca's "alchemy" speech
When Cassius says the conspirators will "See Brutus at his house" before dawn, because "Three parts of him / Is ours already", Casca explains the arithmetic: "O, he sits high in all the people's hearts, / And that which would appear offence in us / His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness." Alchemy was the dream of turning base metal into gold. Casca's image is exact and damning: without Brutus the deed is base metal — a murder by resentful men; with Brutus's face on it, the same deed becomes gold. Cassius accepts the analysis without a blush: "Him and his worth and our great need of him / You have right well conceited" ('conceited' means understood). This is political pragmatism in its purest form. Cassius knows what he looks like — Caesar has already called him "lean and hungry" — and knows a conspiracy of Cassiuses would be seen as what it partly is. Note how Cassius thinks of Brutus in fractions and possessives: "Three parts", "ours", "be sure of him". Brutus is an asset to be secured. Note, too, the pun that runs through Act 1: Brutus's "honourable mettle", "wrought" by Cassius, is now to be the alchemist's gold. And note the tragic irony the play will unfold: the gold comes with Brutus's judgement attached — his refusal to kill Antony, his permission for Antony to speak — and it is Brutus's virtue, not Cassius's cunning, that finally destroys the conspiracy. They needed his face; they got his conscience too.
:::

## CASSIUS'S STATED MOTIVES vs CASSIUS'S POSSIBLE PERSONAL MOTIVES

Here is the question that separates a competent answer from an excellent one: *why* does Cassius do all this? The weak answer has a one-word reply — jealousy — and treats the whole seduction of Brutus as a cover story. The strong answer notices that Shakespeare has made the question genuinely hard, and shows the examiner that it is hard. You need two columns in your head: what Cassius *says* he wants, when other people are listening, and what the text *lets slip* about what he wants when they are not. Then you need the honesty to admit that the two columns may both be true at once.

### What Cassius says he wants: the stated case

Everything Cassius says in public is organised around one word, liberty, and its Roman meaning: that no Roman noble should live subject to another man's will. "I was born free as Caesar" is its first statement; "groaning underneath this age's yoke" its image of the present; "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" its last, desperate form. Beside liberty stands equality among the nobility — the whole point of the weighed names, and of the lament "Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" when Rome contains "but one only man". Beside equality stands the founding legend: "There was a Brutus once" who would not tolerate a king. And over all of it Cassius lays the word he borrowed from Brutus: "honour is the subject of my story", an enterprise "Of honourable-dangerous consequence".

Do not make the mistake of treating this case as a mere disguise. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** gives the same creed, in Act 1 Scene 1, to Flavius, who has no personal quarrel with Caesar at all and fears a man who would "soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness". **HISTORICAL RECORD:** the hatred of the title of king, and the conviction that a perpetual dictatorship was a violation of the Republic, were real and widespread among Romans of Cassius's class. The play's later acts, which are outside this chapter, go on to show Caesar behaving in ways that partly confirm the danger. So the first reading must be allowed its full weight: Cassius may be a sincere republican whose analysis is correct.

### What the text lets slip: the personal evidence

Now read the same scenes for what Cassius reveals without intending to.

**The soliloquy names only a personal grievance.** Alone, with every reason to remind himself of Rome's liberty, Cassius says "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus." That is the sum of his private case against Caesar in the scene's one moment of truth: Caesar dislikes *me*.

**The stories are about Cassius.** The Tiber anecdote exists to show Caesar sinking, but its emotional centre is the speaker: "Cassius is / A wretched creature and must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him." Weigh "carelessly". What burns is not tyranny but neglect — that Caesar barely notices the man he once begged for help. Both anecdotes make Cassius the stronger man and Caesar the weakling, which is a strange shape for a political argument and a natural shape for a wounded ego.

**The sting is in being second to an equal.** "I had as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself." Not 'in awe of a tyrant' but of 'such a thing as I myself' — a man of the same kind. And the climax of the fever speech is "bear the palm alone": the offence is that the prize is not shared, which is a grievance only a competitor feels.

**Caesar's diagnosis.** Within minutes, Caesar will describe men like Cassius as "never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves" — placed, as the portrait section below shows, while the Tiber and the fever are still fresh in our ears.

**Contempt for the people.** Cassius's liberty is not everyone's liberty. He calls the crowd "the rout", and tells Casca that Romans are "sheep", "hinds", "weak straws", "rubbish". A man who despises the people he claims to be freeing may still be sincere about freedom, but his freedom is the freedom of his own class not to bow.

### The range of readings

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION** — scholars and directors genuinely divide here, and your answer may choose any reading provided it argues from the text.

| Reading of Cassius | Strongest evidence | What it explains | What it struggles with |
|---|---|---|---|
| The principled republican: an ideological motive | "I was born free as Caesar"; the ancestor; the willingness to die rather than bow | Why Brutus, Casca and Cinna respect him; why his arguments contain truth; why he can be so brave in the storm | Why, alone, he names only "Caesar doth bear me hard"; why his stories are about himself |
| The envious rival: a personal motive | "bear me hard"; "bear the palm alone"; Caesar's portrait of a man never at ease before "a greater" | The heat of the anecdotes; the readiness to forge letters; the hunger imagery | His genuine courage, and the fact that the play confirms Caesar's ambition |
| The wounded pride of a former equal | "such a thing as I myself"; "now become a god"; "carelessly but nod on him" | The obsession with Caesar's body — a former comrade remembers him as flesh | It makes Cassius a case study in humiliation rather than a political actor |
| Principle energised by envy (a widely held reading) | All of the above, read together | Why the arguments are true *and* the arguer untrustworthy; why Shakespeare gives us the soliloquy | Students must resist collapsing it back into 'he is just jealous' |

Two further complications deserve their own paragraph.

**The Epicurean who uses omens.** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Cassius followed the Epicurean school, which held that gods do not meddle in human affairs and that portents mean nothing. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT:** in Scene 2 he speaks like a perfect Epicurean — "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves". In Scene 3, a month later, he tells Casca that heaven has made the prodigies "instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state", and that the storm's "complexion" is "like the work we have in hand". **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** there are two ways to read the reversal. Either Cassius is a cynic who will use any instrument, including a theology he does not believe, to move a frightened man — which fits Cicero's warning that "men may construe things after their fashion" — or Cassius is himself unsettled, and the sceptic is being drawn, against his philosophy, into the storm's meaning. The play does not settle it in Act 1; it returns to the question at Philippi, where Cassius admits that his opinion of omens has changed. For your purposes, the safe formulation is: 'in Scene 3 Cassius either exploits the omens he does not believe in, or begins to believe them; both readings expose a gap between his philosophy and his practice.'

**The thing he never mentions.** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Cassius fought on Pompey's side in the civil war, commanding part of the Pompeian fleet, and after Pharsalus Caesar pardoned him and later made him praetor for 44 BC — while giving Brutus the more prestigious urban praetorship, which ancient writers treat as a grievance of Cassius's. **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** never lets Cassius speak of the pardon. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** that silence may be Shakespeare streamlining his source, or it may be characterisation — the omission a resentful man would make. Either way, it is why 'selective truth' is the right technical phrase for his method, and why you should never write 'Cassius is evil'. Shakespeare gives a questionable motive to a man whose political analysis the play half-endorses, and leaves you to hold both.

::: history Plutarch's lean men and the historical Cassius
Plutarch, in the Life of Caesar that Shakespeare read in Sir Thomas North's 1579 translation, records Caesar saying that he did not fear fat, long-haired men but the pale, thin ones — meaning Brutus and Cassius. Shakespeare keeps the thought and gives it entirely to Cassius, so that the 'lean' man becomes one character and Brutus is spared the suspicion. The historical Gaius Cassius Longinus was a capable soldier: quaestor (a junior magistrate in charge of finances) under Crassus, he survived the disaster at Carrhae in 53 BC and defended Syria. He was tribune in 49 BC, commanded a Pompeian fleet, was pardoned by Caesar and became praetor in 44 BC. He was an Epicurean, and he was Brutus's brother-in-law, married to Brutus's sister Junia Tertia — a kinship the play makes nothing of in Act 1, where the two men are presented simply as friends. He died by suicide at Philippi in October 42 BC. Hold these facts loosely in an answer: they explain why Shakespeare *could* make Cassius both a genuine republican and a man with private scores to settle, but the play, not Plutarch, is your evidence.
:::

## Caesar's portrait of Cassius: an ironically accurate character sketch

Shakespeare does something unusual in the middle of Act 1 Scene 2. Having let us watch Cassius work on Brutus at length, he brings Caesar back on stage and has him describe Cassius — a man he has not just been listening to — with a precision that makes the audience catch its breath. Read the whole sketch first.

> **Caesar:** Let me have men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights. / Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous.

Antony brushes it aside — "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given" ('well given' is well disposed) — and Caesar presses on:

> **Caesar:** He reads much, / He is a great observer, and he looks / Quite through the deeds of men. He loves no plays, / As thou dost, Antony; he hears no music; / Seldom he smiles, and smiles in such a sort / As if he mocked himself and scorned his spirit / That could be moved to smile at anything. / Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves, / And therefore are they very dangerous.

### Testing the portrait against what we have seen

Take the sketch clause by clause and set each against the evidence Act 1 has already given you. This is exactly the kind of cross-checking an examiner rewards, because it shows you reading the play as a whole rather than scene by scene.

| Caesar's phrase | What it claims | Where Act 1 has already shown it |
|---|---|---|
| "a lean and hungry look" | A man starved of something he wants | Cassius's own imagery is of feeding and appetite: "We both have fed as well"; "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed" |
| "He thinks too much" | A plotter, a mind that will not rest | The "worthy cogitations" he claims to have buried; the detailed plan of the letters, "In several hands" |
| "He reads much" | A bookish, learned man | His speech is full of learning: Aeneas and Anchises, the Colossus, the Epicurean maxim about our stars |
| "He is a great observer" | He watches people | His very first move: "Brutus, I do observe you now of late" |
| "looks / Quite through the deeds of men" | He sees motives beneath behaviour | He reads Brutus's "passions of some difference" before Brutus explains them, and later fits a wholly different method to Casca |
| "He loves no plays... he hears no music" | He takes no pleasure in shared festivity | He is the man who stays behind from the games while the city shouts for Caesar |
| "Seldom he smiles, and smiles in such a sort / As if he mocked himself" | Bitter, self-mocking humour | The pun on Rome and "room enough"; "we have the falling sickness"; "A very pleasing night to honest men" |
| "never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves" | He cannot bear a superior | "Cassius is / A wretched creature and must bend his body"; "bear the palm alone" |

Every clause lands. The portrait is not Caesar's prejudice; it is a summary of the scene we have just watched, placed in the mouth of the one man who did not watch it.

### Why the accuracy is ironic

**Irony one: the seer who will not act.** Caesar sees Cassius exactly, and does nothing. Listen to the frame around the insight: "Would he were fatter! But I fear him not. / Yet if my name were liable to fear, / I do not know the man I should avoid / So soon as that spare Cassius." And the close: "I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar." The judgement is correct and the conclusion is suicidal, because Caesar has made his own name a thing that cannot admit fear. Notice how close this is to the weakness Cassius has just exploited in Brutus. Brutus loves "The name of honour"; Caesar is ruled by the name of Caesar — "if my *name* were liable to fear" — and speaks of himself in the third person as if the name were a separate, invulnerable being. The two men Cassius most needs to understand are both prisoners of their own names, and he understands it. Caesar, who understands Cassius, does not understand himself.

**Irony two: the deaf ear.** The speech ends: "Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him." **HISTORICAL RECORD:** no ancient source gives Caesar a deaf ear; it appears to be Shakespeare's invention. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many readers take the invention as emblematic. At the very moment of his sharpest insight, the man who 'hears' the truth about Cassius turns a deaf ear towards it and asks the wrong adviser for a second opinion. The frail body Cassius has just mocked — the shaking, the sick girl — is confirmed on stage, in Caesar's own voice, not as a swoon but as a sense that does not work.

**Irony three: Antony is wrong and we know it.** "He is a noble Roman, and well given." The audience has heard Cassius plan to be a "glass" that distorts, and will shortly hear him plan the letters. Antony's reassurance is dramatic irony in its pure form: we know what the speaker does not. And it is also a first glimpse of Antony's own limitation — the man of plays and music misjudges the man of books. The play will later give Antony his revenge in a scene of persuasion that outdoes Cassius; here he is simply the worse observer.

**Irony four: a word changes hands.** Cassius, protesting his sincerity to Brutus, said "then hold me dangerous". Caesar, within the hour, says of him "Such men are dangerous" and "therefore are they very dangerous". The word Cassius used to deny a charge becomes the charge. Shakespeare lets the audience hear the echo and the two speakers never do.

**Irony five: the portrait indicts the painter.** Caesar's ideal companions are "men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights" — the contented, the untroubled, the unreflective. A ruler who wants only such men around him is a ruler who wants no one to think. Cassius's charge was that Rome had become "room enough" for "one only man"; Caesar's wish for sleek, sleeping courtiers quietly confirms it. The sketch of Cassius is accurate, and the sketch of the man who wants no Cassiuses near him is accurate too.

::: insight What the portrait does to our view of Cassius
The portrait is a trap for the careless reader as much as for Caesar. Because every clause is confirmed by what we have seen, it is tempting to adopt it as Shakespeare's verdict: Cassius is the envious intellectual, case closed. Resist that. Caesar is describing the type of man who is dangerous *to Caesar* — and the qualities he lists (reading, observing, thinking, seeing through deeds, refusing easy pleasures) are, in any other context, the qualities of a serious citizen. A republic needs men who look "Quite through the deeds of men"; a monarch does not. So the portrait works in two directions at once. It confirms the personal reading of Cassius's motives — the man "never at heart's ease" beside a superior — and at the same time it exposes the political problem Cassius has been describing, by showing us a Caesar who classifies thought itself as a threat. Shakespeare gives us the sketch at the exact midpoint between the seduction of Brutus and Casca's report of the crown, so that we must hold three things together: Cassius is envious; Cassius is right that Caesar wants no equals; and Caesar is right that Cassius is dangerous. A tragedy, not a morality play, is built from truths that do not cancel.
:::

::: level LEVEL 4 — How does it connect to the whole play?
Cassius's method in Act 1 is the template against which every later act of persuasion in the play is measured. In Act 2 the forged letters do their work and Brutus, alone in his orchard, completes Cassius's hints with his own reasoning — which is exactly what the 'seducer' intended: the decision is Brutus's, so the guilt is Brutus's. In Act 3 Antony takes Cassius's tools — the disclaimer of eloquence, the manipulation of the crowd's feelings, the 'mirror' that shows an audience a flattering image of itself — and uses them on a far larger audience with far greater effect; the analytical vocabulary you have built here (flattery, insinuation, selective truth, rhetorical question, appeal to honour) is precisely the vocabulary you will need for the funeral oration. In Act 4 the two kinds of honour that this chapter kept separating — Cassius's honour as reputation, Brutus's honour as conscience — collide in the quarrel at Sardis. And in Act 5 the sceptic who told Brutus the fault was not in our stars confesses that he has begun to credit omens, and dies by the Roman freedom he preached to Casca: "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius". Caesar's portrait, meanwhile, becomes the play's first lesson in the gap between insight and action — a gap that will kill Caesar and, later, the conspirators who inherit his overconfidence. Act 1 plants every one of these seeds, and the Class 10 paper on Acts 3 to 5 is, in effect, an examination on how they grew.
:::

## Writing about Cassius for ICSE

::: icse The vocabulary of manipulation — precise terms and how to use them
**EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE.** Act 1 is examined in your Class IX internal papers, usually through extract questions; the Class 10 board paper sets Acts 3 to 5 but repeatedly asks what characters are doing *and why*, which sends you back to Act 1 for Cassius's motives and methods. In either setting, the terms below let you say in one word what a weak answer takes a paragraph to fumble. Learn the definitions exactly; an imprecise term is worse than none.

| Term | Precise meaning | Sample sentence for an answer |
|---|---|---|
| manipulation | controlling someone's thoughts or actions by indirect, often unfair means, so that the person believes the choice is his own | 'Cassius's manipulation of Brutus lies in never asking him to decide; he works on Brutus's self-image and lets Brutus reach the conclusion himself.' |
| insinuation | suggesting something unpleasant indirectly, by hint rather than statement | 'The letters are pure insinuation: Caesar's ambition is to be, in Cassius's own word, "glancèd at", never stated.' |
| rhetoric | the art of using language to persuade; also the techniques themselves | 'Cassius's rhetoric moves from anecdote to maxim: the Tiber story is followed by "Men at some time are masters of their fates".' |
| flattery | insincere or excessive praise offered to gain influence | 'Cassius disguises flattery as service when he offers to be the ‘glass’ that reveals Brutus's "hidden worthiness".' |
| resentment | lasting bitterness at being treated unfairly or slighted | 'Resentment surfaces when Cassius remembers that he "must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him".' |
| envy | discontent at another's advantages, with a wish to have them oneself | 'Caesar diagnoses envy without naming it: such men are "never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves".' |
| ideological motive vs personal motive | acting from a principle or political belief, as against acting from private grievance or gain | 'Cassius's stated motive is ideological — Roman liberty — but his soliloquy reveals a personal motive: "Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus."' |
| selective truth | using accurate facts, arranged and edited so that only one conclusion is visible | 'Cassius practises selective truth: the fever in Spain is true, but Caesar's clemency and Cassius's own pardon are never mentioned.' |
| political pragmatism | judging actions by their practical results rather than by principle | 'Cassius's political pragmatism is clearest in Scene 3, when he accepts that the conspiracy needs Brutus because his "countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue" what would otherwise look like crime.' |

Three habits that earn marks with these words. First, define as you use: 'Cassius uses insinuation — hinting rather than stating — when…'. Second, attach each term to a quotation of no more than a line or two; the term is the claim, the quotation is the evidence. Third, never let a term do your thinking for you: 'Cassius is a manipulator' is a label, but 'Cassius manipulates Brutus by echoing his own word, honour, so that his cause and Brutus's ruling value appear identical' is analysis.
:::

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
Whatever the question, structure your material on Cassius in four moves. (1) **Situation:** fix the moment precisely in words, never in line numbers — 'after the procession leaves for the games, while two offstage shouts are heard'. (2) **Method:** name two or three techniques with the precise vocabulary above and show each working through a short, exact quotation. (3) **Effect:** say how Brutus (or Casca) receives it — "What you would work me to, I have some aim" is your proof that the persuasion is both visible and effective. (4) **Judgement:** end with the soliloquy or Caesar's portrait, which lets you weigh stated against personal motive without pretending the text settles it. For an extract question, the sub-parts will usually ask what has just happened, what a phrase means in your own words, why the speaker says it, what it reveals of character, and what follows — the four moves above map onto those sub-parts almost exactly. For the Class 10 paper, the same four moves answer the recurring 'why' questions about the conspirators in Acts 3 to 5: when you are asked why Brutus joined, or what kind of man quarrels with him at Sardis, you are being asked for this chapter.
:::

### A worked comparison: two answers to the same question

The question is a classic Class IX one and a frequent silent presence behind Class 10 questions: *How does Cassius persuade Brutus in Act 1 Scene 2?*

::: weak A weak answer
Cassius persuades Brutus by flattering him and telling him that Caesar is weak. He says that Brutus cannot see his own face and that he will be his mirror. Then he tells Brutus two stories. In the first story Caesar challenged Cassius to swim in the Tiber but Caesar could not swim and Cassius had to save him. In the second story Caesar had a fever in Spain and shook and asked for water like a girl. Cassius says that Caesar is like a Colossus and that Brutus's name is as good as Caesar's. He also reminds Brutus about his ancestor who drove out the kings. He tells Brutus that he will write letters to him pretending to be from the citizens. Brutus says he will think about it and they agree to meet. Cassius is a very jealous and evil man who hates Caesar because Caesar does not like him, and he manipulates Brutus because Brutus is honourable and everyone loves him.
:::

Why this earns little. It is a retelling, not an analysis: the stages are listed in order but no technique is named, so the word 'how' in the question is never answered. There is not one exact quotation, so nothing can be credited as evidence. It contains an error — Cassius does *not* tell Brutus about the letters; that is the soliloquy, spoken when Brutus has gone, and the whole point is that Brutus never knows. It also garbles the Tiber story: Caesar proposed the swim and swam it; he tired and cried for help — the text never says he 'could not swim'. It states a contested interpretation ('evil') as fact and offers the single word 'jealous' where the text offers a tangle of motives. And it never once mentions Brutus's response, so the reader cannot tell whether the persuasion worked or why.

::: strong An excellent answer
Cassius persuades Brutus not by argument but by working on Brutus's picture of himself — his honour, his name and his ancestry — while the offstage shouts for Caesar supply the pressure that Cassius himself dare not apply directly.

He begins with a reproach, complaining that he has not had from Brutus's eyes "that gentleness / And show of love as I was wont to have". This puts Brutus on the defensive and makes him confess that he is "with himself at war", which tells Cassius that the ground is already disturbed. Cassius then offers flattery disguised as service: since "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection", he will be Brutus's ‘glass’ and reveal his "hidden worthiness". The praise is attributed to others — "many of the best respect in Rome" — so that Brutus cannot refuse it as flattery. Before going further Cassius protects his credit with a disclaimer, listing the behaviour of a flatterer only to deny it: "then hold me dangerous".

The first shout lets Cassius trap Brutus into a position. When Brutus says "I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king", Cassius seizes the word: "Ay, do you fear it? / Then must I think you would not have it so." Brutus's reply — "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well" — fixes the conflict that Cassius must work around. He does so by echoing Brutus's own values: the moment Brutus declares that he loves "The name of honour more than I fear death", Cassius announces that "honour is the subject of my story".

The body of the persuasion attacks Caesar's body rather than his policies. Two anecdotes — the Tiber, where Caesar cried "Help me, Cassius, or I sink!", and the fever in Spain, where "this god did shake" and begged for drink "As a sick girl" — turn the god of the procession into a frail man. Cassius then shames Brutus with the hyperbole of the Colossus and the maxim "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings", converting servitude from fate into guilt. Rhetorical questions empty Caesar's name of magic — "Why should that name be sounded more than yours?" — and the appeal to ancestry, "There was a Brutus once", makes refusal a betrayal of Brutus's blood.

Finally Cassius retreats, calling his speech "my weak words" so that Brutus credits the fire to himself. The method works: Brutus admits "What you would work me to, I have some aim", yet declares he "had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome" under such conditions, and himself proposes the next meeting.

The soliloquy exposes the method as seduction rather than persuasion. Cassius calls Brutus's "honourable mettle" something that "may be wrought", asks "who so firm that cannot be seduced?", names a personal grievance — "Caesar doth bear me hard" — and plans forged letters "In several hands". Shakespeare thus lets us admire the skill while withholding our trust, and makes Brutus's tragedy visible from the moment it begins.
:::

Why this earns highly. It answers 'how' with named techniques — reproach, flattery as service, the disclaimer, seizing the shout, echoing the target's values, anecdote, hyperbole and maxim, rhetorical question, appeal to ancestry, false modesty — and shows each working through a short, exact quotation. It tracks Brutus's responses, so the effect of the persuasion is demonstrated rather than asserted. It places the soliloquy correctly, as the moment the audience learns what Brutus never learns, and it uses the soliloquy to weigh motive without flattening it: a personal grievance is *named*, not declared the whole truth. It has a thesis in its first sentence and a judgement in its last, and it never retells the plot for its own sake. It is also long enough for a full-mark long answer; for a three- or four-mark extract sub-part, you would lift the single relevant paragraph and let it stand alone.

**The thirteen stages (Act 1 Scene 2):** reproach about lost "gentleness" → the ‘glass’ → the disclaimer ("hold me dangerous") → the shout seized ("Ay, do you fear it?") → "honour is the subject of my story" → "I was born free as Caesar" → the Tiber ("Help me, Cassius, or I sink!") → the fever ("this god did shake") → the Colossus and "not in our stars" → the weighed names → "There was a Brutus once" → "my weak words" → the soliloquy ("who so firm that cannot be seduced?").

**The twelve tools:** flattery as service; comparison; anecdote; rhetorical question; appeal to honour; appeal to liberty; appeal to reputation and ancestry; the body as argument; the constructed 'we'; forged letters; selective truth; emotional sequencing.

**Casca (Act 1 Scene 3):** reinterpret the omens → the "womanish" taunt → suicide as freedom → the "willing bondman" test → "There's a bargain made". Fear and shame for a soldier; honour and name for a philosopher.

**Motives:** stated — liberty, equality, the ancestor, honour; let slip — "Caesar doth bear me hard", "bend his body", "bear the palm alone". Write 'principle energised by envy', never 'evil'.

**Caesar's portrait:** every clause accurate; every irony against Caesar — "I fear him not", "this ear is deaf", Antony's "well given".

**Three insights to carry:** the conspiracy needs Brutus as "richest alchemy"; the soliloquy is honest about dishonesty and that is why it is brilliant; what Cassius does is seduction — it treats Brutus's firmness as a lock, not a virtue.
