# Sources, Method and Quality Audit

*What this chapter does: it shows you exactly how this book was built and checked, so that you can trust what it says and know where its limits lie. A study book that asks you to quote accurately should itself show its working.*

## The source hierarchy this book followed

The book was written under a strict order of authority. Where two sources disagreed, the higher one won, and the disagreement is explained rather than hidden.

| Priority | Source | How it was used |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shakespeare's text of *Julius Caesar* | Every quotation in this book was checked, by an automated comparison and by human-style re-reading, against two independent public-domain texts: the Folger Shakespeare Library edition and the Globe-edition text (Moby/MIT and Project Gutenberg). |
| 2 | The student's prescribed ICSE edition | Not supplied. Editions used in ICSE schools (many based on A. W. Verity's Cambridge text) print the same words with minor spelling differences; this book therefore prints no line numbers and notes name variants (Marullus/Murellus; Calpurnia/Calphurnia). |
| 3 | Official CISCE material | The ICSE 2025 Literature in English board paper (structure, instructions, mark allocation) and the CISCE prescribed-books information for the 2027 examination year (Class IX: Acts I and II; Class X: Acts III, IV and V). Assessment skills are described in the Council's own terms (recall, understanding, application, analysis, evaluation) without inventing wording. |
| 4 | Academic Shakespeare resources | The Folger Shakespeare Library's edition and introduction; the Internet Shakespeare Editions' text of North's Plutarch; scholarly consensus on date (1599), publication (First Folio, 1623) and source (North's Plutarch, 1579). |
| 5 | Roman-history sources | Plutarch's *Life of Caesar* (in translation), Suetonius as reported in reference works, and modern reference articles on the Roman Republic, the Senate, tribunes, the triumph, the Lupercalia, the Ides, and the lives of Caesar, Pompey, Brutus, Cassius, Antony, Casca, Cinna, Decimus Brutus, Calpurnia and the tribunes Marullus and Flavus. |
| 6 | Study guides (LitCharts, SparkNotes, Shakespeare Navigators) | Consulted only to cross-check interpretations and word glosses. Nothing was copied or paraphrased line by line; the analysis, structure and examples in this book are independent. |

## The four labels

Throughout the book four labels keep different kinds of statement apart:

- **SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT** — what the play actually says.
- **HISTORICAL RECORD** — what ancient sources and modern historians say happened.
- **LITERARY INTERPRETATION** — a reading of the text that reasonable scholars might dispute; the book says so when they do.
- **EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE** — how to turn understanding into marks; never a prediction of a specific question.

## Where the sources disagree, and what the book did

::: textnote Text and history
1. **The tribune's name.** The 1623 Folio prints 'Murellus' in Act 1 Scene 1 and 'Marullus' later; Plutarch has Marullus. Most editions (Folger, Globe) print Marullus; some (Oxford, Arden) keep Murellus. This book uses Marullus and notes the variant.
2. **Caesar's wife.** The Folio and Folger print 'Calphurnia'; the Globe text and most ICSE editions print 'Calpurnia'. This book uses Calpurnia.
3. **How many times the crown was offered.** Plutarch describes Antony offering the diadem twice; Shakespeare's Casca says three times. The book treats the three offers as SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT and Plutarch's two as HISTORICAL RECORD.
4. **Caesar's deafness.** No ancient source records it; scholars treat "this ear is deaf" as Shakespeare's addition. The book never presents it as history.
5. **The site of the assassination.** Historically the Curia of Pompey; Shakespeare's characters speak of the Capitol. The book keeps both clear.
6. **Time.** The triumph (October 45 BC), the tribunes' removal of diadems (January 44 BC) and the Lupercalia (15 February 44 BC) are separate historical events that Shakespeare compresses into a single day; a month then passes silently before Scene 3 (the night before 15 March).
7. **Cassius's motive.** Whether Cassius acts from republican principle, personal envy, or both is a genuine interpretive question; the book presents the range of readings rather than a verdict.
8. **The tragic hero.** Critics disagree whether Brutus or Caesar is the play's tragic centre; the book presents this as an open question.
:::

## The honest syllabus position

For the ICSE (Class X) board examination in Literature in English, the prescribed portion of *Julius Caesar* is Acts III, IV and V. Acts I and II belong to Class IX. This book teaches Act 1 because the Class X paper depends on it constantly — motives, foreshadowing, character histories and 'what happened earlier' sub-questions all reach back to it — and because a student who struggled with Act 1 in Class IX cannot read Acts III–V with confidence until that foundation is secure. No question in this book is described as guaranteed, and nothing here predicts the board paper.

## A note on limits

- The book quotes short passages only, for analysis and teaching; it does not reproduce the play. Read the scenes themselves in your prescribed edition alongside each chapter.
- Where this book and your prescribed edition differ in a spelling or a comma, follow your edition in the examination room.
- Where an interpretation is labelled as such, you are free to disagree with it in an answer — provided you argue from the text.
