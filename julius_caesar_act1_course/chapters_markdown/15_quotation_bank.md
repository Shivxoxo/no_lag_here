# The Act 1 Quotation Bank

*What this chapter does: It gathers the thirty-two lines of Act 1 that carry the most analytical weight — from the Cobbler's puns to Casca's alchemy — and treats each one as a small lesson rather than a label to memorise. For every quotation you get the verified wording, who says it to whom and at what moment, a plain-English meaning, a deeper reading, the subtext underneath, what it reveals of character, theme and device, its roots in the Roman record, its connections across the play, the examination vocabulary it unlocks and a model sentence that embeds it. A quick-index table and a box on how to embed a quotation close the chapter, so that what you have learned can be carried straight into an answer.*

## How to Use This Bank

A quotation bank is not a list to be learned by heart and emptied onto the page. Examiners can tell at once when a student has memorised twenty lines and is hunting for somewhere to put them; the quotations arrive like guests at the wrong party. The purpose of this chapter is the opposite. Each entry is built so that you understand the line so thoroughly that you could never misuse it — you know what has just happened when it is spoken, what the speaker is really doing with it, and which argument it can carry.

Read the entries in order the first time, because they follow the act from the street to the storm, and the connections between them (Scene 1's "Pompey's blood" answered by Scene 2's tribunes "put to silence"; Scene 2's "I, your glass" answered by Scene 3's forged letters) only show themselves in sequence. On a second reading, use the Quick-Index at the end to pull out the lines you need for a particular theme or character.

Two honest notes before we begin. First, on the examination: Act 1 is the Class IX portion of the ICSE syllabus, and the Class X board paper examines Acts III–V. So the sentences offered under **Possible answer use:** are of two kinds — the sort you would write in a Class IX extract-based answer on Act 1 itself, and the sort you would write in a Class X answer on a later act, where an Act 1 line supplies the motive, the irony or the earlier context that lifts a reply from adequate to excellent. No question is guaranteed; what is guaranteed is that a student who understands these lines understands why the later acts happen. Second, on wording: every quotation below has been checked against the verified text. Where you see a label marked LITERARY INTERPRETATION, it is a reading I am defending, not a fact; where scholars disagree I say so.

## Scene 1 — The Street

### 1. "Hence! Home, you idle creatures" — Flavius clears the street

**Exact quotation:**

> **Flavius:** Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home! / Is this a holiday? What, know you not, / Being mechanical, you ought not walk / Upon a labouring day without the sign / Of your profession?

**Speaker:** Flavius, a tribune of the people.

**Listener:** A crowd of commoners (the Carpenter and the Cobbler will answer for them).

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 1, the very first words of the play.

**Context:** Nothing has happened yet. The stage fills with working men in their best clothes, and two tribunes — officials whose whole purpose is to protect the common people — begin the play by ordering those very people off the street.

**Simple meaning:** 'Get out of here and go home, you lazy people. This is not a holiday. Do you not know that as tradesmen you are not allowed to walk about on a working day without wearing the tools or dress of your trade?'

**Deep meaning:** The play opens with a question of law and class. 'Mechanical' (a manual worker) is spat out as an insult; 'the sign of your profession' means the leather apron and rule that would mark these men as what they are. Flavius's objection is not merely that they are idle but that they are out of place — dressed above their station, celebrating a man he regards as a threat to the Republic. The first thing we see in Rome is an argument about who owns the public street.

**Subtext:** Underneath the bluster is fear. The tribunes will shortly reveal that they dread Caesar's rise; sending the crowd home is the only power they have left, and they exercise it on the people they are meant to serve. The scolding is displacement — anger at Caesar vented on cobblers.

**Character:** Flavius is decisive, contemptuous of the commons, and republican to the bone. He is also, in a sense the play will make bitter, brave: he acts against Caesar's popularity in the open and pays for it offstage.

**Theme:** Public opinion and the fickle crowd; class; the collapse of the republican bond between tribune and people.

**Device:** Imperative mood piled on imperative ("Hence!", "get you home!") creates urgency; the rhetorical question ("Is this a holiday?") shows contempt; opening a play in the middle of a quarrel (in medias res) throws the audience straight into unrest.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — tribunes of the plebs were created to shield the plebeians from the aristocracy and were sacrosanct. Shakespeare inverts the office: his tribunes bully the plebs. The dating is compressed, too: the triumph being celebrated (October 45 BC) and the Lupercalia (15 February 44 BC) were months apart, but the play makes them one day.

**Scene connection:** The crowd Flavius drives away is the same crowd that will cheer Caesar's refusals of the crown in Scene 2 (as Casca reports) and, in Act 3, be swung by Antony's oration. Flavius's own fate is reported by Casca: he and Marullus "are put to silence".

**ICSE keywords:** in medias res; exposition; fickle mob; class conflict; republicanism; dramatic foreshadowing.

**Possible answer use:** 'From its very first line — "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!" — the play presents the Roman people as a crowd to be commanded, a view Antony will exploit far more skilfully than the tribunes in Act 3.'

### 2. "a mender of bad soles" — the Cobbler's first pun

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cobbler:** A trade, sir, that I hope I may use with a safe conscience, which is indeed, sir, a mender of bad soles.

**Speaker:** The Cobbler (Folger's name; some editions call him the Second Commoner).

**Listener:** Marullus, who has demanded, "But what trade art thou? Answer me directly."

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 1, early, as the tribunes interrogate the crowd.

**Context:** Marullus has asked the Cobbler's trade and received a teasing non-answer ('in respect of a fine workman, I am but, as you would say, a cobbler' — where 'cobbler' also meant a bungler). Pressed to answer directly, the Cobbler produces this.

**Simple meaning:** 'My trade is one I can practise with a clear conscience: I mend worn-out shoe soles.' The joke is that 'soles' sounds like 'souls', so he also claims to be a mender of bad souls — a kind of priest.

**Deep meaning:** The pun is a small act of rebellion. The Cobbler cannot defy a tribune openly, so he plays with language until the tribune cannot pin him down. 'Safe conscience' is barbed: the man who mends souls is implying that the tribunes, who are about to scold their own people, might need mending. The exchange that follows deepens the joke — "Nay, I beseech you, sir, be not out with me. / Yet if you be out, sir, I can mend you." — where 'out' means both 'angry' and 'out at the toes'.

**Subtext:** The commoners are cleverer than their rulers think. The tribunes speak in verse and expect obedience; the Cobbler speaks in prose and runs rings round them. Shakespeare is quietly refusing the idea that the people are mere "blocks" and "stones".

**Character:** The Cobbler is witty, self-possessed, and politically shallow — he will say in a moment that they are out simply "to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph". Quick with words, unreflective about power: that combination is the Roman crowd in miniature.

**Theme:** Appearance versus reality (what a 'trade' is); the gap between the rulers' contempt and the people's intelligence; mob psychology.

**Device:** Pun (soles/souls; out/out); prose for the commoners against the tribunes' verse; comic relief that is also exposition.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — the scene is Shakespeare's invention in detail; Plutarch gives no cobbler. The humour is Elizabethan rather than Roman: London apprentices and artisans would have recognised the type. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — many critics think the mingling of a Roman street with a London one is deliberate, inviting the 1599 audience to see itself in the crowd.

**Scene connection:** The Cobbler's gift for turning words links him, oddly, to Cassius, who will "conjure with" the names Brutus and Caesar later in the act; both treat language as a tool rather than a mirror of truth. The scene's pattern — commoners won over by whoever speaks to them — is repeated with the Plebeians in Act 3.

**ICSE keywords:** pun; wordplay; comic relief; prose versus verse; social hierarchy.

**Possible answer use:** 'The Cobbler's claim to be "a mender of bad soles" is more than a joke: the pun on "souls" lets a commoner mock the tribunes' moral authority while appearing to answer them politely.'

### 3. "a surgeon to old shoes" — the Cobbler's boast

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cobbler:** Truly, sir, all that I live by is with the awl. I meddle with no tradesman's matters nor women's matters, but withal I am indeed, sir, a surgeon to old shoes: when they are in great danger, I recover them. As proper men as ever trod upon neat's leather have gone upon my handiwork.

**Speaker:** The Cobbler.

**Listener:** Flavius, who has just asked, "Thou art a cobbler, art thou?"

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 1, the climax of the comic exchange, just before the tribunes learn why the crowd is out.

**Context:** Having been accused of being a 'naughty knave' and asked his trade for the fourth time, the Cobbler finally confesses it — and then dresses it up grandly.

**Simple meaning:** 'All I earn, I earn with my awl (the pointed tool for piercing leather). I do not interfere in other men's trades or other men's wives; all the same I am a surgeon to worn-out shoes — when they are in danger of dying I bring them back to life. Some of the finest men who ever walked in cowhide have walked on shoes I mended.'

**Deep meaning:** Every phrase has two edges. 'With the awl' sounds like 'withal' (nevertheless), and he promptly uses 'withal' in the next clause, so the ear hears the same sound twice with two meanings. 'Recover' means both 'cure' and 're-cover' (put a new sole on). 'Neat's leather' is cowhide; 'proper men' are handsome, worthy men. The Cobbler inflates a humble trade into medicine, and in doing so gently satirises every grand man who inflates himself — in a play about one man who has "grown so great".

**Subtext:** The claim to 'meddle' in nobody's matters is the Cobbler's defence against the tribunes' real charge, which is political: that the people are meddling in Rome's affairs by celebrating Caesar. He is saying, in effect, 'I am only a shoemaker; politics is not my business' — which is precisely the attitude that lets a Caesar rise.

**Character:** Shrewd, proud of his craft, apolitical by choice. He represents the citizen who would rather be entertained than govern.

**Theme:** Public opinion; the people's abdication of political responsibility; appearance versus reality.

**Device:** Extended pun (awl/withal, recover/re-cover); mock-heroic inflation ('surgeon'); prose; irony (the man who 'meddles' with nothing is part of the crowd meddling with the Republic's future).

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — nothing in Plutarch; the whole exchange is Shakespeare's dramatic invention, built on London humour. The underlying historical reality it dramatises is sound: Caesar's popularity with the urban poor was real and was one reason the senatorial class feared him.

**Scene connection:** The Cobbler's 'surgeon' who 'recovers' things in 'great danger' foreshadows, with grim comedy, the conspirators who will call themselves healers of a sick Rome in Act 2 and Act 3; the question of who is a surgeon and who is a butcher is one Brutus himself raises later in the play.

**ICSE keywords:** pun; mock-heroic; irony; comic relief; characterisation of the commoners.

**Possible answer use:** 'When the Cobbler calls himself "a surgeon to old shoes", Shakespeare lets the lowest character in the play rehearse, comically, the claim the conspirators will make seriously — that they are healers of a diseased Rome.'

### 4. "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!" — Marullus's rebuke

**Exact quotation:**

> **Marullus:** Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? / What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels? / You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things! / O you hard hearts, you cruel men of Rome, / Knew you not Pompey?

**Speaker:** Marullus, the second tribune.

**Listener:** The commoners, chiefly the Cobbler, who has just said they are out "to rejoice in his triumph".

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 1, the turning point of the scene, where comedy gives way to passion.

**Context:** The Cobbler has explained, innocently, that the holiday is to see Caesar's triumph. The word 'triumph' detonates. Marullus, loyal to the dead Pompey, erupts into the first great speech of the play.

**Simple meaning:** 'Why are you rejoicing? What conquest has he brought home? Which conquered foreign rulers follow him to Rome, chained, to decorate his chariot? You are blocks of wood, lumps of stone, worse than things without feeling! You hard-hearted, cruel Romans — did you not know Pompey?'

**Deep meaning:** A Roman triumph was granted for victory over a foreign enemy, and its spectacle included captive kings in chains. Caesar's 'triumph' here celebrates Munda, a victory over Pompey's sons — over Romans. Marullus's three questions expose the hollowness: there are no "tributaries" (subject peoples paying tribute) because the defeated were fellow citizens. The insult 'worse than senseless things' says the crowd is below stone: stone at least cannot be expected to remember, but these men can, and will not.

**Subtext:** The anger is also grief. 'Knew you not Pompey?' is a bereaved man's question — how can you have forgotten him so quickly? There is fear too: a people with no memory can be led anywhere.

**Character:** Marullus is passionate, loyal, eloquent and politically doomed — he mistakes rhetoric for power, as the conspirators will later mistake a killing for a cure.

**Theme:** The fickleness of the crowd; loyalty and betrayal (to Pompey); the civil war beneath the festival; political rhetoric.

**Device:** Rhetorical questions in a rising series (tricolon); apostrophe and direct address ("O you hard hearts"); metaphor ("blocks", "stones"); alliteration on hard consonants that makes the contempt audible.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Caesar's Spanish triumph (October 45 BC) was indeed resented because it celebrated the defeat of Romans; Plutarch records that people thought it unfitting. Pompey had been enormously popular and had celebrated three triumphs of his own.

**Scene connection:** The crowd's amnesia here prepares us for the Plebeians of Act 3 who turn from Brutus to Antony within minutes; Marullus's method — scolding the people — is the one Antony will pointedly refuse, flattering them instead.

**ICSE keywords:** rhetorical question; apostrophe; tricolon; fickleness; triumph; loyalty.

**Possible answer use:** 'Marullus's "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!" establishes in the first scene the view of the crowd that the whole play tests: a people without memory, who will later be as easily swung by Antony as they were by Caesar's procession.'

### 5. "That Tiber trembled underneath her banks" — the memory of Pompey

**Exact quotation:**

> **Marullus:** Have you not made an universal shout, / That Tiber trembled underneath her banks / To hear the replication of your sounds / Made in her concave shores?

**Speaker:** Marullus.

**Listener:** The commoners.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 1, in the middle of the great rebuke, just after "Knew you not Pompey?"

**Context:** Marullus reminds the crowd how they used to wait all day — on "walls and battlements", "chimney tops", "Your infants in your arms" — to watch "great Pompey pass the streets of Rome". This is the climax of that memory.

**Simple meaning:** 'Did you not raise such a united shout that the river Tiber shook beneath its banks when it heard your cheering echoed back from its curved shores?'

**Deep meaning:** The image makes the crowd's voice a natural force, so loud it moves a river. 'Replication' means echo; 'concave shores' are the hollowed, curving riverbanks that throw the sound back. Marullus's point is double-edged: he praises the crowd's old devotion to Pompey, but in doing so he describes exactly the kind of mass emotion that is now being poured out for Caesar. The shout that once shook the Tiber for Pompey is the same shout we will hear, three times, offstage in Scene 2.

**Subtext:** Marullus thinks he is describing loyalty; he is actually describing the raw power of the mob, which has no fixed object. Whoever is in the chariot gets the shout.

**Character:** A man living in the past — a Pompeian in Caesar's Rome, whose eloquence is nostalgic rather than practical.

**Theme:** Public opinion and mob psychology; the transfer of loyalty; memory and forgetting.

**Device:** Hyperbole and personification (the river "trembled"); auditory imagery; the river as a recurring Roman symbol.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Pompey's triumphs (79, 71 and 61 BC) were among the most spectacular Rome had seen; his popularity was real. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — the personified Tiber recalls Marullus's own civic ideal: Rome as a living body that feels what its citizens do.

**Scene connection:** The Tiber returns almost at once, in Scene 2, as the "angry flood" into which Cassius and Caesar leap — a river that in Scene 1 echoed the people's love and in Scene 2 nearly drowns their idol. Flavius will send the commoners to "weep your tears / Into the channel" of the same river.

**ICSE keywords:** personification; hyperbole; auditory imagery; symbol; fickle crowd.

**Possible answer use:** 'The shout that once made "Tiber trembled underneath her banks" for Pompey is the shout that greets Caesar offstage in Scene 2: Shakespeare lets the same sound serve two masters, and so shows us that the crowd's loyalty belongs to no one.'

### 6. "That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood" — the charge of ingratitude

**Exact quotation:**

> **Marullus:** And do you now put on your best attire? / And do you now cull out a holiday? / And do you now strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood? / Be gone!

**Speaker:** Marullus.

**Listener:** The commoners.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 1, the end of the rebuke, immediately followed by the order to pray that the gods "intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude".

**Context:** Having recalled the crowd's love for Pompey, Marullus turns on their present behaviour.

**Simple meaning:** 'And now you dress in your finest, now you pick out a holiday, now you scatter flowers in the path of a man who returns in triumph having shed Pompey's blood? Go away!'

**Deep meaning:** 'Pompey's blood' means both Pompey's family (his sons, defeated at Munda) and the literal blood of the civil war. The anaphora — 'And do you now' three times — hammers the word 'now' against the 'then' of the remembered devotion. The flowers strewn for Caesar are, in Marullus's eyes, laid on Pompey's grave as an insult. His closing instruction to 'pray to the gods to intermit the plague' makes ingratitude a sin that heaven itself will punish — an idea the storm of Scene 3 will seem to confirm.

**Subtext:** Marullus cannot stop the triumph; he can only shame its spectators. The speech is magnificent and futile, and its futility is the point: words alone do not restore a Republic.

**Character:** Principled and rash. He pays for this speech with his office and, Casca implies, perhaps more.

**Theme:** Ingratitude; betrayal; the moral reading of politics (ingratitude brings "plague"); the fall of Pompey as the shadow over Caesar's rise.

**Device:** Anaphora ("And do you now…" repeated); rhetorical questions; metonymy ("Pompey's blood" for his family and cause); juxtaposition of festival imagery (flowers, "best attire") with blood.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Munda (17 March 45 BC) saw Caesar defeat Pompey's sons Gnaeus and Sextus; Gnaeus was killed. Pompey himself had been murdered in Egypt in 48 BC. A triumph over fellow Romans was regarded as improper, which is exactly Marullus's complaint.

**Scene connection:** 'Pompey's blood' is the first blood in a play that will fill with it; the conspirators meet at "Pompey's Porch" in Scene 3, and the assassination itself, in Act 3, takes place in the shadow of Pompey's statue. The ingratitude Marullus condemns in the crowd is the ingratitude Antony will later accuse the conspirators of.

**ICSE keywords:** anaphora; metonymy; ingratitude; juxtaposition; foreshadowing.

**Possible answer use:** 'Marullus's accusation that the people "strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood" plants the Pompeian grievance that Cassius will exploit and that will literally frame the murder in Act 3.'

### 7. "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing" — Flavius's plan

**Exact quotation:**

> **Flavius:** These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch, / Who else would soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness.

**Speaker:** Flavius.

**Listener:** Marullus (the crowd has left).

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 1, the closing lines of the scene.

**Context:** The commoners have slunk off — Flavius notes with satisfaction, "They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness." He now tells Marullus to go towards the Capitol and strip Caesar's statues: "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies." Marullus hesitates because "it is the feast of Lupercal"; Flavius overrules him — "Let no images / Be hung with Caesar's trophies" — and explains why.

**Simple meaning:** 'If we pluck these new feathers (his decorations and his supporters) from Caesar's wing, he will fly only at an ordinary height; otherwise he would soar out of sight of ordinary men and keep us all in frightened slavery.'

**Deep meaning:** The metaphor is from falconry: 'pitch' is the height to which a hawk climbs before it stoops on its prey. Caesar is a bird of prey still growing its flight feathers; the tribunes hope to clip it in time. The image concedes a great deal — Caesar is already airborne, already above the "view of men" — and offers a pitifully small remedy: tearing scarves off statues. 'Servile fearfulness' is the first naming of the play's central dread, the fear of Romans becoming slaves.

**Subtext:** Flavius knows the crowd is the real feather, and the crowd has only been shooed, not changed. His confidence is whistling in the dark.

**Character:** Flavius is the practical tribune, Marullus the emotional one. He makes a plan, however inadequate, and takes a risk for it.

**Theme:** Power and the fear of tyranny; liberty versus slavery ("servile"); the republican instinct to cut down whoever grows too tall.

**Device:** Extended metaphor from falconry; symbolic action (disrobing the images); couplet-like finality closing the scene.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — the tribunes Marullus and Flavus did remove a diadem from Caesar's statue and arrest men who hailed him as king; Caesar had them deposed. Shakespeare moves the incident on to the day of the triumph and the Lupercalia. The 'images' are statues hung with decorations in Caesar's honour.

**Scene connection:** The feathers are plucked and the pluckers are punished: Casca will report that the two tribunes "are put to silence". Cassius's whole project in Scenes 2 and 3 is Flavius's project carried to its logical end — not plucking feathers, but killing the bird. Caesar as something that soars above men becomes Cassius's "Colossus" that bestrides the world.

**ICSE keywords:** extended metaphor; falconry imagery; tyranny; liberty; foreshadowing; symbolic action.

**Possible answer use:** 'Flavius's hope that "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch" is the Republic's first, failed attempt to cut Caesar down to size; Cassius's conspiracy is the same impulse armed with daggers.'

## Scene 2 — Lupercalia, the Soothsayer and the Seduction of Brutus

### 8. "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed" — Antony's obedience

**Exact quotation:**

> **Antony:** I shall remember. / When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed.

**Speaker:** Antony, stripped for the Lupercal race.

**Listener:** Caesar, in front of the whole procession.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, the opening ceremony, before the Soothsayer speaks.

**Context:** Caesar has told Calpurnia to stand in Antony's path and instructed Antony, "Forget not in your speed, Antonius, / To touch Calpurnia, for our elders say / The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse." Antony replies with this.

**Simple meaning:** 'I will remember. Whatever Caesar orders is as good as done.'

**Deep meaning:** Antony's one line in the ceremony is a public creed of loyalty. It is also a statement about Caesar's power: his word is already an act. 'Performed' is a theatrical word, and the whole opening is a performance — Caesar directing his wife, his consul and the festival itself. The request that prompts it reveals Caesar's superstition and his private wound (he has no heir), so Antony's smooth obedience also covers an embarrassment.

**Subtext:** LITERARY INTERPRETATION — Antony is the courtier the tribunes feared: a man who makes Caesar's will seamless. Yet the line also quietly seeds the Antony of Act 3, whose devotion to Caesar is real and will turn into something far more dangerous than obedience.

**Character:** Loyal, graceful, apparently uncomplicated — which is why the conspirators underrate him. Caesar's "He loves no plays, / As thou dost, Antony" later confirms the pleasure-lover's image.

**Theme:** Loyalty; power as command; superstition (the fertility rite).

**Device:** Antithesis of word and deed ('says… performed'); dramatic irony (Antony's obedience will outlive Caesar); the ceremony as spectacle.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — at the Lupercalia (15 February) the Luperci ran through the city striking bystanders with goat-hide thongs to promote fertility; Antony, consul in 44 BC, ran as a Lupercus. Caesar and Calpurnia were childless.

**Scene connection:** The man who performs Caesar's word here will, in Scene 2's reported crown-offering, perform it again — Casca says "Why, Antony" offered the coronet. In Act 3 he will perform something Caesar never commanded.

**ICSE keywords:** loyalty; dramatic irony; characterisation; superstition; antithesis.

**Possible answer use:** 'Antony's "When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed" seems in Act 1 the motto of a mere follower; by Act 3 it has become a vow, and the conspirators' failure to take it seriously destroys them.'

### 9. "Beware the ides of March" — the Soothsayer and Caesar's dismissal

**Exact quotation:**

> **Soothsayer:** Beware the ides of March.
> **Caesar:** He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass.

**Speaker:** The Soothsayer; then Caesar.

**Listener:** Caesar, with Brutus, Cassius, Casca and the whole train watching.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, just after the Lupercal procession is set in motion.

**Context:** A voice calls "Caesar" from the crowd — "a tongue shriller than all the music". Caesar demands "Set him before me. Let me see his face." Brutus relays the warning ("A soothsayer bids you beware the ides of March"), Cassius brings the man forward, Caesar hears it a second time, and dismisses him.

**Simple meaning:** 'Be on your guard on the fifteenth of March.' And Caesar's reply: 'He is a fantasist. Leave him. Move on.'

**Deep meaning:** The warning is the play's central prophecy and its plainest. Caesar's response shows the two sides of him in four words apiece: he is curious enough to stop a festival and look the man in the face, and proud enough to wave the warning away. 'Dreamer' means one who deals in idle fancies; the irony is that the Soothsayer is the one character in Act 1 who sees clearly.

**Subtext:** Caesar is performing fearlessness before an audience. Having summoned the man publicly, he cannot now be seen to heed him. The dismissal is about image, not belief — which is why, in Act 2, Calpurnia's dream will nearly keep him at home.

**Character:** The Soothsayer: anonymous, unrewarded, right. Caesar: imperious, curious, trapped by his own public self.

**Theme:** Fate and free will; omens and superstition; appearance versus reality; pride.

**Device:** Prophecy; dramatic irony (the audience knows the date); contrast between the shrill single voice and the "music" of the procession; the monosyllabic command "Pass."

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch and Suetonius name the seer Spurinna, who warned Caesar of danger coming no later than the Ides. In the Roman calendar the Ides fell on the fifteenth of March, May, July and October. Caesar was killed on 15 March 44 BC.

**Scene connection:** The warning is repeated by the Soothsayer in Act 2 and Act 3, and Caesar will mock him on the morning of the murder. Here it also silently marks Brutus — he is the one who repeats the words aloud, an unknowing herald of his own deed.

**ICSE keywords:** prophecy; dramatic irony; foreshadowing; hubris; fate versus free will.

**Possible answer use:** 'In Act 1 Caesar hears "Beware the ides of March" and answers "He is a dreamer"; the Class X extracts on the assassination gain their full irony only when this early dismissal is remembered.'

### 10. "poor Brutus, with himself at war" — Brutus admits his trouble

**Exact quotation:**

> **Brutus:** Nor construe any further my neglect / Than that poor Brutus, with himself at war, / Forgets the shows of love to other men.

**Speaker:** Brutus.

**Listener:** Cassius, alone with him after the procession has gone.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, the first exchange of the long Brutus–Cassius dialogue.

**Context:** Brutus has declined to watch the race — "I am not gamesome. I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony." Cassius complains that Brutus has lately lost "that gentleness / And show of love" he used to show. Brutus explains that the trouble is internal: he is "Vexèd" with "passions of some difference".

**Simple meaning:** 'Do not read any more into my coldness than this: poor Brutus is fighting a battle inside himself, and has forgotten to show his friends the affection he feels.'

**Deep meaning:** Brutus names the condition of the whole play: civil war begins inside one man. 'Construe' (interpret) is a word Cicero will use of the storm, and it fits — Brutus is a text that others will read for their own ends. His speaking of himself in the third person ('poor Brutus') shows a man already standing outside himself, judging. The phrase 'shows of love' concedes that love has an outward performance which he has let slip; the inner feeling, he insists, is intact.

**Subtext:** Brutus is telling Cassius, without meaning to, exactly what Cassius needs to know: that he is divided, and about matters of 'some difference' — political matters, we infer. A man at war with himself is a man waiting for an ally to take one side.

**Character:** Introspective, honest, troubled, self-dramatising in a gentle way. The soldier's metaphor ('at war') is natural to him.

**Theme:** Inner conflict; honour versus love; appearance versus reality (the 'shows' of love).

**Device:** Metaphor (the mind as a battlefield); third-person self-reference; understatement; the word 'construe' as a thematic key.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch describes Brutus as thoughtful and reserved; his Stoic-leaning family (he was Cato's nephew) prized self-command, which is what Brutus here admits is failing. Caesar had pardoned him after Pharsalus and promoted him, which gives his unease a real foundation.

**Scene connection:** The inner war declared here becomes the soliloquy of Act 2 and, at the last, the sword at Philippi. Cassius seizes on it immediately: his next move is the mirror speech.

**ICSE keywords:** inner conflict; third-person self-reference; metaphor; introspection; tragic hero.

**Possible answer use:** 'Brutus's confession that he is "with himself at war" is the moment the conspiracy becomes possible; Cassius needs only to supply the enemy that Brutus's divided mind is looking for.'

### 11. "I, your glass" — Cassius offers to be Brutus's mirror

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** And since you know you cannot see yourself / So well as by reflection, I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of.

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Brutus.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, soon after Brutus admits his inner war.

**Context:** Cassius has asked, "Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?" and Brutus has answered that "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things." Cassius laments that Brutus has no mirror to show him his "hidden worthiness" and claims that many of the best men in Rome wish "that noble Brutus had his eyes". Brutus asks, warily, "Into what dangers would you lead me, Cassius". Cassius presses on.

**Simple meaning:** 'Since you agree that you can only see yourself in a reflection, I will be your mirror and modestly reveal to you the part of yourself you do not yet know.'

**Deep meaning:** This is the manipulator's charter. A mirror claims to add nothing; it only shows what is there. By calling himself a 'glass', Cassius disguises persuasion as revelation — whatever he says about Brutus will seem to come from Brutus. 'Modestly' is wonderfully dishonest: a mirror cannot be modest, and Cassius is about to flatter. The idea that the eye "sees not itself" is a philosophical commonplace which Cassius turns into a weapon.

**Subtext:** Cassius has heard the word 'war' and is now choosing Brutus's side for him. He will end the speech by insisting that he is no flatterer — "then hold me dangerous" — which is exactly what a flatterer would say.

**Character:** Cassius: subtle, patient, intellectually agile, dishonest in method even where sincere in grievance. Brutus: alert to danger but unable to refuse an appeal to his own virtue.

**Theme:** Manipulation; appearance versus reality (reflection, image); self-knowledge; friendship used as a tool.

**Device:** Extended metaphor of the mirror; irony ('modestly'); the pattern of question and answer that makes Brutus seem to lead the conversation he is being led through.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch reports that friends and anonymous messages urged Brutus to live up to his ancestor's name; Shakespeare gives that whole chorus of voices to one man and a bundle of forged letters. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — the mirror image also belongs to Renaissance thinking about counsel: a good adviser was supposed to be a 'glass' showing a prince his faults. Cassius borrows the honest adviser's language.

**Scene connection:** The false mirror becomes literal at the end of the scene when Cassius plans the letters "As if they came from several citizens", and in Scene 3 when Cinna is sent to place them. Both show Brutus a reflection of himself that Cassius has painted.

**ICSE keywords:** manipulation; extended metaphor; irony; self-knowledge; appearance versus reality; flattery.

**Possible answer use:** 'When Cassius offers to be Brutus's "glass", he disguises persuasion as reflection; the forged letters of the same scene are the mirror made of paper.'

### 12. "The name of honour more than I fear death" — Brutus's creed

**Exact quotation:**

> **Brutus:** If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently; / For let the gods so speed me as I love / The name of honour more than I fear death.

**Speaker:** Brutus.

**Listener:** Cassius.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, immediately after the first offstage shout.

**Context:** A flourish and a shout interrupt them. Brutus blurts out, "What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." Cassius pounces — "Ay, do you fear it? / Then must I think you would not have it so." Brutus admits, "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well", then asks what Cassius wants of him, and sets his conditions.

**Simple meaning:** 'If what you have to say concerns the public good, then put honour before one of my eyes and death before the other, and I will look at both with equal calm; for, so help me gods, I love the name of honour more than I fear death.'

**Deep meaning:** Brutus states his values in a single balanced sentence, and the sentence gives Cassius his key. Note what Brutus actually says: not 'honour' but 'the name of honour' — reputation, how a thing is called. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — many readers find this telling: Brutus loves honour partly as a word, and a man who loves the word can be persuaded that a dishonourable act is honourable if it is named correctly. Cassius replies at once, "Well, honour is the subject of my story."

**Subtext:** Brutus has just confessed, in two consecutive speeches, that he fears Caesar's kingship and that he loves Caesar. Cassius now knows the exact shape of the lever: the love must be made to yield to the fear in the name of honour.

**Character:** Idealistic, Stoic, courageous; also vain in the precise sense that his self-image matters to him more than consequences do.

**Theme:** Honour; the "general good" (republican duty); love versus duty; death.

**Device:** Antithesis (honour/death; love/fear); balanced syntax that performs the 'indifference' it describes; the key word 'name' that echoes Cassius's coming speech on names.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — the historical Brutus was Cato's nephew and later his son-in-law, and Cato's Stoic indifference to death was legendary. Plutarch presents Brutus as the one conspirator moved by principle rather than private grievance.

**Scene connection:** Everything Cassius says next is built to this specification: the Tiber story (Caesar's weakness), the Colossus (Rome's shame), the names (Brutus's ancestor). In Act 2 Brutus will reason his way to the murder in exactly these terms, and in Act 3 his insistence on the conspirators' honour — sparing Antony, letting Antony speak — will undo them.

**ICSE keywords:** honour; antithesis; idealism; tragic flaw; Stoicism; republican duty.

**Possible answer use:** 'Brutus's declaration that he loves "The name of honour more than I fear death" supplies Cassius with his method and the play with its tragedy: every later error of Brutus is made in honour's name.'

### 13. "I, as Aeneas, our great ancestor" — Cassius carries Caesar from the Tiber

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** Caesar cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!' / I, as Aeneas, our great ancestor, / Did from the flames of Troy upon his shoulder / The old Anchises bear, so from the waves of Tiber / Did I the tired Caesar. And this man / Is now become a god

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Brutus.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, the first long speech of Cassius's persuasion.

**Context:** Cassius has declared he "had as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself", and insisted "I was born free as Caesar; so were you". He then tells the story: on a "raw and gusty day" Caesar dared him — "Dar'st thou, Cassius, now / Leap in with me into this angry flood / And swim to yonder point?" — and Cassius, fully dressed ("Accoutered as I was"), plunged in. Caesar followed, tired, and cried for help.

**Simple meaning:** 'Caesar shouted for me to save him from drowning. Just as our ancestor Aeneas carried his old father Anchises out of burning Troy on his shoulders, so I carried the exhausted Caesar out of the Tiber. And this same man has now become a god.'

**Deep meaning:** The comparison is a trap Cassius sets for himself. Aeneas carrying Anchises is the great Roman image of piety — a son saving a father. By casting himself as Aeneas, Cassius casts Caesar as the aged, helpless father, which flatters his own strength and belittles Caesar's. But it also makes Caesar a father, and the play will punish the sons who kill him. The story's point is physical: the 'god' is a man with a body that fails. Cassius follows it with the fever in Spain — "'Tis true, this god did shake" — and the pitiless detail of Caesar crying for water "As a sick girl".

**Subtext:** Every sentence says 'I am stronger than Caesar, and yet I must bow to him.' The grievance is personal, not constitutional; Cassius dresses envy in republican clothes. He also repeats his own name in Caesar's mouth, savouring it.

**Character:** Cassius: proud, physically brave, resentful, a storyteller who shapes facts to purpose. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — the detail that he plunged in first and Caesar merely 'followed' subtly reverses the dare, so that even the story's beginning favours him.

**Theme:** Jealousy; the body of the ruler versus his public image; appearance versus reality ('god' versus drowning man); the Roman past as a standard.

**Device:** Epic simile (Aeneas and Anchises); anecdote as rhetorical proof; bathos ('a god' who cannot swim); direct speech within speech.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Aeneas, legendary ancestor of the Romans, carries his father from burning Troy in Virgil's Aeneid; the Julian family claimed descent from Aeneas, so Cassius is stealing Caesar's own ancestor. The swimming contest is Shakespeare's invention, though Plutarch and Suetonius do record Caesar's epilepsy; the fever in Spain has no specific ancient source.

**Scene connection:** The failing body reappears within minutes in Casca's report — Caesar "swooned and fell down" — and in Caesar's own admission "this ear is deaf". The Tiber that Marullus made tremble for Pompey is here the flood that nearly kills Caesar.

**ICSE keywords:** epic simile; classical allusion; jealousy; bathos; anecdote; appearance versus reality.

**Possible answer use:** 'Cassius's self-portrait as "Aeneas, our great ancestor" rescuing a drowning Caesar reveals the private root of a public conspiracy: the man who claims to fight for Rome cannot forgive having once been the stronger swimmer.'

### 14. "Like a Colossus" — Cassius's image of Caesar

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves.

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Brutus.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, immediately after the second offstage shout.

**Context:** Brutus has guessed that "these applauses are / For some new honours that are heaped on Caesar." Cassius seizes the moment.

**Simple meaning:** 'He stands astride the whole little world like the giant statue of Rhodes, and we small men walk between his enormous legs, looking about for somewhere shameful to be buried.'

**Deep meaning:** The image shrinks Rome ('narrow world') and inflates Caesar to a statue — not a man but a monument, hollow bronze. 'Petty men' (small, insignificant) is a calculated humiliation of Brutus as much as of Cassius: it invites him to resent his own smallness. 'Peep about' is a verb for mice. The 'dishonourable graves' touch Brutus where he has just told Cassius he is most sensitive — the "name of honour".

**Subtext:** Cassius's Caesar is not a tyrant doing wrong but a size doing wrong by existing. The grievance is scale, and the remedy implied is to cut the statue down.

**Character:** Cassius's gift for the vivid, physical image, and his habit of mixing self-pity ('we petty men') with contempt.

**Theme:** Power; jealousy; honour; the individual against the overwhelming state.

**Device:** Simile and hyperbole; classical allusion; visual imagery of scale; the pointed word 'dishonourable' aimed at Brutus's creed.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — the Colossus was the bronze statue of the sun-god at Rhodes (c. 280 BC), one of the Seven Wonders; the idea that it straddled the harbour so ships sailed between its legs is a medieval legend, which Shakespeare's picture depends on.

**Scene connection:** Flavius's soaring bird in Scene 1 has become a statue; in Scene 3 Cassius will call Caesar "prodigious grown". The hollow-monument idea pays off when Caesar is cut down in Act 3 and proves, as Antony says over the body, to be flesh.

**ICSE keywords:** simile; hyperbole; classical allusion; visual imagery; jealousy; power.

**Possible answer use:** 'Cassius's picture of Caesar bestriding "the narrow world / Like a Colossus" makes Caesar's greatness a personal insult to every Roman, which is exactly the feeling he must create in Brutus.'

### 15. "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars" — the most famous line

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** Men at some time are masters of their fates. / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Brutus.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, directly after the Colossus image.

**Context:** Having shown Caesar as a giant, Cassius must prevent Brutus from shrugging ('that is simply how things are'). So he denies fate.

**Simple meaning:** 'There are moments when men control their own destinies. The blame for our being inferiors, dear Brutus, lies not in the stars we were born under but in ourselves.'

**Deep meaning:** The line states the play's great question — fate or free will? — and takes the free-will side. 'Underlings' (subordinates, small people) is the sting. Elizabethans widely believed the stars influenced human fortune; Cassius says, in effect, 'stop blaming astrology and act.' Note 'at some time': even Cassius does not claim men are always masters of their fates — only that now is such a time.

**Subtext:** It is a call to action disguised as a philosophy. 'Dear Brutus' is intimate and manipulative at once.

**Character:** Cassius the rationalist and Epicurean, who believes in human agency — a belief the play will test to destruction.

**Theme:** Fate versus free will; responsibility; liberty.

**Device:** Aphorism (a compact general truth); antithesis (stars/ourselves); direct address; astrological imagery.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — the historical Cassius followed Epicurean philosophy, which denied that the gods interfered in human affairs; the line is true to that. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — the play's events seem to answer him, since the omens, the Soothsayer and later Caesar's ghost all suggest a design larger than men.

**Scene connection:** The same Cassius will, in Scene 3, read the storm as a divine message and bare his breast to the lightning; and in Act 5 the sceptic will admit that his beliefs have changed. Brutus's own fatalism in the final acts is the mirror image of this speech.

**ICSE keywords:** aphorism; antithesis; fate versus free will; dramatic irony; Epicureanism.

**Possible answer use:** 'Cassius's insistence that "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves" is the conspiracy's founding belief, and the play's omens, ghost and defeats test it to breaking point.'

::: insight Why the most famous line in the play is also its most ironic
LITERARY INTERPRETATION. Students quote "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars" as if it were Shakespeare's own motto, a stirring declaration that we make our own fates. Look at where it sits and who says it, and it begins to curdle. It is spoken by a manipulator in the act of manipulating: Cassius is not inviting Brutus to freedom but steering him towards a murder Cassius has already decided on. The man who says we are masters of our fates is busy mastering someone else's. Then look at what follows. Within a scene, this same rationalist is walking bare-chested through a supernatural storm and announcing that heaven has sent the prodigies as "instruments of fear and warning"; the sceptic has become a reader of signs. And the play's later shape seems to side against him — the Ides arrive exactly as warned, the conspirators are driven to the fates they tried to escape, and Cassius dies on his birthday, by his own hand, having misread what his eyes reported. Finally, the line's grammar deserves a second look: 'that we are underlings' is the fault Cassius locates in 'ourselves', but the remedy he proposes is not to rise by one's own merit — it is to remove the man above. The famous assertion of free will is a plan to make one Roman's fate the instrument of another's envy. It remains a magnificent line. But in an answer it is far more impressive to show that it is magnificent and false than to quote it as simple wisdom.
:::

### 16. "'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar'" — the argument from names

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** 'Brutus' and 'Caesar'—what should be in that 'Caesar'? / Why should that name be sounded more than yours? / Write them together, yours is as fair a name; / Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; / Weigh them, it is as heavy; conjure with 'em, / 'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar.'

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Brutus.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, continuing directly from the lines on fate.

**Context:** Cassius turns from philosophy to the one thing Brutus prizes most — his name — and ends by invoking the ancestor who expelled the kings: "There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king."

**Simple meaning:** 'What is so special about the name Caesar? Why should it be spoken more loudly than yours? Written side by side, yours is as fine; spoken, it suits the mouth as well; weighed, it is as heavy; use them as spells, and Brutus will raise a ghost as quickly as Caesar.'

**Deep meaning:** Cassius treats names as objects that can be written, sounded, weighed and used in magic — four tests, each of which Brutus's name passes. The method is an auction of vanity. 'Start a spirit' means summon a ghost: names in this play have real power, and Brutus's name is precisely what the conspiracy needs. Cassius then asks "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great?" and cries "Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" — making Brutus's ancestry a reproach.

**Subtext:** Cassius has found that honour, for Brutus, is partly a word; so he works on the word. The speech also quietly reveals Cassius's own obsession with names (he keeps saying his own).

**Character:** Cassius: rhetorically relentless, appealing to lineage because argument alone will not move Brutus.

**Theme:** Honour and reputation; the burden of ancestry; the power of language; republican memory.

**Device:** Rhetorical questions; parallel imperatives ('Write… Sound… Weigh… conjure'); the conjuring metaphor; allusion to Lucius Junius Brutus.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Lucius Junius Brutus led the expulsion of King Tarquin in 509 BC, founding the Republic; Marcus Brutus claimed descent from him. Plutarch records that anonymous messages reminded Brutus of this ancestor — 'You are no true Brutus' is the sense of them.

**Scene connection:** The spirit Cassius 'starts' with Brutus's name becomes, grimly, the ghost of Caesar in Act 4. The ancestor's statue is used in Scene 3, where Cinna is told to fix a letter "Upon old Brutus' statue".

**ICSE keywords:** rhetorical question; parallelism; allusion; vanity; manipulation; the power of names.

**Possible answer use:** 'By weighing the two names and declaring that "'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar'", Cassius makes Brutus's honour depend on acting; the forged letters that follow are this argument in writing.'

### 17. "Brutus had rather be a villager" — Brutus's conditional promise

**Exact quotation:**

> **Brutus:** Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us.

**Speaker:** Brutus.

**Listener:** Cassius.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, Brutus's reply to the long persuasion, just before Caesar's train returns.

**Context:** Brutus answers with care: "That you do love me, I am nothing jealous. / What you would work me to, I have some aim." He asks not to be pressed further now, promises to "consider" and to "find a time", and then, telling Cassius to "chew upon this", offers this.

**Simple meaning:** 'Brutus would rather be an insignificant villager than call himself a Roman under the harsh conditions this age is likely to impose on us.'

**Deep meaning:** It is a promise wrapped in a refusal. Brutus will not be rushed ('I have some aim' — I partly guess what you want), yet the closing sentence tells Cassius he has already lost the argument he pretends to postpone: he would rather give up Rome than live in it enslaved. The third person again ('Brutus had rather') shows him measuring himself against a public standard. 'Son of Rome' answers Cassius's "breed of noble bloods".

**Subtext:** Brutus believes he is keeping control; Cassius hears consent. Cassius's satisfied reply — "I am glad that my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus" — confirms that a little fire is all he needed.

**Character:** Cautious, dignified, honourable in intention, and already committed in feeling.

**Theme:** Republicanism and liberty; honour; patriotism; the individual against the state.

**Device:** Third-person self-reference; antithesis (villager / son of Rome); conditional phrasing that hides a decision; the metaphor of fire struck from flint in Cassius's reply.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Brutus had served Pompey against Caesar, been pardoned, and then accepted high office from him (urban praetor for 44 BC); the discomfort of a republican flourishing under a near-king is historically grounded.

**Scene connection:** Act 2 opens with Brutus's soliloquy, where the 'hard conditions' are reasoned into a death sentence; Cassius's closing soliloquy here plans the letters that will complete the work.

**ICSE keywords:** third-person self-reference; antithesis; patriotism; liberty; characterisation.

**Possible answer use:** 'Brutus's declaration that he "had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome" under tyranny is the conditional that Cassius's letters will convert into a decision.'

### 18. "a lean and hungry look" — Caesar reads Cassius

**Exact quotation:**

> **Caesar:** Let me have men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights. / Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous.

**Speaker:** Caesar.

**Listener:** Antony (Brutus and Cassius are watching from a distance).

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, as Caesar returns from the games with "The angry spot" glowing on his brow.

**Context:** Caesar has refused a crown three times offstage and fainted; his train "look like a chidden train". Catching sight of Cassius, he calls Antony to him.

**Simple meaning:** 'Give me plump, well-groomed men around me who sleep soundly at night. That Cassius over there looks thin and hungry. He thinks too much. Men like that are dangerous.'

**Deep meaning:** Caesar's political theory in four lines: contented men are safe, and the discontented show it in their bodies. 'Hungry' is literal and metaphorical — hunger for power. The judgement is acute; it is also the judgement of a man who prefers courtiers who do not think. Antony's "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given" is the first of several occasions on which Antony misjudges the conspirators.

**Subtext:** Caesar has seen what Brutus has not: Cassius's envy written on his face. The irony is that the one man in the play who reads Cassius correctly will do nothing about it.

**Character:** Caesar: shrewd, observant, vain about his own fearlessness, surrounded by flatterers of his own choosing.

**Theme:** Appearance and reality (the body as a sign of the mind); leadership; jealousy.

**Device:** Physiognomy as characterisation; antithesis (fat/lean); dramatic irony (the audience has just watched Cassius being dangerous); epigrammatic short sentences.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch reports Caesar saying he did not fear fat, long-haired men but the pale, thin ones, meaning Brutus and Cassius. Shakespeare keeps the thought and sharpens it into verse.

**Scene connection:** Caesar goes on — "He reads much, / He is a great observer, and he looks / Quite through the deeds of men" — and the portrait matches what we have just seen. In Scene 3 Cassius's 'hunger' becomes action, and in Act 3 the man Caesar should have avoided helps to kill him.

**ICSE keywords:** dramatic irony; physiognomy; antithesis; characterisation; appearance versus reality.

**Possible answer use:** 'Caesar's diagnosis that Cassius "has a lean and hungry look" is the sharpest piece of character-reading in Act 1 — and the play's irony is that the man who sees the danger most clearly refuses to act on it.'

### 19. "for always I am Caesar" — the public self and the deaf ear

**Exact quotation:**

> **Caesar:** I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar. / Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him.

**Speaker:** Caesar.

**Listener:** Antony.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, the end of Caesar's speech about Cassius, as the train moves off.

**Context:** Caesar has insisted, "Would he were fatter! But I fear him not", then described Cassius at length — a man who "Seldom he smiles" and is "never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves". He closes with these lines.

**Simple meaning:** 'I am telling you what deserves to be feared, not what I fear — because I am always Caesar. Come round to my right side, since this ear is deaf, and tell me honestly what you think of him.'

**Deep meaning:** Two consecutive lines contain the whole man. 'For always I am Caesar' claims that the name is a role above fear; 'this ear is deaf' admits a failing body. He speaks of himself in the third person, as a public institution, and in the same breath reveals a private defect. The deaf ear is also a metaphor the audience cannot miss: Caesar hears the right things on the wrong side and misses what matters.

**Subtext:** The request to "tell me truly" shows that behind the public mask Caesar does want counsel — and that he asks it of the one man who will tell him what he wants to hear.

**Character:** Caesar: self-mythologising, physically vulnerable, surprisingly candid in private.

**Theme:** Appearance versus reality; the public and private self; leadership and its isolation; pride.

**Device:** Third-person self-reference; antithesis ('what is to be feared' / 'what I fear'); symbolic physical detail; dramatic irony.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Caesar's deafness has no basis in the ancient sources; it appears to be Shakespeare's addition, while the epilepsy mentioned by Brutus and Casca is in Plutarch and Suetonius. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — the invented deafness is Shakespeare's way of making a political blindness physical.

**Scene connection:** Cassius has just mocked the body that cannot swim or hold off a fever; Caesar now confirms the body's weakness himself. In Act 2 and Act 3 'always I am Caesar' hardens into his refusal to yield to anyone's plea.

**ICSE keywords:** third-person self-reference; dramatic irony; symbolism; hubris; public versus private self.

**Possible answer use:** 'The gap between "for always I am Caesar" and "this ear is deaf" in consecutive lines is Shakespeare's compact portrait of a ruler whose public name has outgrown the man who bears it.'

### 20. "he would fain have had it" — Casca reports the crown

**Exact quotation:**

> **Casca:** he put it by once; but for all that, to my thinking, he would fain have had it. Then he offered it to him again; then he put it by again; but to my thinking, he was very loath to lay his fingers off it.

**Speaker:** Casca.

**Listener:** Brutus and Cassius.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, after Caesar's train has left; Brutus has plucked Casca by the cloak.

**Context:** Casca has already said, "there was a crown offered him; and, being offered him, he put it by with the back of his hand, thus, and then the people fell a-shouting." Pressed for "the manner of it", he protests that "It was mere foolery; I did not mark it", corrects himself ("'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets"), and gives this account before describing how "the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps" until Caesar "swooned and fell down at it".

**Simple meaning:** 'He pushed it away once, but in my opinion he would gladly have taken it. Antony offered it again; he refused again; but in my opinion he was very reluctant to take his fingers off it.'

**Deep meaning:** The crucial words are 'to my thinking', twice. Casca gives facts (three offers, three refusals) and then an interpretation (Caesar wanted it), and he is honest enough to mark where one ends and the other begins. The "rabblement" (rabble), the "sweaty nightcaps" and the "stinking breath" show a patrician's disgust for the crowd. We never see the offer; we receive it through a witness who despises everybody involved.

**Subtext:** Casca's sourness is useful to Cassius — Caesar as a hypocrite who performs modesty — but the report also undercuts the conspirators' case, since Caesar did, three times, refuse.

**Character:** Casca: blunt, cynical, snobbish, more observant than he pretends ('I did not mark it' is followed by a detailed account).

**Theme:** Ambition; appearance versus reality; public opinion; political theatre.

**Device:** Reported action (teichoscopy, the narrating of an offstage event); prose; irony; sensory imagery of the crowd.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch describes Antony offering Caesar a diadem at the Lupercalia, with feeble applause for the offer and loud applause for the refusal; in Plutarch it happens twice, in Casca's account three times. The cynical reading that Caesar wanted the crown is Shakespeare's addition through Casca.

**Scene connection:** The three shouts that interrupted Cassius's persuasion are here explained. In Act 3 Antony will use the same refusal as proof that Caesar was not ambitious — the one event, read two ways.

**ICSE keywords:** reported action; irony; ambition; point of view; prose; unreliable witness.

**Possible answer use:** 'Casca's "to my thinking, he would fain have had it" is the Act 1 evidence that Antony turns upside down in his funeral oration, and a student writing on Act 3 who recalls it can show how one refusal serves two opposite arguments.'

::: insight Why Casca's prose is quotable
LITERARY INTERPRETATION. Students often assume that only verse is 'quotable' and skip Casca's report as mere plot. The opposite is true, and understanding why makes you a better reader of the whole play. Casca is the only patrician in Act 1 who speaks prose, and he does it deliberately — Cassius calls it his "sour fashion", Brutus says he "puts on this tardy form". The prose is a disguise: a clever man playing the blunt soldier so that nobody expects anything of him. That gives his sentences a special texture. They are concrete where the verse is abstract — "sweaty nightcaps", "chopped hands", "stinking breath", a crown that is "one of these coronets", Caesar who "plucked me ope his doublet". While Cassius talks of Colossi and stars, Casca tells you what the air smelled like. They are also rhythmically memorable because they are built on repetition and tagging: "to my thinking… to my thinking", "put it by… put it by… put it by", and the famous throwaway "it was Greek to me". And they are judgements pretending to be reports — every noun carries contempt. For the examination this matters practically: Casca gives you the only eyewitness account of the crown-offering, and the phrases are short, exact and easy to embed. For analysis it matters more: his prose is where the play lets a sceptic puncture everybody's rhetoric, Caesar's included. When the same man speaks terrified verse in Scene 3, the change of register tells you how frightening the night is.
:::

### 21. "as they use to do the players in the theatre" — Casca on the crowd

**Exact quotation:**

> **Casca:** If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theatre, I am no true man.

**Speaker:** Casca.

**Listener:** Brutus and Cassius.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, after Cassius's joke about the "falling sickness".

**Context:** Casca has reported that Caesar "fell down in the market-place and foamed at mouth and was speechless". He adds this, then describes Caesar offering the crowd "his throat to cut", the women crying "Alas, good soul!" and his verdict that "if Caesar had stabbed their mothers, they would have done no less". Later in the same speech he reports Cicero spoke Greek — "it was Greek to me" — and that "Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence."

**Simple meaning:** 'If the ragged crowd did not applaud and boo him exactly according to whether he pleased or displeased them, just as they do actors in a playhouse, then I am not an honest man.'

**Deep meaning:** Casca names the play's recurring idea: politics is theatre and the people are its audience. 'Tag-rag' (ragged, worthless) is a snob's word; but the comparison is precise — the crowd's applause is bought by performance, and Caesar's offer of his throat is a stage gesture. The audience in a London theatre in 1599, hearing this, was being told that it, too, could be played upon.

**Subtext:** The sceptic who sees through Caesar's acting is himself performing bluntness; nobody in this scene is quite what he seems.

**Character:** Casca's contempt for the commons and sharp eye for hypocrisy.

**Theme:** Mob psychology; appearance versus reality; political theatre; public opinion.

**Device:** Simile (crowd as theatre audience); metatheatre (a play commenting on playgoing); prose; hyperbole in the oath "I am no true man".

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch records Caesar's epilepsy and the crowd's reactions at the Lupercalia; the offered throat is in Plutarch's account of a different occasion, which Shakespeare folds in here. The fate of the tribunes (deposed from office) is historical; "put to silence" leaves their exact fate deliberately vague.

**Scene connection:** The news about the tribunes closes the loop with Scene 1 — the feather-pluckers have been punished. The theatre simile predicts the Forum scene of Act 3, where two speakers compete for the same audience's applause.

**ICSE keywords:** simile; metatheatre; fickle crowd; mob psychology; irony; prose.

**Possible answer use:** 'Casca's comparison of the crowd to playgoers who "clap him and hiss him" according to his performance prepares us for Act 3, where Antony treats the Forum as a stage and wins the audience Brutus loses.'

### 22. "we have the falling sickness" — Cassius's grim pun

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** No, Caesar hath it not; but you and I / And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness.

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Brutus and Casca.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, in the middle of Casca's report.

**Context:** Cassius, startled — "But soft, I pray you. What, did Caesar swoon?" — hears that Caesar fell and foamed at the mouth. Brutus explains kindly, "'Tis very like; he hath the falling sickness." Cassius turns the diagnosis into a political joke.

**Simple meaning:** 'No — Caesar does not have the falling sickness. We do: you and I and honest Casca are the ones who are falling (losing our standing) while he rises.'

**Deep meaning:** 'Falling sickness' was the ordinary name for epilepsy; Cassius makes it mean Rome's political collapse. The pun is cruel and brilliant — it refuses pity for Caesar and converts his weakness into a charge against everyone who tolerates him. Casca's reply, "I know not what you mean by that", shows that the joke is above the blunt soldier's head, or that he pretends it is.

**Subtext:** Cassius cannot let a fact about Caesar stand without turning it against him. The 'honest' before Casca's name is a first touch of recruitment.

**Character:** Cassius: quick, bitter, incapable of sympathy for Caesar; Brutus: humane, naming the illness without malice.

**Theme:** Jealousy; the ruler's body as a political sign; manipulation.

**Device:** Pun; antithesis (Caesar/we); irony; the contrast between Brutus's compassion and Cassius's wit.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch and Suetonius both report Caesar's epilepsy; modern historians debate the diagnosis. The word 'falling sickness' is the Elizabethan term.

**Scene connection:** Cassius's earlier "this god did shake" and Casca's swoon together build the theme of Caesar's fragile body that Act 2's superstitious, hesitating Caesar will continue. The word 'fall' is reclaimed by Caesar's enemies when he literally falls in Act 3.

**ICSE keywords:** pun; irony; antithesis; characterisation by contrast; political metaphor.

**Possible answer use:** 'Cassius's retort that it is "you and I / And honest Casca" who "have the falling sickness" shows how he converts every fact about Caesar into a grievance — here turning Brutus's compassion into a political diagnosis.'

### 23. "Thy honourable mettle may be wrought" — Cassius alone

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** Well, Brutus, thou art noble. Yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed. Therefore it is meet / That noble minds keep ever with their likes; / For who so firm that cannot be seduced? / Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus.

**Speaker:** Cassius, in soliloquy.

**Listener:** No one; the audience.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 2, the closing speech, after Brutus has gone home.

**Context:** Brutus has left, agreeing to meet tomorrow; Cassius's parting instruction was "think of the world". Before this Brutus remarked that Casca "was quick mettle when he went to school", and Cassius explained that "This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit".

**Simple meaning:** 'Brutus, you are noble; but I can see that your honourable nature can be worked (like metal) away from its natural shape. So noble men should keep company only with their equals — for who is so steadfast that he cannot be led astray? Caesar bears a grudge against me, but he loves Brutus.'

**Deep meaning:** This is the only moment in Act 1 when Cassius tells the truth about what he is doing. 'Mettle' (temperament) puns on 'metal' that can be 'wrought' — hammered into a new shape. The word 'seduced' is his own choice for his method. He then plans the forgeries: letters "In several hands", thrown in at Brutus's windows "As if they came from several citizens", in which "Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at", and closes, "let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure."

**Subtext:** 'If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humour me' — Cassius knows Brutus would never do to him what he is doing to Brutus. That knowledge is as near to a conscience as Cassius comes.

**Character:** Cassius: candid with himself, admiring of Brutus, entirely unscrupulous about means; resentful that Caesar "doth bear me hard".

**Theme:** Manipulation; honour as something that can be forged; betrayal of friendship; appearance versus reality.

**Device:** Soliloquy; metaphor and pun (mettle/metal, wrought); rhetorical question; rhyming couplet to close the scene (sure/endure).

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch reports anonymous notes left on Brutus's praetor's chair and his ancestor's statue urging him to act; Shakespeare makes Cassius their author, turning a popular movement into one man's deceit.

**Scene connection:** The letters are handed to Cinna in Scene 3 and read by Brutus in Act 2, where they tip him into the conspiracy. The idea of noble metal 'wrought' from its true shape is the play's image for what happens to Brutus.

**ICSE keywords:** soliloquy; pun; metaphor; manipulation; dramatic irony; forgery.

**Possible answer use:** 'Only in soliloquy does Cassius admit that Brutus's "honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed"; the forged letters are the hammer, and Brutus's Act 2 decision is the new shape.'


## Scene 3 — The Storm

A word on time before the quotations. Scene 2 ended in the afternoon of the Lupercalia; Scene 3 opens on the night before the Ides of March. HISTORICAL RECORD — that is a month later, and Shakespeare simply does not mention the gap. Cicero's question "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" is the only clue. Keep this in mind when you write about Cassius: the man who stood on the Tiber bank in Scene 2 has had four weeks to recruit, and the storm gives him his last and best night.

### 24. "a tempest dropping fire" — Casca's terror

**Exact quotation:**

> **Casca:** But never till tonight, never till now, / Did I go through a tempest dropping fire. / Either there is a civil strife in heaven, / Or else the world, too saucy with the gods, / Incenses them to send destruction.

**Speaker:** Casca, sword drawn, breathless.

**Listener:** Cicero, who has met him in the street and asked why he is "breathless" and why he stares so.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, the opening lines of the scene.

**Context:** Thunder and lightning. Casca has asked, "Are not you moved, when all the sway of earth / Shakes like a thing unfirm?" and has recalled ordinary storms in which "the scolding winds / Have rived the knotty oaks". This storm is of another order.

**Simple meaning:** 'Never before tonight have I walked through a storm that rained fire. Either the gods are fighting a civil war among themselves, or the world has grown so insolent towards the gods that it has provoked them to destroy it.'

**Deep meaning:** The blunt prose-speaker of Scene 2 now speaks shaken verse, and the shift of register is itself the meaning: what frightens Casca is not weather but the sense that the order of things has broken. Both his explanations are political. 'Civil strife in heaven' projects Rome's own civil wars on to the sky; a world 'too saucy with the gods' names the sin of a man who has let himself be treated as one. The word 'saucy' (insolent, presumptuous) is deliberately small for a cosmic charge — it is Casca's instinct to deflate.

**Subtext:** Casca has not yet said the name 'Caesar', but every image points to him. The storm has done Cassius's work before Cassius arrives: it has made a sceptic believe that something monstrous is loose.

**Character:** Casca: superstitious under the cynicism, physically brave (he has drawn his sword against the sky) and morally pliable — fear will make him join a conspiracy within fifty lines.

**Theme:** Omens and superstition; the disorder of nature mirroring disorder in the state; fear; the gods' displeasure.

**Device:** Pathetic fallacy (the heavens express the political crisis); hyperbole ('dropping fire'); antithesis of the two explanations ('Either… Or else'); repetition ('never till tonight, never till now').

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch's Life of Caesar lists portents before the assassination: lights in the sky, crashing noises at night, birds in the Forum, and a slave whose hand blazed without burning. Shakespeare gives those portents to Casca and lets the audience hear the storm in the theatre's own thunder effects.

**Scene connection:** The 'civil strife in heaven' is the civil war of Acts 4 and 5 forecast in the sky. The same storm rages on through Act 2, where Calpurnia reports the night's horrors and Caesar at first decides not to go to the Capitol.

**ICSE keywords:** pathetic fallacy; omen; portent; hyperbole; change of register (prose to verse); foreshadowing.

**Possible answer use:** 'Casca's "tempest dropping fire" is the first voice in the play to read the weather politically — "a civil strife in heaven" — and his fear is the soil in which Cassius plants the conspiracy a few lines later.'

### 25. "let not men say / 'These are their reasons, they are natural'" — the catalogue of prodigies

**Exact quotation:**

> **Casca:** When these prodigies / Do so conjointly meet, let not men say / 'These are their reasons, they are natural,' / For I believe they are portentous things / Unto the climate that they point upon.

**Speaker:** Casca.

**Listener:** Cicero.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, at the end of Casca's list of wonders.

**Context:** Cicero has asked, calmly, whether Casca saw "anything more wonderful". Casca lists four: a slave whose hand "did flame and burn / Like twenty torches joined" yet "remained unscorched"; a lion near the Capitol that "glazed upon me and went surly by"; "a hundred ghastly women" who swore they saw "Men all in fire walk up and down the streets"; and "the bird of night" — the owl — sitting "Even at noonday upon the market-place, / Hooting and shrieking." Then he draws his conclusion.

**Simple meaning:** 'When so many unnatural signs happen together, nobody should say, 'There are natural explanations for each of these.' I believe they are omens aimed at the very country where they appear.'

**Deep meaning:** Casca's argument is one of accumulation: one wonder might be explained away, but four at once ('conjointly') cannot be. 'Prodigies' are unnatural events taken as omens; 'portentous' means foreshadowing disaster; 'climate' here means region. Notice that Casca anticipates the rationalist objection and forbids it — which tells us that the rationalist objection exists, and is about to be voiced by Cicero. The owl at noon is the most telling image: a creature of darkness in the public square in daylight, which is what a dictator is to a republic.

**Subtext:** Casca is frightened of his own perceptions and wants them confirmed. He is, in effect, asking Cicero to tell him the world has gone mad. Cicero will decline.

**Character:** Casca: an observer, as Caesar said of Cassius — he saw the slave, the lion, the women, the owl — but an observer who interprets by fear.

**Theme:** Superstition versus reason; omens; fate; the unnatural as a sign of political disorder.

**Device:** Catalogue (a list building to a climax); simile ("Like twenty torches joined"); symbolism (the owl, the lion); anticipation of a counter-argument (a form of rhetorical pre-emption).

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — the burning slave is in Plutarch almost word for word; the lion and the owl are Shakespeare's additions, though both carried traditional meanings (the lion of kingship, the owl of death). LITERARY INTERPRETATION — the lion that passes Casca "Without annoying me" is often read as Caesar himself, dangerous but not yet striking; the text leaves it open.

**Scene connection:** Cassius will reuse the lion within minutes — a man who "roars / As doth the lion in the Capitol" — and Caesar in Act 2 will boast that danger and he are 'two lions'. The 'ghastly women' and 'men all in fire' recur in Calpurnia's report of the night in Act 2.

**ICSE keywords:** prodigy; portent; catalogue; symbolism; superstition; rational versus supernatural reading.

**Possible answer use:** 'Casca insists the prodigies are "portentous things" and refuses the natural explanation in advance; Cicero's reply, that men "construe things after their fashion", is the play's own warning against believing him too quickly.'

### 26. "men may construe things after their fashion" — Cicero's calm

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cicero:** Indeed, it is a strange-disposèd time. / But men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves. / Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?

**Speaker:** Cicero, the orator and senator.

**Listener:** Casca.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, Cicero's only substantial speech in Act 1, after Casca's catalogue.

**Context:** Casca has just forbidden anyone to say the prodigies are natural. Cicero's answer is cool, brief and sceptical. Having asked his practical question and heard that Caesar will attend ("he did bid Antonius / Send word to you he would be there tomorrow"), Cicero leaves: "Good night, then, Casca. This disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in."

**Simple meaning:** 'Yes, it is a strangely unsettled time. But people interpret events according to their own inclinations, quite apart from what the events actually mean. Is Caesar coming to the Capitol tomorrow?'

**Deep meaning:** These are among the wisest lines in the play, and nobody on stage listens to them. 'Construe' means interpret; 'after their fashion' means in their own way; 'clean from' means entirely apart from. Cicero does not deny that the night is strange; he denies that Casca's fear is knowledge. Then he changes the subject to the one fact that matters. The move from omens to 'Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?' is the move from superstition to politics — Cicero is, in effect, the only character who keeps the two apart.

**Subtext:** LITERARY INTERPRETATION — Cicero's question suggests he already suspects that tomorrow is decisive; his departure suggests he intends to be nowhere near it. He is the intelligent man who sees and declines to act, which is why the conspirators in Act 2 decide to leave him out.

**Character:** Cicero: urbane, rational, detached, a touch patronising, politically careful. Casca said in Scene 2 that Cicero "spoke Greek" at the Lupercal — a man whose cleverness is above the crowd.

**Theme:** Appearance versus reality; interpretation and misreading; reason against superstition; the limits of intellect in a crisis.

**Device:** Aphorism; antithesis (men's 'fashion' against 'the things themselves'); a deflating change of subject; dramatic irony (the audience knows the omens are, in this play, accurate).

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BC) was Rome's greatest orator and a defender of the Republic; he was not part of the conspiracy, though he welcomed Caesar's death, and he was killed on the triumvirs' orders in December 43 BC. His speaking Greek is plausible: educated Romans were bilingual.

**Scene connection:** Cicero's warning about construing is the key to the whole play's pattern of misreading — Brutus construing the forged letters as the voice of Rome in Act 2, Caesar accepting Decius's flattering construction of Calpurnia's dream, and Cassius misreading what he sees at Philippi in Act 5. Brutus used the same verb of himself in Scene 2: "Nor construe any further my neglect".

**ICSE keywords:** aphorism; dramatic irony; scepticism; interpretation; appearance versus reality; foil (Cicero as foil to Casca).

**Possible answer use:** 'Cicero's observation that "men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves" is a one-sentence theory of the whole tragedy, since every disaster that follows begins with somebody misreading a sign.'

### 27. "Have bared my bosom to the thunder-stone" — Cassius defies the storm

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** For my part, I have walked about the streets, / Submitting me unto the perilous night, / And thus unbracèd, Casca, as you see, / Have bared my bosom to the thunder-stone; / And when the cross blue lightning seemed to open / The breast of heaven, I did present myself / Even in the aim and very flash of it.

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Casca (Cicero has gone).

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, immediately after the two men identify each other — "Who's there?" "A Roman." "Casca, by your voice."

**Context:** Casca has asked, "Cassius, what night is this!" Cassius replies with this account of what he has been doing while Casca cowered.

**Simple meaning:** 'As for me, I have been walking the streets, exposing myself to this dangerous night; with my doublet unlaced, as you see, I have bared my chest to the thunderbolt, and when the forked blue lightning seemed to tear open the sky I stood directly in its path.'

**Deep meaning:** Cassius makes himself the opposite of Casca: where Casca trembles, Cassius dares. 'Unbracèd' means with his clothing unfastened; the 'thunder-stone' was the stone that Elizabethans believed fell with a lightning strike. The gesture is a piece of theatre — he has been standing in the rain preparing an entrance — and it works. Casca's reaction is the pious one: "It is the part of men to fear and tremble / When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us." Cassius's reply is the rebuke of a recruiting officer: "You are dull, Casca, and those sparks of life / That should be in a Roman you do want".

**Subtext:** The Epicurean who told Brutus that the fault is not in our stars is now using the stars. He does not say the storm means nothing; he says it means Caesar. The sceptic has become a prophet because prophecy is useful tonight.

**Character:** Cassius: fearless, theatrical, adaptable in belief, contemptuous of weakness in others. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — some readers find real exaltation here, a man who feels the night is on his side; others see pure calculation. Both are probably present.

**Theme:** Fate and free will (now entangled); manliness and fear; the manipulation of belief; the individual against the heavens.

**Device:** Hyperbole; personification ('the breast of heaven'); visual imagery ('cross blue lightning'); contrast between the two men; the symbolic gesture of the bared chest.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — the historical Cassius was an Epicurean, and Epicureans held that gods did not send signs; Shakespeare knows this and makes the contradiction a character trait. Plutarch records that Cassius, before Philippi, spoke of his philosophy wavering.

**Scene connection:** The bared breast returns in Act 4, when Cassius offers Brutus his dagger and his 'naked breast' in the quarrel; and in Act 5 the same man admits that he has begun to give credit to omens. The contradiction with the "fault, dear Brutus" speech of Scene 2 is the chief reason that line is ironic.

**ICSE keywords:** hyperbole; personification; contrast; characterisation; symbolic gesture; inconsistency as character.

**Possible answer use:** 'When Cassius boasts that he has "bared my bosom to the thunder-stone", the rationalist of Scene 2 reinvents himself as a man in league with the storm, and the change tells us that his beliefs serve his purposes rather than the reverse.'

### 28. "yet prodigious grown / And fearful" — the unnamed man

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** Now could I, Casca, name to thee a man / Most like this dreadful night, / That thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars / As doth the lion in the Capitol; / A man no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action, yet prodigious grown / And fearful, as these strange eruptions are.

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Casca.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, after Cassius has rebuked Casca's fear.

**Context:** Cassius has told Casca that the gods have sent the prodigies "instruments of fear and warning / Unto some monstrous state". Now he lets Casca guess the man he means. Casca does: "'Tis Caesar that you mean, is it not, Cassius?" Cassius: "Let it be who it is."

**Simple meaning:** 'I could name you a man who is exactly like this terrible night — who thunders, flashes, opens graves and roars like the lion at the Capitol; a man no stronger than you or me in his own body, yet grown unnaturally huge and frightening, just like these strange outbursts of nature.'

**Deep meaning:** Cassius does to the storm what he did to the Tiber: he turns a fact into a portrait of Caesar. The storm becomes a metaphor for a man; the man becomes a prodigy (an unnatural growth). The brilliance lies in 'no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action' — the Scene 2 argument about the drowning swimmer, now compressed to a line. The refusal to say the name ('Let it be who it is') is itself persuasion: Casca must speak the name himself, and so commit himself.

**Subtext:** Cassius tests whether Casca is safe. By making Casca say 'Caesar', he gets a confession before he has made one. His follow-up — "Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish" — shames Casca into manliness, the same lever he used on Brutus's honour.

**Character:** Cassius: a master of the indirect approach, who never asks a man to join him but arranges for the man to offer.

**Theme:** Tyranny as unnatural growth; manipulation; the equality of men's bodies against the inequality of their power; manliness as a political value.

**Device:** Extended metaphor (Caesar as the storm); simile (the lion); periphrasis (describing without naming); verbal series ('thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars').

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Casca adds the news that "the senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king", to wear his crown "In every place save here in Italy". Shakespeare takes this from Plutarch and Suetonius, who report a rumour that a Sibylline prophecy said only a king could conquer Parthia, and that Caesar would be named king outside Italy. It was a rumour, not a decree, but the play treats it as the trigger.

**Scene connection:** Flavius's soaring hawk and Cassius's Colossus reach their third form here: Caesar as a storm. All three are images of unnatural size. The lion 'in the Capitol' links Casca's omen to Caesar's own boast in Act 2 and to the Capitol where the play's Caesar dies.

**ICSE keywords:** extended metaphor; periphrasis; manipulation; tyranny; imagery of unnatural growth; rhetorical strategy.

**Possible answer use:** 'By describing Caesar as a man "no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action, yet prodigious grown", Cassius converts the storm from an omen into an argument, and Casca's naming of Caesar is the first step of his recruitment.'

### 29. "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" — the dagger and the self

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cassius:** I know where I will wear this dagger then; / Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius. / Therein, you gods, you make the weak most strong; / Therein, you gods, you tyrants do defeat.

**Speaker:** Cassius.

**Listener:** Casca.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, directly after Casca reports that the Senate means to crown Caesar tomorrow.

**Context:** The rumour of a crown provokes Cassius's most passionate outburst. He goes on: "Nor stony tower, nor walls of beaten brass, / Nor airless dungeon, nor strong links of iron / Can be retentive to the strength of spirit; / But life, being weary of these worldly bars, / Never lacks power to dismiss itself." Casca agrees — "So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity."

**Simple meaning:** 'Then I know where I shall carry this dagger — into my own heart. Cassius will free Cassius from slavery. In that way, gods, you make the weak strongest; in that way you defeat tyrants.'

**Deep meaning:** On its surface this is a Stoic commonplace: no tyrant can enslave a man who is willing to die, because death is always available. 'Bondage' is slavery; 'retentive to' means able to hold in. But listen to the grammar — 'Cassius… will deliver Cassius', the name twice, the self rescuing the self. It is the Tiber story again, with Cassius as both the drowning man and the saviour. The threat of suicide is also a rhetorical feint: a man who says he will kill himself rather than live under Caesar is a breath away from saying he will kill Caesar instead, and that is the step Casca takes for him.

**Subtext:** The speech sounds like despair and functions as recruitment. It shows Casca the exit from fear — act, or die — and dares him to be less brave than Cassius. There is real feeling in it, too: Cassius's hatred of 'bondage' is the one constant in him.

**Character:** Cassius: proud to the point of self-worship, genuinely contemptuous of servility, and already, in Act 1, rehearsing the death he will choose at Philippi.

**Theme:** Liberty versus slavery; tyranny; free will (death as the last freedom); the Roman ideal of the noble suicide.

**Device:** Repetition of the name (a form of antimetabole, the same word at both ends); apostrophe to the gods; anaphora ('Therein, you gods'); the list of prisons ('stony tower… walls of beaten brass… airless dungeon… links of iron') as rhetorical amplification.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Roman culture honoured suicide as an escape from dishonour; Cato's death at Utica (46 BC), rather than accept Caesar's pardon, was the model. The historical Cassius died by his own hand at Philippi on 3 October 42 BC. LITERARY INTERPRETATION — the lines read as a deliberate forecast of that end.

**Scene connection:** The dagger Cassius says he will turn on himself is turned on Caesar in Act 3 and finally on Cassius in Act 5. The idea that life 'never lacks power to dismiss itself' returns in Brutus's debate about suicide before the battle and in his own death.

**ICSE keywords:** antimetabole; apostrophe; anaphora; liberty versus bondage; Stoicism; foreshadowing.

**Possible answer use:** 'Cassius's vow that "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" is at once a Stoic creed and a recruiting speech; for a student writing on Act 5, it is the earliest statement of the death he eventually chooses.'

### 30. "Hold. My hand." — Casca joins

**Exact quotation:**

> **Casca:** You speak to Casca, and to such a man / That is no fleering telltale. Hold. My hand. / Be factious for redress of all these griefs, / And I will set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest.
> **Cassius:** There's a bargain made.

**Speaker:** Casca; then Cassius.

**Listener:** Cassius; then Casca.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, the moment the conspiracy gains its first recruit on stage.

**Context:** Cassius has gone further than he intended — "What trash is Rome, / What rubbish and what offal, when it serves / For the base matter to illuminate / So vile a thing as Caesar!" — and then checked himself: "I perhaps speak this / Before a willing bondman; then I know / My answer must be made. But I am armed, / And dangers are to me indifferent." Casca answers the implied challenge.

**Simple meaning:** 'You are talking to Casca, and to a man who is no sneering informer. Stop — here is my hand. Form a party to put right all these wrongs, and I will go as far as the man who goes farthest.'

**Deep meaning:** Three monosyllables — 'Hold. My hand.' — do what three hundred lines of persuasion did for Brutus. Casca's pledge is physical, a handshake, and absolute: he will go 'as far / As who goes farthest'. 'Fleering' means sneering or grinning falsely; 'telltale' is an informer; 'factious' here means forming a faction, which in Elizabethan ears was a loaded, dangerous word. Casca is swearing loyalty to a faction without yet knowing its plan or its members. Cassius's reply, 'There's a bargain made', is the language of the market: a deal has been struck, and the price is to be paid in blood.

**Subtext:** Casca's offended dignity ('no fleering telltale') shows that Cassius's apparent fear of informers was the final lever: nobody wants to be suspected of cowardice or treachery. Casca joins partly to prove he is not what Cassius pretended to fear.

**Character:** Casca: impulsive, proud, brave, easily led; the man who will strike the first blow in Act 3 is recruited in a sentence. Cassius: closing the deal with businesslike speed and at once telling Casca the others "stay for me / In Pompey's Porch".

**Theme:** Loyalty and faction; manipulation; honour as a lever; the speed with which fear becomes violence.

**Device:** Monosyllabic, broken syntax ('Hold. My hand.') for decision; commercial metaphor ('bargain'); the handshake as symbolic action; contrast with Brutus's slow consent.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Publius Servilius Casca Longus struck the first blow on the Ides of March; Plutarch says he struck Caesar in the neck. Shakespeare's Casca, recruited here, fulfils that record in Act 3. Cassius's words that the night's "complexion" is like "the work we have in hand, / Most bloody, fiery, and most terrible" make the plan explicit for the first time.

**Scene connection:** Casca's 'foot' that will go farthest is the hand that strikes first in Act 3. The 'bargain' here contrasts with Brutus's refusal, in Act 2, to let the conspirators swear an oath — Cassius trades, Brutus trusts.

**ICSE keywords:** symbolic action; monosyllables; commercial metaphor; recruitment; faction; characterisation by contrast.

**Possible answer use:** 'Casca's "Hold. My hand." seals the conspiracy in three words and sets Casca's impulsiveness against Brutus's month of agonising; Cassius's "There's a bargain made" reveals how he sees the enterprise — as a transaction.'

### 31. "O Cassius, if you could / But win the noble Brutus to our party" — Cinna's plea

**Exact quotation:**

> **Cinna:** Yes, you are. O Cassius, if you could / But win the noble Brutus to our party—

**Speaker:** Cinna, a conspirator, arriving in haste.

**Listener:** Cassius, with Casca listening.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, after Casca's pledge, as the conspirators begin to gather.

**Context:** Cassius knows Cinna "by his gait". Cinna, nervous — "What a fearful night is this! / There's two or three of us have seen strange sights" — confirms that the others are waiting for Cassius ("Am I not stayed for? Tell me." "Yes, you are."), and then blurts out the thing the whole conspiracy wants.

**Simple meaning:** 'Yes, they are waiting for you. Oh, Cassius, if only you could bring the noble Brutus over to our side—'

**Deep meaning:** The sentence breaks off — Cassius interrupts with "Be you content" — and the break is eloquent: the wish is so large that Cinna cannot finish it. 'Party' means faction. The adjective 'noble' is fixed to Brutus like a title; the conspirators do not want Brutus's sword but his reputation. This is the Scene 2 argument about the name 'Brutus' heard from the other side: Cassius said the name was as good as Caesar's, and here are men who agree. The plea also confirms what Cassius's soliloquy implied — the plot already exists without Brutus and knows it is incomplete.

**Subtext:** Cinna's anxiety ('two or three of us have seen strange sights') shows that fear is general among the conspirators; they need Brutus partly as a cure for their own nerves. Cassius's brisk reply — handing out the forged papers — shows that he has already done the work Cinna begs for.

**Character:** Cinna: eager, nervous, a follower; useful to Shakespeare as the voice of the ordinary conspirator. Cassius: in command, dealing out tasks like a general.

**Theme:** Honour as political capital; reputation; the conspiracy's dependence on one man; fear.

**Device:** Aposiopesis (a sentence deliberately broken off); the fixed epithet 'noble'; dramatic irony (we know the letters are forged).

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — this is Lucius Cornelius Cinna, praetor in 44 BC and a conspirator, not to be confused with Cinna the poet whom the mob kills in Act 3. Plutarch confirms that the conspirators sought Brutus because his name would make the deed appear just rather than factious.

**Scene connection:** Cassius's instructions follow at once: "Good Cinna, take this paper, / And look you lay it in the praetor's chair, / Where Brutus may but find it; and throw this / In at his window; set this up with wax / Upon old Brutus' statue." These are the letters planned in the Scene 2 soliloquy and read in Act 2. Cinna's wish is granted in Act 2 when Brutus takes the conspirators' hands.

**ICSE keywords:** aposiopesis; epithet; reputation; dramatic irony; the conspiracy's dependence on Brutus.

**Possible answer use:** 'Cinna's unfinished plea — "if you could / But win the noble Brutus to our party—" — shows that the conspirators value Brutus for his name, which is exactly why Cassius's forged letters are addressed to that name.'

### 32. "like richest alchemy" — Casca on what Brutus is for

**Exact quotation:**

> **Casca:** O, he sits high in all the people's hearts, / And that which would appear offence in us / His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness.
> **Cassius:** Him and his worth and our great need of him / You have right well conceited.

**Speaker:** Casca; then Cassius.

**Listener:** Cassius; then Casca.

**Act/Scene:** Act 1 Scene 3, the closing lines of the act.

**Context:** Cinna has gone to deliver the papers. Cassius has just said, "Three parts of him / Is ours already, and the man entire / Upon the next encounter yields him ours." Casca explains why Brutus is worth the trouble, and Cassius agrees before they leave, "For it is after midnight, and ere day / We will awake him and be sure of him."

**Simple meaning:** 'He is held in great esteem by all the people, and what would look like a crime if we did it, his approval — like the finest alchemy — will turn into virtue and honour.' And Cassius: 'You have understood him, his value, and how badly we need him, exactly right.'

**Deep meaning:** Alchemy was the supposed art of turning base metal into gold. Casca's simile is brutally honest: the deed is an 'offence' — he uses the word — and Brutus's 'countenance' (his face, his approval, his public support) is wanted to transmute it. This is the clearest statement in the act of what the conspiracy is: a crime in search of a respectable face. 'Conceited' means conceived, understood. Cassius's 'Three parts of him / Is ours already' treats Brutus as a thing to be acquired in fractions — the mirror, the mettle to be wrought, the names, and now the purchase.

**Subtext:** Neither man believes the deed is virtuous in itself; they believe Brutus can make it look so. The irony is that Brutus will believe it is virtuous, and his honest conviction will lead him to the errors — sparing Antony, allowing the funeral speech — that destroy them.

**Character:** Casca: shrewder than his bluntness suggests, and cynical about appearances. Cassius: already counting Brutus as won.

**Theme:** Appearance versus reality; honour as a commodity; public opinion; manipulation.

**Device:** Simile (alchemy); the metal imagery that links 'mettle', 'wrought' and 'alchemy' across the act; metaphor of possession ('ours'); closing rhymeless couplet of resolve.

**Historical connection:** HISTORICAL RECORD — Plutarch states plainly that the conspirators believed Brutus's reputation would make the deed seem honourable, and that without him it would seem merely a plot; Shakespeare gives Plutarch's judgement to Casca as an image. Alchemy was a living Elizabethan pursuit, so the simile would have sounded modern, not quaint.

**Scene connection:** The act ends where Scene 2's soliloquy pointed: at Brutus's door before dawn. Act 2 opens with Brutus awake in his orchard. The alchemy fails in Act 3, when the 'offence' is not transmuted but exposed by Antony, and the 'people's hearts' Casca counts on are won away in an afternoon.

**ICSE keywords:** simile; alchemy; appearance versus reality; reputation; metal imagery; dramatic irony.

**Possible answer use:** 'Casca's hope that Brutus's "countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness" an act he himself calls an "offence" exposes the conspiracy's dependence on appearances, and Antony's oration in Act 3 is the moment the gold turns back to lead.'

## HOW TO EMBED A QUOTATION

::: icse How to embed a quotation so that it earns marks
EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE. Examiners reward quotations that are *used*, not displayed. Three rules, each with a worked example from this bank.

**Rule 1 — Quote short and embed.** A quotation should sit inside your own sentence, grammatically, and be no longer than the point needs. Compare: 'Cassius says: "Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves."' That is four lines of Shakespeare and no marks, because nothing has been argued. Now: 'Cassius makes Caesar's greatness a personal insult, shrinking Brutus to one of the "petty men" who "peep about" beneath a "Colossus".' Three fragments, one sentence, and the analysis is already under way.

**Rule 2 — Never let a quotation speak for itself.** Every quotation must be followed by analysis: what the word choice does, what it reveals, where it leads. The pattern is claim → quotation → comment. Example: 'Caesar's self-image is a role rather than a man (claim); he insists "for always I am Caesar" even as he admits "this ear is deaf" (quotation); the gap between the public name and the failing body in consecutive lines is Shakespeare's compact portrait of a ruler who can no longer hear what he needs to (comment).' A quotation without the comment is a quotation left to do your work for you, and the examiner will not do it either.

**Rule 3 — Show line breaks with a slash.** When you quote more than one line of verse inside your prose, mark the end of each line with a forward slash with a space on either side, and keep the capital letter that begins the new line: "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings." If you set out three or more lines, you may instead write them as separate lines, indented, exactly as printed. Casca's prose needs no slashes — quote it as ordinary sentences: "to my thinking, he would fain have had it".

Three further habits. Name the speaker and the listener the first time ('Cassius tells Brutus that…'), because the same words from a different mouth would mean something else. Put a single word in quotation marks when the word itself is the point — Brutus loves "The name of honour", and the word 'name' is where the analysis lies. And keep your own words in single quotation marks and Shakespeare's in double, so the examiner can see at a glance where the text ends and your thinking begins.

An honest note on marks. In the current ICSE paper the drama extract carries 16 marks in sub-parts, and the higher sub-parts reward the explained link — context to meaning, meaning to theme, theme to the wider play. Act 1 itself is examined in Class IX; in the Class X paper on Acts III–V an embedded Act 1 line is the surest way to show that you understand why a later moment matters. Nothing here guarantees a question; everything here raises the quality of whatever answer you write.
