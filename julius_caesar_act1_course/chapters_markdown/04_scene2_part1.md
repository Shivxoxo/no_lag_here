# Act 1 Scene 2 (Part One) — Lupercalia, the Soothsayer, and the Seduction of Brutus

*This chapter teaches the first half of Act 1 Scene 2, from Caesar's entrance at the feast of Lupercal to the moment Caesar's procession returns. It is the scene in which the play's two great engines start turning: fate speaks (the Soothsayer) and persuasion begins (Cassius working on Brutus). You will first move through the whole scene in five levels, then examine it from twenty angles as a complete dramatic unit, and then study every important passage line by line with the fixed teaching labels. Part Two of this scene (the next chapter) handles Caesar's return, Casca's report of the crown and Cassius's soliloquy, and it carries the revision sheet for the whole scene.*

## 1. Where You Are Standing

In Scene 1 you watched a street. Two tribunes, Flavius and Marullus, drove holiday-making commoners home and decided to strip the decorations from Caesar's statues. Nobody important appeared. You heard Caesar's name shouted, feared, resented — but you did not see the man.

Scene 2 changes the camera angle. Now the great enter: Caesar himself, his wife Calpurnia, Antony dressed to run in the festival race, the senators Cicero and Decius, and the men who will kill Caesar a month later — Brutus, Cassius and Casca. The Folio stage direction (in single quotes because stage directions are not verified dialogue) reads roughly 'Enter Caesar, Antony for the course, Calpurnia, Portia, Decius, Cicero, Brutus, Cassius, Casca, a Soothsayer'. In a single sweep Shakespeare puts almost the whole cast of the tragedy on stage together, arranged around one man. That picture — Rome as a procession revolving around Caesar — is itself the first thing the scene teaches.

The first half of the scene has a very simple shape, and you should hold it in your head before you learn anything else:

```chain
Caesar commands the Lupercal ritual (Antony must touch Calpurnia)
↓
A Soothsayer calls out a warning; Caesar hears it twice and dismisses it
↓
The procession leaves; Brutus and Cassius are left alone
↓
Cassius probes Brutus's mood and offers to be his 'mirror'
↓
A shout offstage: Brutus admits he fears Caesar may be made king
↓
Brutus names his price: honour and the general good
↓
Cassius tells two stories (the Tiber, the fever) to shrink Caesar
↓
A second shout; Cassius attacks Caesar's greatness and the name 'Caesar'
↓
Brutus gives a guarded, dignified reply: he will think, and answer later
↓
Caesar's procession returns (Part Two begins here)
```

## 2. The Five Levels: One Pass Through the Whole First Half

Before the twenty angles and the line-by-line work, take the scene in five passes. Do not skip Levels 1 to 4 to reach the exam material: the exam material only makes sense once the earlier levels are secure.

::: level LEVEL 1 — What happened?
It is the feast of Lupercal, a Roman festival in February. Caesar enters in procession and gives an order: his wife Calpurnia must stand in Antony's path during the ritual race, because tradition holds that a barren (childless) woman touched by a runner in this holy race will be cured. Antony promises to obey. As the procession sets off, a voice cries Caesar's name from the crowd. It is a Soothsayer (a fortune-teller who claims to foresee the future), and his message is five words long: "Beware the ides of March." Caesar has the man brought before him, hears the warning a second time, calls him "a dreamer" and moves on. Everyone follows Caesar to watch the games except two men, Brutus and Cassius. Cassius asks Brutus why he has grown cold and distant. Brutus says he is troubled by private thoughts and is 'at war' with himself. Cassius offers to act as Brutus's mirror and show him his own hidden worth. Shouting is heard from the games; Brutus says he fears the people are choosing Caesar as king, yet admits he loves Caesar. He declares that if the public good demands it he will face death for honour. Cassius then tells two stories: how he once had to rescue Caesar from drowning in the Tiber, and how Caesar shook and groaned like a sick girl during a fever in Spain. A second shout is heard. Cassius delivers his great speech against Caesar's greatness: Caesar straddles the world like a Colossus while other men creep beneath him; the fault lies in themselves for accepting it; the name 'Brutus' is as good as the name 'Caesar'; and there was once another Brutus who would sooner have tolerated the devil in Rome than a king. Brutus replies with careful reserve: he guesses what Cassius is driving at, will think about it and answer another day, but he would rather be a villager than a Roman under the conditions he sees coming. Cassius is satisfied that he has struck a spark. Caesar's procession returns.
:::

::: level LEVEL 2 — What does it mean?
Three meanings sit beneath the events. First, Caesar's opening command shows a ruler who governs even the private and the sacred: he arranges his wife's fertility by public order, and he expects ritual to obey him as men do. Second, the Soothsayer episode shows that the same Caesar who trusts the old fertility superstition will not trust a warning about his own life. He is superstitious where it flatters his hopes and sceptical where it threatens his self-image. Third, and most importantly, the long conversation shows how a conspiracy is born not from a plan but from a mood. Brutus has no plot. He has a disturbance — "passions of some difference" — and a fear that the people may make Caesar king. Cassius's whole task is to translate that private disturbance into political action, and he does it by talking not about Caesar first but about Brutus: about Brutus's face, his hidden worth, his name, his ancestor. The scene means this: tyranny is resisted, in Shakespeare's Rome, not by institutions but by persuading one honourable man that his honour requires it.
:::

::: level LEVEL 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way?
Consider the choices. Shakespeare could have shown the crown being offered to Caesar; instead he sends the crown offstage and keeps the stage for two men talking, with shouts breaking in. That is a decision to make the drama of Rome's future happen inside Brutus's hearing and reaction, not in a spectacle. He could have had Cassius argue politics directly; instead he gives Cassius the image of the mirror, so that the argument for killing Caesar starts as a conversation about self-knowledge. He could have delayed the Soothsayer until the conspiracy existed; instead he places the prophecy before any plot is spoken, so that the audience knows the date of Caesar's death before the killers have agreed to kill. Each choice serves the same purpose: to make the audience feel that the future is already forming in words, looks and silences before anyone draws a dagger. Shakespeare is also using what Plutarch gave him with great freedom — compressing a triumph of October 45 BC and a festival of February 44 BC into one day, inventing the Tiber swimming story (no ancient source records it), and turning Plutarch's anonymous graffiti urging Brutus to act into Cassius's spoken and, later, written persuasion.
:::

::: level LEVEL 4 — How does it connect to the whole play?
Almost every thread of the tragedy is tied here. The Soothsayer's warning fixes the date that gives the play its clock; Caesar will meet the same man again on the morning of the Ides. Brutus's phrase "with himself at war" names the inner division that his orchard soliloquy in Act 2 will dramatise and that his quarrel with Cassius in Act 4 will inherit. His demand that honour and the "general good" justify any action becomes the exact argument of his speech to the people after the assassination, and honour is the word Antony will turn against him in the funeral oration. Cassius's Tiber and fever stories expose a Caesar whose body is weaker than his myth, preparing us for the deaf ear, the falling sickness and the man who fears nothing and is killed. The Colossus image and the line about fates and stars set up the play's argument between free will and destiny, an argument that Cassius himself will lose at Philippi when a misread report drives him to suicide. Even the crown that is offered offstage returns: Antony will remind the crowd in the funeral speech that he offered Caesar a crown three times at the Lupercal.
:::

::: level LEVEL 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer?
Be clear about the syllabus first. Act 1 is the Class IX portion; your Class X board paper examines Acts III to V. So this scene will not appear as a Class X extract. It earns you marks in three other ways. (1) In Class IX internal examinations, this scene is a favourite for extract-based ('reference to the context') questions — the Soothsayer exchange, the mirror passage, the honour speech and the Colossus speech are natural extracts. (2) In the Class X paper, questions on the Act 3 to 5 extracts constantly reach back: 'Why does Brutus join the conspiracy?', 'What had Cassius earlier told Brutus about Caesar?', 'What is the significance of the ides of March?' The 2025 paper, for instance, asked what the phrase 'the work the ides of March begun' refers to — unanswerable without this scene. (3) Character and theme questions on Brutus, Cassius and Caesar need the evidence planted here: Brutus's conflict of love and principle, Cassius's method of flattery and belittlement, Caesar's blend of superstition and pride. When you write, quote short, exact phrases — "He is a dreamer", "with himself at war", "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars" — and always attach a point of analysis to the quotation, never the quotation alone.
:::

## 3. The Scene as a Complete Dramatic Unit: Twenty Angles

A scene is not a list of events. It is a designed unit with a setting, a set of wants, a change, and a set of consequences. The twenty headings below are the questions a professional reader asks of any scene. Learn to ask them yourself; they will serve you for every scene in the play. (These twenty cover the whole of Scene 2, including the half taught in Part Two, because the scene must be understood as one unit.)

### 1 Setting

The scene is set in a public place in Rome on the feast of Lupercal. Nothing in the dialogue names a street or square precisely; Shakespeare's stage was bare, and the setting is created by what people say and do. Three things build it. First, ceremony: a procession, trumpets (a 'flourish' is a fanfare; a 'sennet' is a longer ceremonial trumpet signal marking a formal entrance or exit), a ritual race about to begin. Second, a crowd: Caesar speaks of "the press" (the crush of people), Casca shouts for silence, and the shouts that later interrupt Brutus and Cassius come from a mass of people offstage. Third, a calendar: it is the Lupercal, and a voice names another date, the ides of March. The setting is therefore public, crowded and festive — exactly the kind of place where a private conversation stands out. Shakespeare then empties the stage of everyone but two men, so that the setting flips from ceremony to intimacy without a change of place. Historically the Lupercalia was celebrated on 15 February; Shakespeare has moved Caesar's triumph, which took place in October of the previous year, onto the same day, so that the crowd of Scene 1 and the festival of Scene 2 belong to one continuous morning.

### 2 Characters

Caesar dominates without saying much: he speaks in short commands and refers to himself in the third person. Calpurnia speaks three words ("Here, my lord") and is otherwise a body to be touched and, later, a pale cheek to be read. Antony is defined by one promise of instant obedience. Casca appears first as a crowd-controller ("Peace, ho! Caesar speaks."), later as the sour, prose-speaking reporter. The Soothsayer is a nameless voice with a single sentence. Brutus is reserved, courteous and inwardly troubled. Cassius is watchful, eloquent and relentless. Cicero, Decius and Portia enter with the procession but do not speak in this half; Portia's presence is only in the entry direction. Marullus and Flavius, in the Folger text, follow the procession — a silent reminder of Scene 1 whose meaning becomes clear when Casca reports that they have been silenced. The design is deliberate: the one man with the most power says the least that is thoughtful, and the two men who say the most are already, without knowing it, describing the shape of his death.

### 3 Political situation

Rome is a republic in name and Caesar's in fact. He has defeated Pompey and Pompey's sons in a civil war and holds the dictatorship — a Roman emergency office, traditionally limited to six months, which Caesar had stretched into a permanent post. The republican institutions still exist: consuls, senators, tribunes of the people, praetors (Brutus and Cassius both held praetorships in 44 BC). But the question hanging over the city is whether Caesar will take the one title Romans had hated since they expelled their last king in 509 BC: 'rex', king. Scene 1 showed the tribunes trying to pull down Caesar's decorations; Scene 2 shows the aristocrats who fear the same thing but are far more dangerous because they have swords and standing. Brutus's fear that the people may "Choose Caesar for their king" is the political centre of the scene. The crown that Antony offers offstage is, in reported form, the test of whether the people want a king, and the reported answer (they cheer the refusal) is unstable and ambiguous. Nobody in the scene knows what Rome wants; that uncertainty is the political situation.

### 4 What happens

Stripped to actions: Caesar orders Antony to touch Calpurnia in the race; the Soothsayer warns Caesar twice and is dismissed; the procession exits; Cassius questions Brutus's coldness; Brutus confesses inner conflict; Cassius offers to be Brutus's mirror; a shout interrupts; Brutus admits his fear about the kingship and his love for Caesar; Brutus states the terms on which he will listen (honour, the general good); Cassius tells the Tiber and fever anecdotes; a second shout; Cassius attacks Caesar's greatness and the name 'Caesar' and invokes the ancestral Brutus; Brutus promises to consider and answer later; Caesar returns, angry, and judges Cassius dangerous; Casca reports the three offers of the crown, Caesar's swoon and the silencing of the tribunes; Casca agrees to dine with Cassius; Brutus and Cassius part; Cassius, alone, resolves to forge letters. Notice the rhythm: two intrusions of sound (the shouts), each of which pushes the conversation one step further towards treason.

### 5 Why it happens

Every action has a cause you can name. Caesar orders the touch because he wants an heir and believes the ritual works. The Soothsayer warns because, in the play's world, the future can be read and someone reads it. Caesar dismisses him because a man who calls himself Caesar cannot be seen to flinch at a stranger's words in public. Brutus stays behind because he is "not gamesome" and in no mood for a festival; Cassius stays because he wants Brutus alone. Cassius probes because he has a plan that cannot succeed without Brutus's reputation — the conspirators themselves later say that Brutus's good name will turn what would look like crime into virtue. Brutus opens up because Cassius accuses him of coldness to a friend, and Brutus's sense of courtesy will not let that stand. The shouts happen because the crowd is reacting to the offered crown, and they push Brutus to speak his fear aloud. Cassius tells the anecdotes because Brutus has just said that he loves Caesar; the stories are designed to make Caesar less lovable and less godlike. Brutus stalls because he is honourable and slow, not because he disagrees. Cassius plots the letters because, as he says in his soliloquy, he has seen that Brutus's honourable "mettle may be wrought" — that a noble temperament can be worked upon.

### 6 What each character wants

Caesar wants an heir, a smooth ceremony and unbroken authority; when the Soothsayer intrudes he wants, above all, to appear unafraid. Calpurnia, so far as the text lets us see, wants to obey; the scene gives her no wish of her own, and that emptiness is itself expressive. Antony wants to please Caesar and to run his race. The Soothsayer wants Caesar to listen. Brutus wants to be left alone with his thoughts and, more deeply, wants to be a man of honour who never has to choose between friendship and Rome. Cassius wants Brutus in the conspiracy, and beneath that he wants an end to living in awe of a man he considers no better than himself: "I had as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself." Casca wants to be seen as unimpressed by everything. The scene's tension comes from the gap between what Brutus wants (to avoid choosing) and what Cassius wants (to force the choice).

### 7 What changes during the scene

At the start, Brutus's trouble is private and formless: "passions of some difference", "Conceptions only proper to myself". By the end of the first half he has spoken his fear aloud to another man, admitted he would not have Caesar king, named honour as a value he would die for, and promised to hear Cassius out. That is a large change, even though he agrees to nothing. Cassius changes from tentative to confident: he begins by complaining of Brutus's coldness and ends by declaring himself glad to have struck fire. Caesar changes from the serene master of ceremony to the man who returns with "The angry spot" glowing on his brow, and Casca's report reveals a Caesar who fainted in public. The crowd's mood, reported rather than seen, has shifted three times with three offers of the crown. Most of all, the audience's relation to the play changes: at the start we watch a public festival; by the end we are inside a secret.

### 8 What the audience learns

The audience learns the date of Caesar's death; that Caesar is superstitious about fertility but dismissive of prophecy; that Caesar and Calpurnia have no child; that Brutus is troubled and fears Caesar's kingship while loving the man; that Cassius has a hidden purpose and considerable skill; that Caesar's body has failed him at least twice (the Tiber, the fever), and, from Casca, a third time (the swoon); that a crown was offered and refused three times and that Casca believed Caesar wanted it; that the tribunes have been silenced; and that Cassius will forge letters. We also learn the play's method: important events (the crown offering, the tribunes' punishment) reach us through reports by interested witnesses, so we must learn to weigh the speaker as well as the speech.

### 9 What Shakespeare deliberately withholds

He withholds the crown scene itself — we hear cheering and later get Casca's cynical version, never the thing. He withholds Caesar's inner thoughts: there is no soliloquy for Caesar in this scene or anywhere in the play, so his mind is always deduced from his public voice. He withholds the content of Brutus's "passions of some difference": Brutus never tells Cassius what he has been brooding on, and the audience must wait until the orchard in Act 2 to hear him reason it out. He withholds any explicit proposal of murder; Cassius never says the word, and the nearest he comes is the ancestral Brutus who would not have "brooked" a king. He withholds Cicero's Greek remark, which even Casca did not understand. And he withholds any reply from the Soothsayer to Caesar's dismissal; the man simply vanishes into the crowd, which makes the warning feel less like a character's opinion and more like the play's own voice.

### 10 Important language

Notice five kinds of language working at once. Language of command: Caesar's clipped imperatives — "Set on", "Set him before me", "Let us leave him. Pass" — and his habit of naming himself in the third person, as though 'Caesar' were an office rather than a man. Language of sight: eyes, faces, mirrors, reflections, shadows, looks, "the bend" of Caesar's eye; the whole Brutus–Cassius conversation is conducted through metaphors of seeing. Language of the body: barren wombs, a shaking fever, coward lips losing colour, a groan, a swoon, foaming at the mouth — Caesar's greatness is repeatedly measured against his flesh. Language of weighing and measuring: names written, sounded and weighed; Caesar's stature as a "Colossus"; petty men beneath huge legs. Language of honour and its opposites: "honour" is repeated by Brutus and immediately picked up by Cassius as "the subject of my story", while "dishonourable graves" is the fate Cassius holds up as the alternative. Learn to name these families of language; an examiner is far more impressed by 'Cassius conducts the argument through imagery of sight' than by 'Cassius uses good language'.

### 11 Important images

An image is a picture made in words. The scene's central image is the **mirror**: the eye "sees not itself / But by reflection", Brutus lacks "such mirrors" as would show him his worth, and Cassius appoints himself "your glass". Around it cluster related images of **eyes and shadows**. The second great image is the **Colossus**: Caesar as the giant statue straddling the world, with "petty men" creeping between his legs looking for graves. Third, the **river**: the "troubled Tiber chafing with her shores", the "angry flood", the "torrent" — nature as a test of manhood that Caesar failed. Fourth, **fire from flint**: Cassius's "weak words" have "struck but thus much show of fire" — persuasion as striking a spark. Fifth, **disease and the failing body**: the fever, shaking, pale lips, groans, and the "sick girl". Sixth, **the yoke**: Rome "groaning underneath this age's yoke", the image of an ox harnessed to a burden, which turns political submission into a physical weight. Seventh, the **conjured spirit**: 'Brutus' will "start a spirit" as readily as 'Caesar' — a picture of names as magic words that raise ghosts, chillingly fulfilled when Caesar's ghost visits Brutus in Act 4.

### 12 Literary devices

Only name a device where it is genuinely present. In this half you will find: **dramatic irony** (the audience knows what the ides of March will bring; Caesar does not); **foreshadowing** (the Soothsayer; "death i' th' other"); **metaphor** (the glass, the yoke, the palm of victory, chewing on a thought); **simile** (Caesar "Like a Colossus"; groaning "As a sick girl"); **personification** (the Tiber "chafing", the "angry flood", Age and Rome addressed as persons); **apostrophe**, the device of addressing an absent thing directly ("Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!"); **classical allusion** (Aeneas and Anchises; the Colossus of Rhodes; "the great flood"; the ancestral Brutus); **hyperbole** (Caesar's eye "whose bend doth awe the world"); **antithesis**, the balancing of opposites ("honour in one eye and death i' th' other"; "villager" against "son of Rome"); **rhetorical question** ("Why should that name be sounded more than yours?"; "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed"); **parallelism** in the sequence of imperatives ("Write them together… Sound them… Weigh them… conjure with 'em"); **pun** (Rome and "room enough", the two words pronounced alike on the Elizabethan stage); **aphorism**, a compact general truth ("Men at some time are masters of their fates"); **irony** in Cassius's mouth ("this god did shake"; "immortal Caesar"); and **anachronism**, an object out of its historical time ("Th' eternal devil", a Christian idea placed in pagan Rome). The chapter 'The Act 1 Literary-Device Bank' gathers these with definitions and further examples.

### 13 Themes

Six themes surface in the first half. **Fate and free will**: a prophecy is delivered and rejected, and Cassius insists that "The fault… is not in our stars, / But in ourselves". The scene puts the two positions side by side without settling them. **Public image versus private reality**: Caesar's godlike public self against the shaking, groaning, drowning man of Cassius's stories; Brutus's cold face against his inner war. **Honour**: Brutus's absolute value, immediately made "the subject" of Cassius's story — the theme and the manipulation begin together. **Friendship and its use**: Cassius approaches Brutus as a wounded friend, and the language of love ("your friend that loves you", "yet I love him well") frames every political move. **Republicanism and the fear of kingship**: "Choose Caesar for their king" is the horror that unites Brutus and Cassius; the ancestral Brutus is invoked as the founder of Roman liberty. **Superstition and omens**: the fertility ritual, the Soothsayer, and the question of who believes what. The two 'Themes of Act 1' chapters develop each of these across the whole act.

### 14 Historical context

Behind the scene stands Plutarch's Life of Caesar, in Sir Thomas North's English translation of 1579. Plutarch records the Lupercalia of 44 BC: young noblemen and magistrates ran through the city striking bystanders with strips of goat-hide, and women placed themselves in their way in the belief that the touch aided fertility and childbirth; Antony, who was consul that year, ran as one of the priests and offered Caesar a diadem, which the crowd applauded feebly and whose refusal they applauded loudly. Plutarch and Suetonius both record a seer who warned Caesar of danger that would come no later than the ides of March; Suetonius names him Spurinna. Shakespeare keeps the warning and makes the seer nameless. Plutarch also records Caesar's epileptic seizures (the "falling sickness" of Casca's report), and that they were said to have first troubled him in Spain — which may lie behind Cassius's "fever when he was in Spain", though the swimming contest in the Tiber has no ancient source and is generally treated as Shakespeare's invention. Caesar and Calpurnia were indeed childless; Caesar's only legitimate child, Julia, had died in 54 BC. Marcus Brutus really did claim descent from Lucius Junius Brutus, who expelled the last king of Rome in 509 BC, and anonymous messages really were left for him urging him to act, which Shakespeare turns into Cassius's forged letters. Shakespeare compresses time (triumph and Lupercalia in one day) and, in Casca's report, increases Plutarch's two offers of the diadem to three.

### 15 Foreshadowing

The scene is thick with it. "Beware the ides of March" fixes the day. Brutus's "with himself at war" foreshadows both his internal struggle in the orchard and the literal civil war of Acts 4 and 5. "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other" foreshadows the choice he will make on the Ides and the death he will choose at Philippi. Cassius's "dishonourable graves" foreshadows the graves the conspirators will actually find. "Men at some time are masters of their fates" will be tested and, for Cassius, contradicted: he dies because he misreads a report and acts as if fate had already decided. "'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar'" is fulfilled literally when Caesar's spirit appears to Brutus. Cassius's stories of Caesar's weak body prepare the deaf ear, the swoon and the "falling sickness" of Part Two, and the mortal body that twenty-three wounds will open. Even Caesar's "leave no ceremony out" hints at a man for whom ceremony is a shield, a hint Calpurnia's dream and Decius's flattery will exploit in Act 2.

### 16 Dramatic irony

Dramatic irony exists when the audience knows something a character does not. Shakespeare's first audiences, like you, knew that Caesar was murdered on the ides of March; so every line about that date carries a charge the speakers cannot feel. Caesar's "He is a dreamer" is the scene's sharpest case. There is a further, subtler irony: the man who repeats the warning to Caesar is Brutus — "A soothsayer bids you beware the ides of March" — so that Brutus, the eventual killer, voices the prophecy of his own deed. When Cassius calls Caesar "immortal", he means to mock, but the audience hears a double edge: mortal on the Ides, immortal in reputation for two thousand years. When Cassius says "then hold me dangerous", he means it as a denial, and the audience knows he is precisely the dangerous man Caesar will name in Part Two. And when Brutus fears that "the people / Choose Caesar for their king", the audience will shortly learn from Casca that the people cheered Caesar's refusal — Brutus's fear runs ahead of the facts.

### 17 Character development

Brutus develops from silence to speech: from a man who will not explain his mood to a man who has named his fear, his love and his principle. He does not decide anything, and his refusal to be "any further moved" for now shows the deliberate, procedural mind that will later insist on doing the murder as a formal sacrifice. Cassius develops from friend to recruiter: watch how his language moves from love to honour to contempt to ancestry. Caesar develops in a different way — not by changing but by being revealed. In the first fourteen lines he is all authority; in the Soothsayer exchange he shows curiosity and then dismissal; in Cassius's stories he becomes a man who nearly drowned and cried out for help; when he returns he is angry; in Casca's report he has fainted. The scene does not change Caesar; it peels him. Antony, in one line of obedience, is established as the loyal instrument, which makes his later independence in Act 3 a genuine development rather than a surprise.

### 18 Scene connections

Backwards: Scene 1's tribunes reappear silently in the procession and are reported "put to silence"; the crowd that Marullus scolded is the same crowd that shouts three times offstage; Flavius's fear of Caesar's "servile fearfulness" is Cassius's "In awe of such a thing as I myself" spoken by a nobleman. Forwards within Act 1: Cassius's soliloquy at the end of the scene plans the letters that he hands to Cinna in Scene 3; Casca, sounded out here over dinner, is won over in the storm; Cicero's silent presence here becomes his sceptical voice in Scene 3. Into Act 2: Brutus's inner war becomes the orchard soliloquy; the forged letters arrive; Calpurnia's silent obedience becomes her desperate pleading; Caesar's dismissal of the Soothsayer becomes his dismissal of Calpurnia's dream. Into Act 3: the Soothsayer meets Caesar again on the way to the Capitol; Antony's funeral speech reminds the crowd of the crown offered at the Lupercal; Brutus justifies the killing by honour and the general good. Into Acts 4 and 5: the ghost, the quarrel between the two men who whisper here, and their deaths. The chapter 'The Scene Connection Engine and Where Act 1 Reappears Later' maps these in detail.

### 19 Future consequences

Because of what happens here: Brutus will receive letters he believes come from Roman citizens and will treat them as Rome's voice; Cassius will be able to bring him into the conspiracy at their next meeting, as Cassius predicts in Scene 3; Caesar, having dismissed the Soothsayer, will establish a habit of dismissing warnings that makes his walk to the Capitol possible; the crowd's fickleness, reported by Casca, will be exploited first by Brutus and then, far better, by Antony; and Brutus's insistence on honour will shape the conspiracy's mistakes — sparing Antony, letting Antony speak, refusing to take money by dishonourable means before Philippi. In a sense, the tragedy is what follows when a man who says he loves "The name of honour more than I fear death" is taken at his word.

### 20 ICSE exam relevance

**EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE**, stated honestly. Act 1 is prescribed for Class IX, and the Class X board paper draws its drama extracts from Acts III, IV and V. This scene will therefore feed your Class X marks indirectly but constantly. When an Act 3 extract asks why Brutus joined the conspiracy, the evidence is here: his fear of kingship, his love for Caesar, his standard of honour and the general good, and Cassius's work on his vanity. When an Antony extract from the funeral scene refers to the crown offered three times at the Lupercal, the examiner expects you to know this is the offstage event of Scene 2. When a Caesar extract from Act 3 mentions the ides of March, you must know who first spoke of them and how Caesar responded. When a question asks what Cassius had said about Caesar's weaknesses, the Tiber and the fever are your answer. For Class IX internal examinations, this scene supplies classic extracts, and the sub-question types you will meet mirror the board pattern: context (who speaks, to whom, what has just happened), explanation of a phrase, reasons for a speaker's words, what a passage reveals of character, what follows, and a short evaluative response. The 2025 board paper's drama question carried sixteen marks split [3], [3], [3], [3], [4]; practise answering Act 1 extracts to that shape, because the skills are identical.

::: keyword Keyword box: terms this scene teaches you to use
**Dramatic irony** — the audience knows what the character does not. **Foreshadowing** — an early hint of later events. **Soliloquy** — a speech by a character alone on stage (Cassius has one at the end of this scene). **Hubris** — excessive pride leading to downfall (use with care: Caesar's "He is a dreamer" is evidence, not proof). **Superstition** — belief in supernatural causes; Caesar's fertility ritual. **Internal conflict** — Brutus "with himself at war". **Manipulation** — steering another person by indirect means; Cassius's method. **Flattery** — praise used as a tool; the mirror. **Republicanism** — the political belief in rule without a king. **Illeism** — speaking of oneself in the third person; Caesar's "Caesar is turned to hear" and Brutus's "poor Brutus". **Anecdote** — a short story told to make a point; the Tiber and the fever. **Allusion** — a reference to a well-known person, story or object; Aeneas, the Colossus. **Antithesis** — balanced opposites. **Apostrophe** — addressing an absent thing as if present. ## 4. Caesar at the Lupercal: Ritual, Wife, Prophecy

### Lupercalia as staged by Shakespeare

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The Lupercalia was one of Rome's oldest festivals, held on 15 February. It was a rite of purification and fertility. Its priests, the Luperci, ran through the city striking the people they met, especially women, with thongs of goat-hide; the touch was believed to promote fertility and easy childbirth. In 44 BC a third college of Luperci, the Juliani, had been created in Caesar's honour, and Antony — consul that year, a man of about forty — ran as one of them. Marullus's protest in Scene 1, "You know it is the feast of Lupercal", tells us the tribunes know they are defacing images on a holy day.

**SHAKESPEARE'S TEXT.** Shakespeare stages almost none of this. There are no goat-hide thongs, no half-naked runners, no crowd being struck. What he keeps is a single detail — the touch that cures barrenness — and he hands it to Caesar as a command. The festival becomes visible only through Caesar's order to Antony, Antony's promise, and Cassius's later invitation to "go see the order of the course" (the proceedings of the race). Everything else happens offstage, where Antony will also offer the crown.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** Why so little? Three answers reinforce each other. First, economy: an Elizabethan company could not stage a city festival, and Shakespeare turns the limitation into a method, letting sound (trumpets, shouts) stand for spectacle. Second, focus: by reducing the Lupercal to one command, Shakespeare makes the festival express Caesar rather than Rome — it becomes the setting in which Caesar's private hope for an heir is spoken as public policy. Third, irony: a fertility festival frames a scene about a childless ruler and a state that cannot reproduce its own liberty; Cassius will soon say that Rome "hast lost the breed of noble bloods". The festival's theme of fruitfulness runs against the grain of everything that follows.

::: history The Lupercal and the crown: what Plutarch says and what Shakespeare changes
Plutarch's Life of Caesar describes the Lupercalia of 44 BC as the occasion when Antony, running as a Lupercus, brought Caesar a diadem wrapped in laurel. The crowd applauded thinly at the offer and loudly when Caesar pushed it away; the offer was repeated, and Caesar finally sent the diadem to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitol. Plutarch describes two offers; Shakespeare's Casca will say three. Historically Caesar's Spanish triumph (October 45 BC) and the Lupercalia (15 February 44 BC) were four months apart, and the episode of the tribunes removing a diadem from Caesar's statue came before the Lupercalia; Shakespeare folds all three into one morning. He is not careless. He wants the audience to see, in a single scene, Caesar celebrated, Caesar tempted by a crown, and Caesar's critics punished — the whole political weather of Rome in one day.
:::

### Antony's race and Caesar's command to touch Calpurnia

The scene opens with a name called twice. That in itself is a lesson in staging: the first word spoken by the most powerful man in the world is his wife's name, and Casca must silence the crowd so that she can hear it.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Caesar:** Stand you directly in Antonius' way / When he doth run his course.

> **Caesar:** Forget not in your speed, Antonius, / To touch Calpurnia, for our elders say / The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse.

> **Antony:** I shall remember. / When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed.

> **Caesar:** Set on and leave no ceremony out.

**Simple English:** Calpurnia, stand right in Antony's path when he runs the race. Antony, as you run, do not forget to touch Calpurnia: the old people say that a childless woman touched during this sacred race is freed from the curse of childlessness. Antony: I will remember; whatever Caesar orders is done. Caesar: Begin, and do not leave out any part of the ritual.

**Difficult words:** 'directly' — straight, exactly in the path; 'course' — the race run by the Luperci; 'speed' — haste, running; 'our elders' — the old, the ancestors, tradition; 'barren' — unable to have children; 'touchèd' (two syllables, for the verse rhythm) — touched; 'holy chase' — the sacred race; 'sterile' — infertile; 'curse' — infertility understood as a supernatural affliction; 'Set on' — proceed, go forward; 'ceremony' — ritual observance.

**Literal meaning:** Caesar arranges for his wife to be touched in the ritual so that she may conceive. Antony agrees. Caesar orders the procession to begin with every rite observed.

**Subtext:** Caesar wants an heir, and he wants it enough to say so in front of Rome. The politeness of "our elders say" places responsibility for the belief on tradition rather than on himself, but the order is his. Antony's reply is not just agreement; it is a public advertisement of Caesar's absolute authority, spoken by the consul of Rome as though he were a servant.

**Hidden meaning:** A ruler who can command a fertility miracle by scheduling it is a ruler who believes the world answers to his voice. "Leave no ceremony out" reveals a mind that treats ritual as insurance: perform every part, and the gods are bound. The same mind will refuse the Soothsayer moments later, because a prophecy of death is a ceremony Caesar did not order.

**Character psychology:** Caesar is anxious beneath the calm. Childlessness in a dynasty-builder is a private grief and a political vulnerability, and his instinct is to master it by public procedure. He speaks of himself in the third person through Antony's mouth ("When Caesar says"), and the habit tells us that 'Caesar' has become for him a public institution rather than a person. Antony's psychology is simpler and telling: he is the willing arm.

**Political meaning:** Rome watches its dictator try to secure a succession. To a republican ear, an heir is exactly what a republic must not have; magistrates are elected, not born. Caesar's wish for a child is therefore heard, by men like Cassius, as the wish for a dynasty. Antony's line, meanwhile, announces that the consulship has become an instrument of one man's will.

**Historical context:** Caesar had married Calpurnia in 59 BC; the marriage was childless, and Caesar's only legitimate child, Julia, had died in 54 BC. He would in fact name his great-nephew Octavian as heir in his will. Caesar was also pontifex maximus, Rome's chief priest, so his insistence on complete ceremony is in character for the historical man. Elizabethan audiences may have felt an echo of their own situation: in 1599 Elizabeth I was sixty-six, unmarried and without a named successor, and succession anxiety was a live public fear. Do not overstate this; it is context, not a code.

**Literary devices:** Metaphor in "sterile curse" (infertility as a curse to be shaken off, like a garment or a burden); the euphemistic 'holy chase' for a festival of running and striking; Caesar's illeism (third-person self-reference) relayed by Antony; the clipped imperative "Set on", which is characteristic of Caesar's command style throughout the play.

**Effect:** In fourteen short lines Shakespeare establishes hierarchy (everyone falls silent, Antony obeys), intimacy (a husband's hope), and unease (the ruler is not complete). The audience sees a man who governs even the womb.

**Dramatic significance:** This is Caesar's first appearance, and first appearances define. He is shown commanding, superstitious and childless. Each of the three will matter: command becomes rigidity, superstition becomes selective, childlessness becomes the question of who inherits Rome — a question that Antony and Octavian will answer with civil war.

**Foreshadowing:** Antony's promise of instant obedience foreshadows his role as Caesar's loyal executor and avenger; his 'performing' of Caesar's will continues after Caesar's death. "Leave no ceremony out" foreshadows Caesar's dependence on ritual and omen in Act 2, where Calpurnia's dream and the priests' sacrifice briefly stop him from going to the Senate.

**Scene connection:** Marullus's "feast of Lupercal" in Scene 1 is now on stage. The race Antony runs is the occasion of the crown offering that Casca will report in Part Two; the Lupercal will be named again in Antony's funeral speech in Act 3.

**ICSE keywords:** superstition, dynastic anxiety, authority, obedience, illeism, public versus private, foreshadowing.

**Exam application:** Asked what Caesar's opening words reveal about him, you could write: 'Caesar's first command — that Antony should "touch Calpurnia" so that she may "Shake off their sterile curse" — reveals a superstitious ruler who treats even his wife's fertility as a matter of public ceremony, and it quietly exposes his greatest weakness: he has no heir.'

### Calpurnia

Calpurnia speaks three words in this scene, "Here, my lord", and then falls silent. Later, when Caesar returns, Brutus will read her face — her "cheek is pale" — as a sign that something has gone wrong. That is the whole of her presence in Act 1: a summons, an answer, and a complexion to be interpreted by men.

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** It is tempting to say Shakespeare neglects her. Look more carefully. He gives her an entrance in the procession beside the greatest man in Rome; he has that man's first word be her name; he has the crowd hushed for her; and then he has her body made the object of an order. The silence is not neglect but portraiture. Calpurnia is what Caesar's Rome does to the private life: it turns it into ceremony. Her three words of obedience are exactly matched by Antony's line of obedience that follows — wife and consul answer the same voice in the same way. Only in Act 2 will she speak at length, when she begs Caesar not to go to the Senate, and the contrast will be painful: the woman who said "Here, my lord" in public pleads in private and is overruled.

Historically (HISTORICAL RECORD) Calpurnia was the daughter of Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, married Caesar in 59 BC, and was remembered by Plutarch for the ominous dream she had on the night before the Ides. Shakespeare makes that dream the centre of her Act 2 scene. Here he plants only the seed: she is the wife who cannot give Caesar what he wants, in a scene where everyone else gives him everything.

::: textnote The spelling of her name
The Folger text, following the First Folio, prints 'Calphurnia'. The MIT/Globe text and most ICSE editions print 'Calpurnia', which is the historical Roman spelling. This book uses 'Calpurnia' throughout; the two spellings are the same character and either is acceptable in an answer, provided you are consistent.
:::

### Caesar's superstition

You must hold two facts together, because examiners love the tension between them. Caesar believes the fertility ritual will work and orders his men to "leave no ceremony out"; moments later he hears a prophecy of his own danger and calls the prophet "a dreamer". Is he superstitious or sceptical?

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** The answer is that Caesar's superstition is selective, and the selection is governed by his self-image. A ritual that promises to give him an heir enlarges Caesar; he embraces it. A warning that he may be struck down diminishes Caesar; he rejects it. He is not choosing between belief and disbelief. He is choosing, in each case, the reading that makes him greater. This is the pattern Act 2 will confirm: Calpurnia's dream and the priests' warning briefly frighten him, but as soon as Decius reinterprets the dream as a vision of Caesar's glory he goes to the Capitol. What he believes is whatever keeps Caesar 'Caesar'.

**HISTORICAL RECORD.** The ancient biographers give some support for this portrait. Suetonius presents Caesar as a man who was not deterred from his undertakings by religious scruple and who bent unfavourable omens to his own purposes; yet the same tradition records that he took the seer's warning seriously enough to joke about it on the fatal morning. Shakespeare seems to have read this mixture accurately.

::: insight Why the Soothsayer appears so early
A school guide will tell you the Soothsayer 'creates suspense'. Ask the better question: why here, at the very start of the second scene, before a single word of conspiracy has been spoken? Consider what the placement does. Cassius has not yet approached Brutus; no plot exists on stage. Yet the date of Caesar's death is already announced. Shakespeare has therefore made fate arrive before free will. When Cassius says a few minutes later that men "are masters of their fates", the audience has already heard the calendar of a murder that no one has yet planned. The prophecy hangs over the persuasion scene and quietly asks: is Cassius the author of these events or their instrument? Second, the placement establishes Caesar's method of hearing before we see anything else of his mind. "Caesar is turned to hear" — he turns, he looks, he asks to hear it again; and then "He is a dreamer". The pattern of attention followed by dismissal is set in five lines, and Act 2 will repeat it at length. Third, notice who relays the message: Brutus. The man who will strike is the man who, unprompted, repeats the warning of his own future deed. The Soothsayer speaks to Caesar, but the play speaks through Brutus. A prophecy that early, that short, delivered through that mouth, is not suspense. It is structure.
:::

### The Soothsayer and "Beware the ides of March"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Caesar:** Who is it in the press that calls on me? / I hear a tongue shriller than all the music / Cry 'Caesar.' Speak. Caesar is turned to hear.

> **Soothsayer:** Beware the ides of March.

> **Caesar:** What man is that?

> **Brutus:** A soothsayer bids you beware the ides of March.

> **Caesar:** Set him before me. Let me see his face.

> **Caesar:** What sayst thou to me now? Speak once again.

> **Soothsayer:** Beware the ides of March.

> **Caesar:** He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass.

**Simple English:** Who in this crowd is calling me? I can hear one voice, higher and sharper than all the music, shouting 'Caesar'. Speak: Caesar has turned round to listen. Soothsayer: Be on your guard on the fifteenth of March. Caesar: Who is that man? Brutus: A fortune-teller is telling you to be careful on the ides of March. Caesar: Bring him in front of me; I want to see his face. What are you saying to me now? Say it once more. Soothsayer: Be on your guard on the fifteenth of March. Caesar: He is a man who imagines things. Let us leave him. Move on.

**Difficult words:** 'press' — the crowd, the crush of people; 'shriller' — higher-pitched and more piercing; 'soothsayer' — literally a 'truth-sayer', one who foretells the future; 'ides' — in the Roman calendar the middle marker-day of the month, the fifteenth in March; 'Beware' — be on guard against; 'sayst' — sayest, do you say; 'dreamer' — one who sees visions, a fantasist; 'Pass' — move on, proceed.

**Literal meaning:** Caesar hears a single voice above the ceremonial music and asks who it is. The Soothsayer warns him to be careful on 15 March. Caesar has him brought forward, looks at his face, hears the warning repeated and dismisses him as a fantasist.

**Subtext:** Caesar's curiosity is real — he stops a procession for a voice — but it is the curiosity of a man who expects to be able to judge truth by looking at a face. Having looked, he decides. The dismissal is not argued; it is pronounced. Underneath lies a need to be seen not to flinch: a public warning must be publicly waved away, or Caesar looks afraid.

**Hidden meaning:** The word "shriller" is telling. Caesar hears the Soothsayer as noise that rises above harmony, an unpleasant interruption of the music of his own celebration. He classifies the warning by its sound before he weighs its sense. "Let me see his face" reveals Caesar's theory of knowledge: character and truth can be read from appearance. He will apply the same theory, with more accuracy, to Cassius's "lean and hungry look" in Part Two — and it will fail him with Decius and Brutus.

**Character psychology:** Caesar is confident, curious and closed. He gives the man two chances to speak, which shows that he is not simply arrogant; he then decides against him in a single line, which shows that his openness has a very short life. The third-person "Caesar is turned to hear" shows a man performing himself. The Soothsayer's psychology is a blank, and that blankness is the point: he has no motive, no interest, no fear, only the sentence.

**Political meaning:** A ruler who cannot receive unwelcome information is a ruler whose advisers will learn to bring only welcome information. The scene stages, in miniature, why courts fail. It is also a moment where the crowd, so easily swayed in Scene 1, contains one voice that will not be swayed, and Caesar cannot tolerate it.

**Historical context:** Plutarch and Suetonius record that a seer (Suetonius names Spurinna) had warned Caesar to beware of danger coming no later than the ides of March, and that on the fatal morning Caesar mocked him: the Ides had come and nothing had happened, to which the seer replied that they had come but not yet gone. Shakespeare will dramatise that later exchange in Act 3; here he gives the warning without a name, which turns a historical individual into something closer to the voice of fate.

**Literary devices:** Dramatic irony (the audience knows what the Ides will bring); foreshadowing in its purest form — a prophecy; illeism ("Caesar is turned to hear"); the auditory image of a "tongue shriller than all the music"; repetition of the warning, which gives it the weight of an incantation; the curt monosyllable "Pass" as a verbal gesture of dismissal.

**Effect:** The festive music is pierced by a single sentence, repeated. The audience feels the calendar of the play start ticking. Caesar's dismissal produces the particular unease of watching a man walk past a sign he has read aloud.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the play's first prophecy and Caesar's first refusal of a warning; the two together define the tragic pattern of the first three acts. It also introduces the Soothsayer as a recurring figure who will meet Caesar again on the morning of the Ides and speak briefly with Portia in Act 2.

**Foreshadowing:** The date of the assassination is named. Caesar's habit of dismissing warnings is established and will govern his response to Calpurnia, the augurers and Artemidorus. The Soothsayer's own reappearance is prepared.

**Scene connection:** Brutus's relaying of the warning links him, ironically, to Caesar's death before Cassius has said a word. Caesar's face-reading here connects to his judgement of Cassius when he returns; his "dreamer" connects to Cicero's remark in Scene 3 that "men may construe things after their fashion" — Caesar construes the Soothsayer as a dreamer because that reading suits him.

**ICSE keywords:** dramatic irony, foreshadowing, omen, prophecy, hubris (with care), dismissal, fate versus free will, illeism.

**Exam application:** Asked how Caesar's reaction to the Soothsayer reveals his character, write: 'Caesar hears the warning twice, even calling "Let me see his face", yet dismisses the man as "a dreamer" — showing a ruler curious enough to listen but too proud to be seen taking a stranger's warning seriously in public. The audience, knowing what the ides of March will bring, hears dramatic irony in every word.'

### Caesar's public image versus his private characteristics as seen so far

Draw up two columns in your mind. In the public column: a man whose name silences a crowd; who speaks in imperatives; whose consul says that whatever "Caesar says" is "performed"; who stops a procession to look a heckler in the face and dismisses him without a tremor; whom Cassius will sarcastically call "immortal Caesar" and describe as having "become a god". In the private column, so far: a husband who cannot father a child and hopes a ritual will help; a man who needs to say "Caesar is turned to hear" rather than 'I am listening'; and — through Cassius's hostile stories — a man who once cried "Help me, Cassius, or I sink!" and who, shaking with fever, begged for a drink "As a sick girl".

**LITERARY INTERPRETATION.** The scene's method is to build the public column on stage and the private column through report. That asymmetry matters. We see Caesar's greatness directly; we hear about his weakness from a man who hates him. So the audience is not told that Caesar is weak; it is invited to wonder whether the greatness is a performance and whether Cassius's stories are true. Part Two will add evidence from a neutral-hostile witness, Casca — the swoon, the "falling sickness" — and from Caesar himself, who admits that "this ear is deaf". By the end of the scene, both columns are full, and the question 'which Caesar is real?' has become the play's question. For the full portrait, go to 'Caesar in Act 1 — A Character Masterclass'.

## 5. The Seduction of Brutus: Cassius's First Approach

The word 'seduction' is chosen carefully. Cassius will use it himself at the end of the scene — "For who so firm that cannot be seduced?" — and it captures what happens here better than 'persuasion'. Persuasion works on reasons; seduction works on the self. Cassius barely argues about Caesar until Brutus has already confessed his fear. What he does first is make Brutus look at Brutus.

### Brutus — "I am not gamesome"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Will you go see the order of the course?

> **Brutus:** Not I.

> **Brutus:** I am not gamesome. I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony. / Let me not hinder, Cassius, your desires. / I'll leave you.

**Simple English:** Cassius: Will you come and watch the race? Brutus: Not me. I am not fond of sport; I lack some of that lively energy that Antony has. Do not let me stop you doing what you want, Cassius. I will leave you.

**Difficult words:** 'order of the course' — the proceedings of the race; 'gamesome' — fond of games and sport, playful; 'quick spirit' — lively, energetic temperament; 'hinder' — obstruct; 'desires' — wishes.

**Literal meaning:** Brutus declines to watch the games, explaining that he is not the sporting type and lacks Antony's liveliness, and he politely tries to leave.

**Subtext:** Brutus wants solitude. His comparison with Antony is more than modesty; it separates him from the pleasure-loving, physically vigorous world that surrounds Caesar. Cassius's invitation is a test: had Brutus gone to the games, there would have been no conversation.

**Hidden meaning:** The line quietly divides Rome into two temperaments — the "gamesome" men who run and revel with Caesar, and the reflective men who stand apart and think. Caesar himself will make the same division in Part Two when he says Cassius "loves no plays" and "hears no music". Brutus places himself, without knowing it, in the dangerous category.

**Character psychology:** Brutus is honest about a limitation and courteous in withdrawal — two traits that never leave him. He is also, at this moment, slightly withdrawn and low in spirits; a man preoccupied.

**Political meaning:** In a city where the ruler's favourites are men of appetite and display, the sober man who refuses the festival is already a kind of dissident, even before he dissents.

**Historical context:** Plutarch's Brutus is studious and philosophical, a reader and a writer of compressed Latin, close to the Stoic tradition through his uncle Cato; Shakespeare's "not gamesome" is a faithful distillation. The historical Antony's reputation for revelry is also well attested.

**Literary devices:** Contrast (Brutus against Antony); understatement ("some part"), which is characteristic of Brutus's modest self-description.

**Effect:** The audience registers Brutus as different from the crowd and from Caesar's circle before he has said anything political. The stage empties around two men who did not go to the party.

**Dramatic significance:** This refusal is the hinge of the scene: it creates the private space in which the conspiracy is conceived. It also introduces the Brutus–Antony contrast that will structure Act 3, when the man without "quick spirit" speaks to the crowd first and the quick-spirited man speaks second, and wins.

**Foreshadowing:** The comparison with Antony foreshadows their rivalry over the crowd and Brutus's fatal underestimation of Antony as a mere reveller.

**Scene connection:** Caesar's judgement of Cassius in Part Two — a man who "loves no plays" — echoes Brutus's self-description here; Caesar's instinct about un-gamesome men is right, though he aims it at the wrong one.

**ICSE keywords:** contrast, characterisation, foil (Antony as a foil to Brutus), introspection.

**Exam application:** 'Brutus's admission that he is "not gamesome" and lacks Antony's "quick spirit" establishes him as a reflective, withdrawn man — the opposite of Caesar's pleasure-loving favourite — and it is this withdrawal from the games that gives Cassius his opportunity.'

### Cassius's opening approach

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Brutus, I do observe you now of late. / I have not from your eyes that gentleness / And show of love as I was wont to have. / You bear too stubborn and too strange a hand / Over your friend that loves you.

**Simple English:** Brutus, I have been watching you recently. I no longer see in your eyes the kindness and the signs of affection I used to receive from you. You are treating your friend, who loves you, too harshly and too coldly.

**Difficult words:** 'observe' — watch closely; 'of late' — recently; 'show of love' — outward signs of friendship; 'wont' — accustomed; 'stubborn' — harsh, unyielding; 'strange' — distant, like a stranger; 'bear… a hand over' — to handle, control (an image from managing a horse by the reins, as many editors note).

**Literal meaning:** Cassius complains that Brutus has recently become cold and distant towards him, a friend who loves him.

**Subtext:** This is an accusation dressed as a hurt. Cassius does not ask 'what is troubling you?'; he says 'you have wronged me'. A courteous man like Brutus must now defend himself, and to defend himself he must explain — which is exactly what Cassius wants. Note also "I do observe you": Cassius announces that Brutus is under observation.

**Hidden meaning:** The opening move of the whole conspiracy is a complaint about the "show of love". The play's politics begin in the vocabulary of friendship, and the word 'show' — appearance, display — introduces the theme of appearance and reality on Cassius's own lips. He asks for shows; Brutus, in reply, will confess that he has been forgetting the "shows of love".

**Character psychology:** Cassius is a watcher (Caesar will say so: "He is a great observer"). He has noticed a change in Brutus and has decided that the change is an opening. His hurt may be partly genuine — the text does not exclude real affection — but it is deployed with precision.

**Political meaning:** Conspiracies require trust, and trust is built by the language of intimacy. Cassius is establishing that he has a claim on Brutus before he asks anything of him.

**Historical context:** Plutarch reports that Brutus and Cassius had been estranged over the competition for the more prestigious praetorship of 44 BC, which Caesar awarded to Brutus, and that Cassius sought a reconciliation before approaching Brutus about Caesar. Shakespeare leaves the praetorship out of this scene and converts the reconciliation into a complaint about coldness.

**Literary devices:** Metaphor of horsemanship in "bear… a hand" (interpretation, not certainty); the emotional appeal of "your friend that loves you" placed at the line-end for emphasis.

**Effect:** The audience sees a friend reproaching a friend and only gradually realises it is watching a recruitment.

**Dramatic significance:** It sets the pattern of Cassius's method: begin with Brutus, not with Caesar; begin with feeling, not with argument.

**Foreshadowing:** The quarrel between the two men in Act 4 will again begin with each accusing the other of wronging a friend; the friendship that starts as a tool becomes, by the end, real.

**Scene connection:** Cassius's soliloquy at the end of this scene ("Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus") reveals that he thinks in terms of who loves whom; his opening here is consistent with that mind.

**ICSE keywords:** manipulation, emotional appeal, appearance versus reality, friendship, observer.

**Exam application:** 'Cassius opens not with politics but with a reproach — Brutus no longer shows "that gentleness / And show of love" he once did — forcing the courteous Brutus to explain himself and so handing Cassius the opening he needs.'

### Brutus's internal conflict — "passions of some difference", "with himself at war"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** Be not deceived. If I have veiled my look, / I turn the trouble of my countenance / Merely upon myself. Vexèd I am / Of late with passions of some difference, / Conceptions only proper to myself, / Which give some soil, perhaps, to my behaviors.

> **Brutus:** Nor construe any further my neglect / Than that poor Brutus, with himself at war, / Forgets the shows of love to other men.

**Simple English:** Do not misunderstand me. If I have hidden my expression, the trouble in my face is aimed only at myself. Lately I have been troubled by conflicting feelings, thoughts that concern me alone, which perhaps stain my behaviour. Do not read my neglect as anything more than this: poor Brutus, fighting a war inside himself, forgets to show affection to other people.

**Difficult words:** 'veiled my look' — hidden my expression; 'countenance' — face, expression; 'Vexèd' — troubled, distressed; 'passions of some difference' — conflicting emotions, feelings at odds with one another; 'Conceptions' — thoughts, ideas; 'proper to myself' — belonging to me alone, private; 'soil' — stain, blemish; 'construe' — interpret; 'neglect' — failure of attention to friends; 'shows of love' — outward signs of friendship.

**Literal meaning:** Brutus reassures Cassius that his coldness is not aimed at him. He is troubled by private, conflicting feelings that have spoiled his manners, and he asks his friends not to read anything more into it.

**Subtext:** Brutus is telling the truth and concealing it at the same time. He confirms that something is wrong, admits it is a conflict, and refuses to say what it is. To Cassius, this is the best possible answer: a divided Brutus is a recruitable Brutus.

**Hidden meaning:** "with himself at war" is the most important phrase in Brutus's first long speech. He reaches for the vocabulary of war to describe his mind, in a scene where nobody has yet mentioned violence. The word arrives before the thing. Notice, too, the third person: "poor Brutus". Like Caesar, Brutus has begun to speak of himself as a public figure, a name — the very habit Cassius will exploit when he weighs the names 'Brutus' and 'Caesar'.

**Character psychology:** Brutus is introspective, scrupulous and secretive. He apologises for a discourtesy he has not really committed; he shields his friends from his moods; and he presents his conflict as a fault of manners rather than a matter of politics. The "passions of some difference" are, we infer, love for Caesar against fear for Rome, but Brutus will not name them even to himself for another act.

**Political meaning:** The inner division of Rome's most honourable man is the state's division in miniature. A republic whose best citizen cannot decide what he thinks about its ruler is a republic that will be decided by someone less scrupulous.

**Historical context:** Plutarch describes Brutus in the months before the Ides as sleepless and visibly troubled, so much so that his wife Porcia noticed. Shakespeare transfers that observed trouble to the eyes of Cassius.

**Literary devices:** Metaphor ("with himself at war"; "veiled my look"; "give some soil"); illeism ("poor Brutus"); inversion ("Vexèd I am") that throws weight on the emotion; the deliberately abstract diction of "passions of some difference" and "Conceptions only proper to myself", which is Brutus's way of naming a thing without naming it.

**Effect:** The audience learns that Brutus is troubled before it learns why, and is drawn into the same curiosity as Cassius.

**Dramatic significance:** This speech is the first sight of Brutus's mind, and the phrase it leaves behind — "with himself at war" — becomes the frame for his whole tragedy.

**Foreshadowing:** Foreshadows the orchard soliloquy of Act 2, where the war is fought in words; the quarrel of Act 4, where the war spills between friends; and the civil war itself.

**Scene connection:** Cassius's soliloquy at the end of the scene turns this speech into a plan: a man "with himself at war" is a man whose "honourable mettle may be wrought" — a nature that can be shaped.

**ICSE keywords:** internal conflict, introspection, secrecy, illeism, foreshadowing, private versus public.

**Exam application:** 'Brutus's confession that he is "with himself at war", troubled by "passions of some difference", reveals the divided conscience that defines him: he already feels the conflict between love for Caesar and duty to Rome, though he will not yet name it, and the military metaphor foreshadows the literal war his decision will bring.'

::: insight Why Shakespeare connects private emotion with public politics
Here is something a summary will never tell you. Shakespeare has Brutus describe a private mood — a bad temper, a coldness to friends — in the language of war, and he does it in the one scene where Rome's public future is being decided offstage by shouting crowds. The private and the public are made to speak the same words. Why? Because the play's central claim about politics is that the state is not a machine but a body of persons, and that its convulsions begin as convulsions inside individual men. Brutus's war "with himself" will become the war between Brutus and Cassius, then the war between the conspirators and the triumvirs. Rome will bleed because one man could not reconcile two feelings. Shakespeare presses the parallel throughout: Cassius's political grievance is described as a personal humiliation (he must "bend his body" if Caesar nods); Caesar's political stubbornness is a personal vanity; the crowd's public verdict on the crown is a matter of shouts and "sweaty nightcaps". The lesson for your reading is practical. Whenever a character in this play describes an emotion, ask what public consequence that emotion is about to have; and whenever a public event occurs, ask which private feeling produced it. That double reading is what an examiner means by 'analysis'.
:::

### The mirror and the glass: the play's theory of self-knowledge

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?

> **Brutus:** No, Cassius, for the eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things.

> **Cassius:** And it is very much lamented, Brutus, / That you have no such mirrors as will turn / Your hidden worthiness into your eye, / That you might see your shadow.

> **Cassius:** And since you know you cannot see yourself / So well as by reflection, I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of.

**Simple English:** Cassius: Tell me, Brutus, can you see your own face? Brutus: No, because the eye cannot see itself except in a reflection, in something else. Cassius: Exactly. And many people regret that you have no mirror to show your hidden worth to your own eye, so that you could see your own image. Since you know you can only see yourself by reflection, I will be your mirror, and modestly reveal to you the side of yourself you do not yet know.

**Difficult words:** 'reflection' — a mirrored image; 'lamented' — regretted, mourned; 'hidden worthiness' — unrecognised merit; 'shadow' — reflected image (an older sense; the word also carried its modern sense, which the play lets us hear); 'glass' — mirror; 'modestly' — without exaggeration; 'discover' — reveal, uncover.

**Literal meaning:** Cassius draws Brutus into agreeing that no one can see himself directly, then offers to be the mirror that shows Brutus his own hidden greatness, which many respected Romans, he claims, wish Brutus could see.

**Subtext:** This is a piece of logic with a trap in it. Brutus supplies the harmless premise (the eye cannot see itself); Cassius supplies the conclusion (therefore trust me to tell you who you are). Between the two lies the claim that "many of the best respect in Rome" wish Brutus "had his eyes" — that is, could see what they see. Cassius makes himself the channel of Rome's opinion, precisely what the forged letters will do in writing.

**Hidden meaning:** A mirror shows you reversed, and it shows only what is placed before it. Cassius's mirror is angled: it will show Brutus the nobility that Cassius wants him to act upon, and hide the treason that the action involves. The word "shadow" is double-edged. In Shakespeare's English it means a reflection; but a shadow is also the dark shape a body casts, and an audience may hear that Cassius is offering to show Brutus his shadow side. "Modestly" is itself immodest: the man who says he will be modest is about to compare Brutus with Caesar.

**Character psychology:** Cassius here is at his most intelligent. He has understood that Brutus cannot be moved by an attack on Caesar but can be moved by an appeal to his own image of himself. He flatters not with praise but with the suggestion of unrecognised worth, which is far more effective on a modest man. Brutus's response — "Into what dangers would you lead me, Cassius, / That you would have me seek into myself / For that which is not in me?" — shows a man who senses the trap, names the danger, and still keeps listening.

**Political meaning:** Political recruitment in the play works by supplying men with a picture of themselves. The people in Scene 1 were given a picture of ingratitude by the tribunes; Brutus is given a picture of hidden greatness by Cassius; the crowd in Act 3 will be given competing pictures by Brutus and Antony. Whoever holds the mirror holds power.

**Historical context:** The idea that the eye cannot see itself was a commonplace of Renaissance thought, and the mirror was a favourite Tudor image for instruction: the popular collection of cautionary verse tragedies called A Mirror for Magistrates (first printed in 1559) offered rulers examples in which to see themselves. Elizabethan audiences may have heard Cassius offering Brutus exactly such a mirror.

**Literary devices:** Extended metaphor (eye, reflection, mirror, glass, shadow); rhetorical question used as a trap ("can you see your face?"); aphorism ("the eye sees not itself / But by reflection"); wordplay on "shadow"; irony in "modestly".

**Effect:** The audience watches a mind being opened with a key made of its own logic. There is intellectual pleasure and dread at once.

**Dramatic significance:** This passage is the method of the whole conspiracy in one image. It also introduces the theme of self-knowledge that the tragedy will pursue: Brutus never does see himself fully, and his errors of judgement come from trusting reflections — Cassius's, the letters', his own idealised image.

**Foreshadowing:** Foreshadows the forged letters, which are written mirrors; and the wider pattern in which Brutus acts on how Rome supposedly sees him rather than on what he sees himself.

**Scene connection:** Connects directly to Cassius's soliloquy ("Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name") and to the letters delivered in Scene 3 and read in Act 2.

**ICSE keywords:** extended metaphor, flattery, self-knowledge, manipulation, appearance versus reality, irony.

**Exam application:** 'By making himself Brutus's mirror — "I, your glass" — Cassius turns a harmless truth — that "the eye sees not itself / But by reflection" — into a claim on Brutus's trust: only Cassius can show Brutus his "hidden worthiness". The flattery works because it appeals not to vanity but to modesty, and it prepares the forged letters that will play the same role in writing.'

::: insight The mirror as the play's theory of self-knowledge
Take the mirror passage seriously as philosophy, not just as flattery. Brutus states a real truth: no one sees his own face directly. We know ourselves through reflections — through what others tell us, what we read of ourselves in their eyes, the reputation that returns to us as report. Julius Caesar is a play in which almost everyone lives by such reflections. Caesar knows himself through the crowd's shouts and the flattery of Decius. Brutus knows himself through Cassius's mirror and then through letters that seem to come from Rome. The crowd knows what it thinks through whoever spoke to it last. The tragedy is not that people trust mirrors; it is that every mirror in the play is held by someone with a motive. Cassius's genius, and his corruption, lie in the phrase "I, your glass": he offers to become the instrument through which Brutus perceives himself, and Brutus accepts. From that moment Brutus's self-image — the descendant of the liberator, the man of honour Rome is waiting for — is partly Cassius's construction. When, in Act 2, Brutus reads the planted letters and hears in them Rome's voice, he is looking into the same angled glass. Shakespeare is proposing something unsettling: that the honourable man is more vulnerable than the cynic, because he believes the reflection shows his duty. Cassius, who trusts no mirror, cannot be flattered; Brutus, who wants to be worthy, can. Keep this in mind whenever an examiner asks why a noble man did a terrible thing.
:::

### The shout offstage and Brutus's admission

Before the shout, Cassius has been protesting his own sincerity: he is no "common laughter" (Folger; the MIT text reads 'laugher', a habitual joker), he does not "stale with ordinary oaths" his love, he does not "fawn on men and hug them hard / And after scandal them" — and if Brutus ever finds him doing such things, "then hold me dangerous". The audience, who will shortly hear Caesar call this very man dangerous, enjoys the irony. Then the stage direction — in single quotes, 'Flourish and shout' — breaks in.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king.

> **Cassius:** Ay, do you fear it? / Then must I think you would not have it so.

> **Brutus:** I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well. / But wherefore do you hold me here so long? / What is it that you would impart to me?

**Simple English:** Brutus: What is that shouting about? I am afraid the people are choosing Caesar as their king. Cassius: Oh, you fear it? Then I must conclude you would not want it to happen. Brutus: I would not want it, Cassius — and yet I love him dearly. But why are you keeping me here so long? What is it you want to tell me?

**Difficult words:** 'means' — signifies; 'fear' — am afraid; 'Choose' — elect, make; 'have it so' — wish it to be so; 'wherefore' — why; 'impart' — communicate, tell.

**Literal meaning:** Hearing the crowd, Brutus voices his fear that Caesar is being made king. Cassius seizes on the word 'fear' to establish that Brutus opposes it. Brutus agrees, adds that he loves Caesar, and asks Cassius to come to the point.

**Subtext:** Cassius has said nothing about Caesar; Brutus raises him unprompted. That is the moment Cassius has been waiting for, and his reply is a small masterpiece of pressure: he converts an emotion ('fear') into a position ('you would not have it so') and makes Brutus confirm it. Brutus's "yet I love him well" is a plea for the conversation to remain complicated; Cassius will ignore it.

**Hidden meaning:** The shout is heard, not understood. Brutus interprets it in the direction of his fear. We learn later that the crowd was cheering Caesar's refusal of the crown; Brutus's fear ran ahead of the facts. Shakespeare shows how an anxious mind reads ambiguous sounds, and how a manipulator uses the reading rather than correcting it.

**Character psychology:** Brutus's mind holds two loyalties at once and states them in one line, with the tiny hinge "yet": "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well." The whole tragedy of Brutus is in that sentence. His impatience ("wherefore do you hold me here so long?") is not rudeness; it is the discomfort of a man who wants a painful subject either dropped or made explicit.

**Political meaning:** The republic's fate is being decided by an offstage crowd whose shouts cannot be interpreted. Two senators stand at the edge, guessing. This is the political condition of the whole play: decisions made in public noise, interpreted in private fear.

**Historical context:** The historical Brutus had been pardoned by Caesar after siding with Pompey and had been advanced by him to the urban praetorship; the "love" he speaks of has a real basis in obligation. The fear of the title 'king' was a genuine Roman reflex dating from the expulsion of the Tarquins.

**Literary devices:** Dramatic irony (we will learn the shout meant the opposite); antithesis in "I would not… yet I love him well"; the sound effect of the offstage shout as a structural device; Cassius's rhetorical echo ("Ay, do you fear it?").

**Effect:** The audience feels the conversation lurch from generalities to the forbidden subject. Tension rises with the noise.

**Dramatic significance:** Brutus's fear and love, spoken in one breath, give Cassius his target and give the audience the terms of Brutus's dilemma. Nothing Cassius says afterwards would matter if Brutus had not said this.

**Foreshadowing:** Foreshadows the orchard soliloquy, where Brutus reasons from exactly this fear (Caesar may be crowned; a crown may change him) to the decision to kill; and the funeral speech, where he tells the crowd he loved Caesar.

**Scene connection:** Casca's report in Part Two tells us what the shout meant; Cassius's "They shouted thrice" shows he was counting. Brutus's misreading anticipates Cassius's own fatal misreading of a shout at Philippi.

**ICSE keywords:** dramatic irony, conflict of loyalties, ambiguity, offstage action, manipulation, republicanism.

**Exam application:** 'When the crowd shouts, Brutus admits "I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king" and immediately adds "yet I love him well" — the single line that contains his whole dilemma. Cassius seizes on the word "fear" to make Brutus confirm his opposition, showing how he converts Brutus's emotion into a political commitment.'

::: insight Why Shakespeare lets us hear the shouting but not see the crown
Shakespeare had the crown scene ready-made in Plutarch, and it is spectacular: a consul running through the city, a diadem held up, a dictator refusing, a crowd roaring. He chose not to stage it. Ask why, and refuse the lazy answer about a small company. First, keeping the crown offstage keeps the stage for Brutus's reaction. The scene is not about whether Caesar gets a crown; it is about what the possibility does to Brutus, and Shakespeare wants our eyes on his face when the shout comes. Second, the offstage crown puts the audience in Brutus's position. We too hear noise and must guess; when Casca later explains, we too discover we misread. The play thus trains us in its central lesson — that public events reach us as sounds and reports, coloured by the fears of the hearer and the bias of the reporter. Third, the crown never appears on stage at all. The play's most important object is an absence. Caesar's kingship remains, for the whole tragedy, a matter of rumour, fear and interpretation, never a fact; the conspirators kill him for what he might become. By denying us the sight of the crown, Shakespeare denies us certainty about Caesar's ambition, and forces us to judge the murder without the evidence the murderers claim to have. That is a far more disturbing design than any spectacle.
:::

### Honour — "Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other"

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other / And I will look on both indifferently; / For let the gods so speed me as I love / The name of honour more than I fear death.

> **Cassius:** I know that virtue to be in you, Brutus, / As well as I do know your outward favour. / Well, honour is the subject of my story.

**Simple English:** Brutus: If what you want to say concerns the public good, then put honour before one of my eyes and death before the other, and I will look at both without preferring either; for — may the gods favour me only as far as this is true — I love the name of honour more than I fear death. Cassius: I know you have that quality, Brutus, as surely as I know your face. Well then, honour is what my story is about.

**Difficult words:** 'aught' — anything; 'toward' — concerning, tending to; 'general good' — the public welfare, the good of all; 'indifferently' — impartially, without preferring one; 'so speed me' — so prosper me, so favour me (an oath formula); 'the name of honour' — honour itself, or the reputation of honour; 'virtue' — quality, excellence; 'outward favour' — face, appearance.

**Literal meaning:** Brutus declares that if the matter concerns Rome's welfare he will weigh honour against death without flinching, because he values honour above life. Cassius says he knew this, and announces that honour is his theme.

**Subtext:** Brutus is setting conditions — it must be "toward the general good" — but in setting them he tells Cassius exactly which key fits the lock. Cassius's reply is almost comic in its speed: "honour is the subject of my story". He has not chosen his subject; Brutus has chosen it for him.

**Hidden meaning:** Look at "the name of honour". Brutus does not say he loves honour; he loves the name of it. Many critics read this as an unconscious confession that Brutus is attached to his reputation, to being seen as honourable, as much as to the thing itself. The text leaves it open, but the phrase is a gift to Cassius, who is about to argue entirely in terms of names. Notice also "indifferently": Brutus claims he can look on death and honour without preference — a claim of Stoic detachment that the frightened, sleepless Brutus of Act 2 will not quite sustain.

**Character psychology:** Brutus's self-image is now fully visible: a man for whom the moral quality of an act, and the public good, outrank his life. This is sincere, and it is also his vulnerability, because a man who has announced that he fears nothing for honour's sake has left himself no honourable way to refuse an honourable-sounding proposal.

**Political meaning:** Republican virtue, in the Roman sense, meant putting the res publica — the public thing — above private life and private feeling. Brutus states the creed in one sentence. Cassius, who has just said he would rather not exist than live in awe of a man like himself, has a different creed: personal dignity. Their alliance is built on the overlap between these two ideas of honour, and their quarrel in Act 4 comes from the gap.

**Historical context:** The historical Brutus was associated with philosophy and with his uncle Cato, the republican who chose death over submission to Caesar; Brutus's language of honour and indifference to death echoes that tradition, which Shakespeare found in Plutarch.

**Literary devices:** Antithesis and visual metaphor ("honour in one eye and death i' th' other" — two abstract nouns personified as objects to be looked at); oath formula ("let the gods so speed me"); repetition of "honour", which Cassius immediately picks up — a rhetorical hand-over.

**Effect:** The audience hears Brutus's terms with respect and with dread, because the phrase "death i' th' other" is spoken in a play whose ending it knows.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the hinge between Cassius's flattery and his attack on Caesar. Brutus has now supplied the moral framework within which the murder will be argued: honour, the general good, indifference to death.

**Foreshadowing:** Foreshadows Brutus's speech to the people after the assassination, built on honour and the good of Rome; Antony's ironic repetition of the word in the funeral oration; and Brutus's death at Philippi, where honour and death are indeed looked on together.

**Scene connection:** Cassius's soliloquy calls Brutus's temperament "honourable mettle" that "may be wrought"; this speech is the moment Cassius learns exactly what that metal is made of.

**ICSE keywords:** honour, republican virtue, antithesis, Stoicism, the general good, foreshadowing, vulnerability.

**Exam application:** 'Brutus's declaration that he loves "The name of honour more than I fear death" defines his character and his tragedy: it is sincere republican virtue, but it also tells Cassius precisely which argument will move him, and Cassius replies at once that "honour is the subject of my story".'

## 6. Cassius's Case Against Caesar: Two Stories and a Statue

Once Brutus has named honour as his price, Cassius changes method. Until now he has worked on Brutus's image of himself; from here he works on Brutus's image of Caesar. He does it not with political argument — he never once mentions a law, a tax, a legion or the Senate — but with two anecdotes and one picture: a swimming race, a sickbed, and a giant statue. Every one of them attacks the same target: the gap between the Caesar that Rome worships and the body of the man Cassius has seen up close. Hold on to this as you read. Cassius is not trying to prove that Caesar is a tyrant. He is trying to make Brutus feel that it is absurd — physically, bodily absurd — for a man like Brutus to bow to a man like that.

He opens with a creed, and you should know it before the story begins. "I cannot tell what you and other men / Think of this life; but, for my single self, / I had as lief not be as live to be / In awe of such a thing as I myself." In plain English: I do not know how you or anyone else feels about living, but speaking only for myself, I would as soon not exist as live in fear of a creature no different from me. ('Had as lief' means 'would as willingly'; 'such a thing as I myself' means 'a being of the same kind as me'.) Then the claim of equality: "I was born free as Caesar; so were you; / We both have fed as well, and we can both / Endure the winter's cold as well as he." Notice how the three verbs — born, fed, endure — reduce Caesar to a biological specimen: birth, food, weather. That is the register in which the whole attack will be conducted.

### Cassius's Tiber anecdote and the Aeneas/Anchises allusion

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** For once, upon a raw and gusty day, / The troubled Tiber chafing with her shores, / Caesar said to me 'Dar'st thou, Cassius, now / Leap in with me into this angry flood / And swim to yonder point?' Upon the word, / Accoutred as I was, I plungèd in / And bade him follow; so indeed he did.

> **Cassius:** But ere we could arrive the point proposed, / Caesar cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!' / I, as Aeneas, our great ancestor, / Did from the flames of Troy upon his shoulder / The old Anchises bear, so from the waves of Tiber / Did I the tirèd Caesar. And this man / Is now become a god, and Cassius is / A wretched creature and must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him.

**Simple English:** Once, on a cold, windy day, when the rough Tiber was beating angrily against its banks, Caesar said to me, 'Cassius, do you dare jump into this raging river with me now and swim to that point over there?' The moment he said it, I jumped in fully dressed and armed, and told him to follow — and he did. But before we could reach the agreed spot, Caesar shouted, 'Help me, Cassius, or I'll drown!' And just as Aeneas, the ancestor of all Romans, carried his old father Anchises on his shoulder out of burning Troy, so I carried the exhausted Caesar out of the Tiber. And this is the man who has now become a god, while Cassius is a miserable creature who must bow if Caesar so much as nods carelessly in his direction.

**Difficult words:** 'raw' — cold and damp; 'gusty' — windy; 'troubled' — rough, agitated; 'chafing with her shores' — rubbing angrily against its banks (the river is imagined as a woman, 'her'); 'Dar'st thou' — do you dare; 'angry flood' — the raging river; 'yonder point' — that spot over there; 'Upon the word' — the instant he said it; 'Accoutred' — dressed and equipped, in armour and clothing (which would weigh a swimmer down); 'plungèd' — two syllables, for the rhythm; 'bade' — told, commanded; 'ere' — before; 'arrive the point proposed' — reach the agreed goal; 'Aeneas' — the Trojan hero and legendary founder of the Roman race; 'Anchises' — Aeneas's aged father; 'tirèd' — two syllables; 'wretched' — miserable, pitiable; 'bend his body' — bow; 'carelessly' — casually, without even noticing; 'nod on him' — nod towards him.

**Literal meaning:** Cassius recalls a swimming challenge Caesar issued on a stormy day. Cassius accepted at once; Caesar followed but gave up and cried for help; Cassius carried him to shore as Aeneas carried his father out of Troy. Now that same rescued man is treated as a god, and his rescuer must bow to him.

**Subtext:** The surface is a story about courage; the subtext is a story about debt and reversal. Cassius was the stronger man and the saviour; the natural order, as he sees it, has been turned upside down. The real complaint is not 'Caesar is weak' but 'Caesar is weaker than I am, and yet I bow'. Listen to the rhythm of the verbs at the end — "become a god", "must bend his body", "but nod" — the climax of the speech is Cassius's own humiliation, not Caesar's failure.

**Hidden meaning:** The Aeneas comparison is doing more than decorating the story. Aeneas carried his *father*; by casting himself as Aeneas, Cassius casts Caesar as a helpless old parent, a burden on younger shoulders. It is a quietly contemptuous image, because it turns the saviour of Rome into dead weight. At the same time, the allusion flatters Cassius: he claims the founding hero's role, the man who carried Rome's future out of a burning past. One strong reading is that Cassius unconsciously reveals his own ambition here — he too would like to be Rome's bearer and founder. Notice also the opposition of elements: Aeneas rescued his father from fire, Cassius rescued Caesar from water. The two great destroying elements frame one man's greatness as borrowed.

**Character psychology:** Cassius is a man who keeps accounts. He remembers the exact day's weather, the exact words of the challenge, the exact moment Caesar cried out. This is the memory of a man who has turned the episode over for years, nursing it. His bitterness is personal before it is political; the words "wretched creature" are said of himself, and they hurt. Caesar, in the story, is impulsive (he issues a dare), proud (he will not be out-dared) and finally dependent — a portrait that anticipates the Caesar we see later, who insists on going to the Capitol because he will not seem afraid.

**Political meaning:** This is the republican argument in its most intimate form: rulers are men, their bodies fail like ours, and the worship of one man by his equals is a lie about nature. The phrase "bend his body" turns the question of monarchy into a question of posture — whether a free Roman will bow. In Rome the bow belonged to the East, to the courts of kings; a Roman who bowed had, in his own eyes, stopped being Roman.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: there is no swimming race with Cassius in Plutarch or any other ancient source; this story appears to be Shakespeare's invention. What the sources do record is that Caesar was a famously strong swimmer — Plutarch and Suetonius both tell how, during the fighting at Alexandria, he swam to safety holding papers out of the water. Shakespeare therefore seems to have taken a known strength of the historical Caesar and reversed it in Cassius's mouth. That tells you something important about how to read the speech: it is Cassius's testimony, not the play's verdict. The Aeneas story comes from Virgil's Aeneid, where Aeneas carries his father Anchises from burning Troy; the Romans, including Caesar's own family, the Julii, claimed descent from Aeneas — which gives Cassius's comparison an extra sting, since he borrows Caesar's own ancestral boast.

**Literary devices:** Classical allusion (Aeneas and Anchises); extended simile ("as Aeneas… so from the waves of Tiber / Did I"); personification of the river ("troubled Tiber chafing with her shores", "angry flood"); direct speech embedded in narrative, which makes the memory vivid and lets Cassius mimic Caesar's cry; antithesis between "a god" and "A wretched creature"; the bitter irony of "this man / Is now become a god"; alliteration in "bend his body".

**Effect:** The audience is drawn into a gripping little story and then ambushed by its moral. For a moment Caesar is not the voice that silenced the crowd but a frightened swimmer shouting for help. The effect is to make the audience, like Brutus, see the god's body — and then to feel the sourness of the narrator, which is Shakespeare's safeguard against our believing him entirely.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the first attack on Caesar's person in the play, and it sets the terms: not 'what has Caesar done?' but 'what is Caesar made of?'. It also discloses the real engine of the conspiracy as Shakespeare presents it — envy clothed as principle — which Caesar himself will diagnose minutes later in his portrait of the lean man who is "never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves".

**Foreshadowing:** The image of a man who cries for help and must be carried prepares the audience for the two reports that follow: the fever and the swoon in the marketplace. More distantly, the river that nearly drowns Caesar anticipates the many images of flood and tide in the play, and Caesar's pride in accepting a dare foreshadows his fatal refusal to seem afraid on the Ides.

**Scene connection:** Marullus in Scene 1 made the Tiber tremble at the people's shouts for Pompey; Cassius now makes the same river the scene of Caesar's weakness. In Scene 3 Cassius will again measure himself against the elements, baring his chest to the lightning — the same temperament that leaps into a storm-swollen river "Accoutred as I was".

**ICSE keywords:** anecdote, classical allusion, envy, personal grievance, appearance versus reality, the body of the ruler, republican equality, biased narrator.

**Exam application:** If asked what the Tiber story reveals about Cassius rather than Caesar, you might write: 'Cassius casts himself as Aeneas carrying "The old Anchises" and Caesar as the helpless burden who "cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!'" — yet the climax of his story is his own humiliation, that he "must bend his body" to the man he saved. The anecdote exposes a wounded pride that the play presents as the private root of his public republicanism.'

### The fever in Spain — "this god did shake", "As a sick girl"

Cassius does not pause between the river and the sickbed. The second story follows the first without a connecting word, as though one proof of weakness naturally summons another. Where the Tiber story showed Caesar's body failing in action, this one shows it failing in illness — and, more cruelly, shows his *voice* failing, the very instrument of his authority.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** He had a fever when he was in Spain, / And when the fit was on him, I did mark / How he did shake. 'Tis true, this god did shake. / His coward lips did from their colour fly, / And that same eye whose bend doth awe the world / Did lose his lustre. I did hear him groan.

> **Cassius:** Ay, and that tongue of his that bade the Romans / Mark him and write his speeches in their books, / 'Alas,' it cried 'Give me some drink, Titinius' / As a sick girl. Ye gods, it doth amaze me / A man of such a feeble temper should / So get the start of the majestic world / And bear the palm alone.

**Simple English:** He had a fever when he was in Spain, and when the attack came on I noticed how he trembled. Yes, it is true: this so-called god trembled. His cowardly lips went pale; that same eye whose glance makes the whole world afraid lost its brightness. I heard him groan. And that tongue of his — the tongue that told Romans to pay attention to him and copy his speeches into their notebooks — cried 'Oh no! Give me something to drink, Titinius', like a sick little girl. By heaven, it astonishes me that a man of such a weak constitution should have got ahead of the whole magnificent world and taken the prize of victory for himself alone.

**Difficult words:** 'fit' — an attack of illness, a seizure; 'mark' — notice, observe (and later 'Mark him' — pay attention to him); 'coward lips' — cowardly lips (the adjective transferred from the man to his lips); 'did from their colour fly' — lost their colour, went pale (the colour is imagined as fleeing like a deserter); 'bend' — glance, direction of the eye; 'awe' — fill with fear and reverence; 'lustre' — brightness, shine; 'bade' — commanded; 'Titinius' — a Roman officer, here imagined as Caesar's companion in Spain; 'feeble temper' — weak constitution, poor physical make-up; 'get the start of' — outstrip, get ahead of; 'the majestic world' — the whole splendid world; 'bear the palm alone' — carry off the prize of victory by himself (the palm branch was the classical symbol of victory).

**Literal meaning:** Cassius describes watching Caesar in a feverish seizure in Spain: trembling, pale, dull-eyed, groaning, and whimpering for a drink. He marvels that such a physically weak man should have risen above the whole world and taken all the glory for himself.

**Subtext:** Cassius is not reporting an illness; he is prosecuting a case. Every detail is chosen to contradict a piece of Caesar's public image: the eye that awes the world went dull; the tongue that dictated to Rome begged for water; the god shook. The repeated "did" — "did mark", "did shake", "did from their colour fly", "Did lose", "did hear" — is the rhythm of sworn testimony: I saw, I saw, I heard. Cassius wants Brutus to feel that he, uniquely, has seen behind the curtain.

**Hidden meaning:** The phrase "As a sick girl" is the most revealing in the speech, and it reveals Cassius more than Caesar. His contempt is gendered: weakness is female, authority is male, and a man who whimpers has forfeited his sex. He will say the same thing in Scene 3 — "we are governed with our mothers' spirits. / Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish." For Cassius, politics is a contest of manliness; tyranny is suffered by the unmanly and overthrown by the manly. That is a very different creed from Brutus's honour, though the two men will act as if it were the same. Notice, too, that the one word Caesar is reported as speaking is "Alas" — the word of lament. Cassius gives the great orator exactly one word, and chooses a word of defeat.

**Character psychology:** Cassius here is pitiless and precise. There is no sympathy for a sick man, only the satisfaction of a witness to weakness; the ironic stress on "this god" is spoken with relish. Yet the speech also betrays bewilderment — "it doth amaze me" — and the bewilderment is genuine. Cassius cannot understand how a body he has seen fail can command a world. He never grasps what Caesar's authority rests on, which is not muscle but the willingness of others to believe in him. That blind spot governs the whole conspiracy: they kill the body and leave the belief alive, for Antony to use.

**Political meaning:** The republican case rests on human equality, and illness is the great equaliser; Cassius uses it to argue that nothing in Caesar's nature licenses his position. But note the logical slide. From 'Caesar's body is weak' Cassius moves to 'Caesar should not rule alone'. The premise is about flesh; the conclusion is about power. Shakespeare lets the audience hear the gap, and Brutus — who is a reasoner — never actually accepts this argument. His own reasoning in Act 2 will concern what Caesar may *become*, not what his body is.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch says that Caesar suffered from epilepsy (which Shakespeare, following North's translation, calls the "falling sickness") and that the illness first attacked him at Corduba in Spain. Shakespeare has therefore grounded Cassius's story in a real detail of Caesar's biography, though the scene of whimpering for a drink is dramatic elaboration, not record. The historical Titinius was an officer of Cassius, and he appears in the play's last act in that role; Shakespeare's placing of him at Caesar's side in Spain is a dramatist's convenience, not history. The detail that Romans were told to "write his speeches in their books" reflects Caesar's real fame as an orator and writer, whose Commentaries on the Gallic War were already classics.

**Literary devices:** Simile ("As a sick girl"); irony and sarcasm ("this god did shake"); transferred epithet ("coward lips" — the cowardice belongs to the man, not the lips); personification of colour fleeing the lips; metonymy in "that tongue of his" (the tongue standing for Caesar's eloquence) and "that same eye" (the eye for his commanding presence); hyperbole ("whose bend doth awe the world"); metaphor of the race and the prize ("get the start of the majestic world / And bear the palm alone"); anaphoric repetition of "did"; reported speech.

**Effect:** The audience, who has just watched Caesar's imperious handling of the Soothsayer, is given a second Caesar: trembling, pale, calling for water. The two images collide, and the collision produces the question that drives Acts 1–3: which Caesar is the real one? The sarcasm also produces unease about the speaker; an audience can enjoy Cassius's wit while distrusting his pleasure in another man's suffering.

**Dramatic significance:** This speech prepares the ground for Casca's report, minutes later, that Caesar "swooned and fell down" in the marketplace and "foamed at mouth". When that report comes, Brutus will supply the diagnosis — the "falling sickness" — as though Cassius's story had already lodged in his mind. The speech is thus a planted seed, and the audience watches it grow within the same scene.

**Foreshadowing:** Caesar's physical frailty, first heard of here, will be confirmed on stage by his own admission that one ear is deaf, and will be at the heart of Calpurnia's fears in Act 2. The exposure of a 'god' as a body foreshadows the assassination itself, in which the body of the man is what the conspirators can reach and the idea of Caesar is what escapes them.

**Scene connection:** The eye "whose bend doth awe the world" connects to Caesar's own face-reading — "Let me see his face" — and to his later reading of Cassius's "lean and hungry look": the play is full of eyes that judge and eyes that fail. The tongue that commanded Rome connects to the voice that silenced the crowd at the scene's start, and to Antony's tongue in Act 3, which will do what Caesar's can no longer do.

**ICSE keywords:** sarcasm, irony, transferred epithet, simile, metonymy, physical weakness, appearance versus reality, envy, misogyny (Cassius's equation of weakness with femininity), biased witness.

**Exam application:** 'Cassius's sneer that "this god did shake" and begged for water "As a sick girl" is designed to collapse the distance between Caesar's public image and his mortal body. It tells us less about Caesar — whose illness is a matter of record — than about Cassius, whose contempt for weakness and whose amazement that such a man should "bear the palm alone" betray envy rather than principle.'

### The Colossus speech

A second shout interrupts. Brutus's response is worth hearing on its own before Cassius seizes it: "Another general shout! / I do believe that these applauses are / For some new honours that are heaped on Caesar." Three things to notice. He is still guessing; he cannot see. He now assumes the worst by default ("new honours… heaped") — Cassius's two stories have done their work on his mood even though he has said nothing. And the word "heaped" is an image of excess, piling up — exactly the picture Cassius is about to enlarge into a statue so big the world must pass beneath it.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** Another general shout! / I do believe that these applauses are / For some new honours that are heaped on Caesar.

> **Cassius:** Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves.

**Simple English:** Brutus: Another shout from the whole crowd! I believe this applause is for some new honours being piled on Caesar. Cassius: Why, man, he stands with his legs astride the whole cramped world like the giant statue at Rhodes, and we insignificant men walk about between his enormous legs, peering around for a place to be buried without honour.

**Difficult words:** 'general' — from the whole people, universal; 'applauses' — bursts of applause; 'heaped' — piled up; 'bestride' — stand with one leg on each side of; 'narrow' — small, cramped (the world shrinks beside Caesar); 'Colossus' — the gigantic bronze statue of the sun-god at Rhodes, one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world; 'petty' — small, insignificant; 'peep about' — peer around timidly; 'dishonourable graves' — graves without honour, the deaths of men who did nothing worth remembering.

**Literal meaning:** Caesar is like a giant statue straddling a shrunken world, while ordinary men walk between his legs looking for nothing better than an obscure, shameful grave.

**Subtext:** Cassius has turned a shout into a monument. Brutus guessed at 'honours'; Cassius makes the honours into a bronze giant and places Brutus beneath its legs. The move is from the ear to the eye: he gives Brutus a picture to be humiliated by. The real force of the image is in the second half — not Caesar's size but *our* smallness, and the shame of a life spent looking for a grave. To a man who has just said he loves the name of honour more than he fears death, "dishonourable graves" is the precise threat: not death, which Brutus does not fear, but death without honour, which he does.

**Hidden meaning:** A Colossus is a statue, not a man; it is hollow, made by men, and famously it fell. (The real Colossus of Rhodes collapsed in an earthquake within about sixty years of its building.) Many critics note that the image therefore undermines itself: Cassius means to say Caesar is gigantic, but he has chosen for his comparison the most celebrated fallen monument of antiquity. The audience may also hear a stage picture in it. On the Elizabethan stage, as later, Caesar's body will lie at the foot of Pompey's statue; here Caesar *is* the statue and the conspirators are the small men at its base. The play repeatedly asks what a great man is once he has become an image — Scene 1's "images" "decked with ceremonies", the statues from which scarves are pulled, the Colossus, and finally the corpse Antony displays.

**Character psychology:** Cassius thinks in images of posture and position — bending, bowing, walking under, peeping about. His politics is spatial: who stands over whom. The intensity of "peep about" suggests how acutely he feels the indignity of smallness; the speech is self-portrait as much as caricature. Brutus's single contribution, that he believes the shouts are for "new honours", shows a mind now disposed to read every sound against Caesar.

**Political meaning:** This is the sharpest republican image in the scene. A republic is a space in which many men stand upright together; Cassius pictures a Rome in which there is room for only one upright figure, and everyone else is reduced to crawling. The word "narrow" is pointed: Caesar does not merely dominate the world, he shrinks it. The republican fear is that monarchy does not simply put one man on top but diminishes every other citizen into a subject.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: the Colossus of Rhodes, a bronze statue of the sun-god Helios roughly thirty metres high, was completed around 280 BC and stood for only a few decades before an earthquake brought it down. The picture of the statue straddling the harbour mouth so that ships sailed between its legs is a medieval legend, not ancient fact — but it is exactly the picture Shakespeare's image depends on, and it was the picture his audience knew. The real Rhodes was a place Caesar knew (he had studied there as a young man), though the play does not use that fact.

**Literary devices:** Epic simile ("Like a Colossus"); classical allusion; hyperbole; antithesis of scale ("huge legs" against "petty men", the "narrow world" against the giant who straddles it); the diminishing verbs "Walk under… peep about"; the oxymoronic sting of "dishonourable graves" for a man obsessed with honour; alliteration ("petty men… peep").

**Effect:** The image is so vivid that it has become the most quoted picture of Caesar in English, and in the theatre it does what Cassius intends — it makes the audience, for a moment, feel small. It also produces a flash of the grotesque: grown men peering between a statue's legs is both frightening and faintly ridiculous, and the ridicule is aimed at the men, not the statue.

**Dramatic significance:** The Colossus marks the point where Cassius stops describing Caesar's past and starts describing Brutus's present. From here on his speech is about what *we* are — "we petty men", "we are underlings", "yours is as fair a name". It is the pivot from denigration to incitement.

**Foreshadowing:** The fallen Colossus foreshadows the fall of Caesar and, beyond it, the collapse of the men who brought him down. Shakespeare returns to the idea of Caesar as a towering body brought low in Antony's funeral oration and in the image of Caesar's spirit walking abroad in Act 3; the conspirators who stood beneath his legs will find at Philippi that his shadow still covers them.

**Scene connection:** Flavius in Scene 1 wanted to pluck Caesar's "growing feathers" so that he would "fly an ordinary pitch" rather than "soar above the view of men"; Cassius's statue is the same fear in a different image — a Caesar who has outgrown human scale. The "dishonourable graves" connect to the honour Brutus set in one eye and death in the other, a few lines earlier.

**ICSE keywords:** simile, hyperbole, classical allusion, antithesis, scale, republicanism, incitement, dishonour, iconic image.

**Exam application:** 'When Cassius describes Caesar as a Colossus beneath whose "huge legs" the Romans "peep about / To find ourselves dishonourable graves", he is not merely exaggerating Caesar's power. He is aiming at Brutus's one declared fear — a death without honour — and converting Caesar's greatness into Brutus's shame.'

### "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars"

::: quote The most quoted lines of Act 1
> **Cassius:** Men at some time are masters of their fates. / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.

Spoken by Cassius to Brutus in Act 1 Scene 2, immediately after the Colossus image and before the weighing of the two names. Learn these three lines exactly; they are among the handful of Shakespeare's sentences that have passed into everyday English.
:::

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** Men at some time are masters of their fates. / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.

**Simple English:** There are moments when men control their own destinies. The blame, dear Brutus, does not lie in the stars under which we were born; it lies in ourselves, because we allow ourselves to be inferiors.

**Difficult words:** 'at some time' — at certain moments, on some occasions (not 'always'); 'masters of their fates' — in control of their destinies; 'fault' — blame, responsibility (also, as some editors note, a 'fault' in the sense of a flaw); 'stars' — the astrological influence of the heavens at a person's birth, which Elizabethans widely believed shaped a person's fortune; 'underlings' — inferiors, subordinates, people kept beneath others.

**Literal meaning:** Men are not always helpless before destiny; at certain moments they can seize control of it. Our subjection to Caesar is not fated by the heavens but is our own doing — we have chosen to be inferior.

**Subtext:** The sentence answers an objection Brutus has not spoken: 'perhaps this is simply how things are; perhaps Caesar's rise is destiny'. Cassius forecloses that excuse. If subjection is a choice, then continuing to submit is also a choice — and a shameful one. The word "underlings" is chosen to sting; it is a servant's word, and it is spoken to a praetor of Rome.

**Hidden meaning:** Read the first line carefully: "at some time". Cassius does not claim that men are always masters of their fates; he claims that there are moments of opportunity, and his whole argument implies that this is one. That is a subtler and more dangerous claim than simple free will, because it makes inaction at the chosen moment into the fault. There is also a deep irony that the audience hears and Cassius cannot: this speech is made within minutes of a prophecy fixing the date of Caesar's death. The man who denies the stars is speaking in a play where the stars, or something like them, have already spoken. And in his own last act Cassius will reverse himself, confessing that he has come to believe in omens and dying on his birthday, as though the stars had kept their appointment after all.

**Character psychology:** Cassius here speaks as a man of will, and it is probably his sincerest moment in the scene; the contempt for passivity is real and is his own, not a performance for Brutus. His philosophy makes him effective and makes him blind: because he believes everything is choice, he cannot credit Caesar's authority with any reality beyond the Romans' cowardice, and he cannot imagine that the plot might fail for reasons outside anyone's control.

**Political meaning:** This is the founding statement of political agency in the play. A republic depends on citizens who believe they are responsible for their own condition; a monarchy is easier to accept if one believes rulers are given by heaven. Cassius's line is therefore not just a proverb but a republican argument: to blame the stars for Caesar is to accept Caesar. Elizabethan audiences may have heard something more uneasy in it, since the official doctrine of their own state held that rulers were appointed by God and that to rise against them was to rebel against heaven; Cassius's denial of the stars could sound like the voice of sedition itself.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: the historical Cassius was an Epicurean, a follower of a philosophy that denied that the gods meddle in human affairs and that regarded omens and astrology as superstition. Shakespeare could have found hints of this in Plutarch, and the line fits that temperament exactly. Belief in astrological influence was widespread in Shakespeare's England, and the play elsewhere lets characters speak as if the heavens did govern events — most strikingly in Scene 3, where the storm is read as a sign. The play as a whole does not settle the question; it stages the argument.

**Literary devices:** Aphorism, a short general truth memorably expressed; antithesis ("not in our stars, / But in ourselves"); metonymy ("stars" standing for fate or destiny); alliteration ("fault… fates"; "stars… ourselves"); the direct address "dear Brutus", which turns a general statement into personal pressure; the humiliating diction of "underlings".

**Effect:** The lines have the ring of wisdom, and that is their power: an audience and a Brutus nod to the truth of the proverb before noticing what it is being used to justify. Shakespeare gives his manipulator the play's most quotable philosophy, which is one of the ways the play refuses to make judgement easy.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the thematic hinge of Act 1. The scene began with the Soothsayer (fate) and now gives its most memorable lines to the denial of fate (free will). Everything that follows in the play — omens ignored, dreams reinterpreted, letters forged, a ghost appearing, a misread shout at Philippi — is a test of which of these two voices is right.

**Foreshadowing:** It foreshadows Cassius's own conversion to belief in omens before Philippi and his death on the day of his birth; and, in the nearer term, it foreshadows the conspirators' confidence that they can control events after the murder — a confidence that Antony's speech destroys within an hour of stage time.

**Scene connection:** The line directly answers the Soothsayer earlier in the scene, though Cassius did not hear him; the two speeches frame the first half of the scene as a debate between prophecy and will. It also connects forward to Cassius's "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius" in Scene 3, where mastery of fate is narrowed to the freedom to die.

**ICSE keywords:** fate versus free will, aphorism, antithesis, agency, responsibility, dramatic irony, republican argument, manipulation.

**Exam application:** 'Cassius's claim that "The fault… is not in our stars, / But in ourselves" is the play's clearest statement of free will, placed with deliberate irony in the very scene in which the Soothsayer has fixed the date of Caesar's death. The audience is thus asked, from the start, whether the conspirators are masters of their fates or its instruments — a question that Act 5 answers against Cassius.'

### The name-weighing passage — 'Brutus' and 'Caesar'

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** 'Brutus' and 'Caesar' — what should be in that 'Caesar'? / Why should that name be sounded more than yours? / Write them together, yours is as fair a name; / Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; / Weigh them, it is as heavy; conjure with 'em, / 'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar.'

**Simple English:** 'Brutus' and 'Caesar' — what is there in the name 'Caesar'? Why should it be spoken aloud more than yours? Write the two names side by side, and yours is just as handsome; speak them, and yours sits just as well in the mouth; weigh them, and yours is just as heavy; use them in a magic spell, and 'Brutus' will raise a spirit just as quickly as 'Caesar'.

**Difficult words:** 'sounded' — pronounced, proclaimed, spoken aloud; 'fair' — handsome, fine; 'become the mouth' — suit the mouth, be pleasant to pronounce; 'conjure' — summon spirits by magic words; ''em' — them; 'start a spirit' — raise or call up a spirit (a 'start' is a sudden movement, so the spirit leaps up at the name).

**Literal meaning:** Cassius argues that there is nothing in the name Caesar that makes it superior to the name Brutus: written, spoken, weighed or used in a spell, the two are equal.

**Subtext:** This is the mirror passage completed. There, Cassius offered to show Brutus his hidden worth; here he shows it by setting Brutus's name beside Caesar's and finding them equal. The purpose is to make Brutus imagine himself in Caesar's place — not as a tyrant but as a man of equal weight whose name could also be "sounded". It is also an answer to the fear Brutus spoke after the first shout: if the people are choosing a name to shout, why should that name not be Brutus's?

**Hidden meaning:** Cassius's argument proves the opposite of what he intends, and the audience is meant to notice. Of course there is nothing magical in the letters C-A-E-S-A-R. The power of Caesar's name is not in the name but in what Rome has agreed to attach to it: victories, authority, the habit of obedience. Cassius treats a name as a physical object — something you can write, sound, weigh — because he cannot see that political power is made of belief. Yet the passage is also strangely prophetic. The name Brutus *will* start a spirit: the forged letters will work by appealing to the name, the ancestral Brutus will be summoned in the next lines, and in Act 3 Brutus's reputation will be the thing that gives the murder its credit with the crowd. And the name Caesar will outlast the man so completely that it becomes the title of emperors — a fact Shakespeare's audience knew and the play quietly relies on.

**Character psychology:** For Brutus, who has just spoken of loving "The name of honour", this is a passage about names addressed to a man who thinks in names. Cassius has listened well. For Cassius himself, the fixation on sound and weight reveals a mind that measures men by rank and reputation, and that cannot bear to be outranked by an equal.

**Political meaning:** The passage dramatises the republican horror of a *name* that outweighs the law — the thing the tribunes feared in Scene 1 when they stripped Caesar's "trophies" from his "images". A republic is government by offices, which anyone qualified may hold; Caesar threatens to make government a matter of one proper noun. Cassius's levelling of the two names is therefore the republican principle itself: no citizen's name should be "sounded more" than another's. The irony is that his method of levelling is to raise one more name, Brutus's, into a rival magnitude.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: the Brutus family name carried real political weight in Rome because of its legendary founder, and the historical conspirators did exploit it — Plutarch records anonymous messages reproaching Brutus with his ancestor's example. The idea that names have occult power — that one can "conjure with" them — reflects Elizabethan beliefs about magic and witchcraft that were current in 1599, and should not be read as Roman religion. It is Shakespeare giving a Roman a sixteenth-century image.

**Literary devices:** Rhetorical questions ("what should be in that 'Caesar'?", "Why should that name be sounded more than yours?"); parallelism and anaphora in the four imperatives ("Write them… Sound them… Weigh them… conjure with 'em"), each followed by a clause of equality; the conceit of treating names as physical objects with weight and sound; the metaphor of magic ("conjure", "start a spirit"); repetition of the two names, which makes the audience hear them as sounds rather than as men.

**Effect:** The passage has the hypnotic quality of incantation — appropriate, since it ends in conjuring. The audience hears Cassius working the name 'Brutus' like a spell, and may feel the uneasy pleasure of watching persuasion become enchantment.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the moment the conspiracy's real strategy is spoken aloud: it will trade on the name of Brutus. Cassius says so again in his soliloquy, when he plans letters "tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name", and Casca says so in Scene 3, when he observes that Brutus's "countenance, like richest alchemy", will turn offence into virtue. The conspirators do not want Brutus's judgement; they want his name.

**Foreshadowing:** Foreshadows the forged letters, which are precisely attempts to "conjure with" the name Brutus; and Antony's funeral oration, which will conjure with the name Caesar — reading the will, displaying the body, repeating the name — far more successfully than Cassius conjures here.

**Scene connection:** Picks up Brutus's "The name of honour" from a few lines earlier and Caesar's habit, heard at the scene's opening, of using his own name as a title ("Caesar is turned to hear"). The scene is full of people treating 'Caesar' as a word with independent power; Cassius is the one who says so and the one who most resents it.

**ICSE keywords:** rhetorical question, parallelism, anaphora, conceit, incantation, name and reputation, republican equality, flattery, foreshadowing.

**Exam application:** 'By demanding why Caesar's name should "be sounded more than yours" and insisting that "'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar'", Cassius flatters Brutus into imagining himself Caesar's equal. The argument is logically empty — a name has no weight in itself — but it reveals the conspiracy's true strategy: to use the name of Brutus as a spell upon Rome.'

### "There was a Brutus once"

Between the names and the ancestor Cassius inserts a lament for Rome that you should know, because it is often set as an extract. "Now, in the names of all the gods at once, / Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great? Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" In plain English: in the name of every god together, what food has this Caesar of ours been eating to make him grow so huge? Our era should be ashamed; Rome has lost its stock of noble families. The question about "meat" continues the bodily argument — Caesar's greatness as a matter of diet, not merit — while "breed" and "bloods" shift the ground to ancestry, which is where Cassius is heading. He then complains that no previous age "since the great flood" was "famed with more than with one man", and makes his famous pun: "Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man." (In Elizabethan pronunciation Rome and room sounded alike; the joke is that the city is now spacious because only one man counts as living in it.) All of this is the run-up to the sentence that finally makes Brutus speak.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Cassius:** O, you and I have heard our fathers say / There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king.

**Simple English:** Oh, you and I have both heard our fathers say that there was once a Brutus who would have put up with the devil himself holding court in Rome as readily as he would have put up with a king — that is, he would have tolerated neither.

**Difficult words:** 'our fathers' — our ancestors, the older generation; 'a Brutus once' — Lucius Junius Brutus, the legendary founder of the Roman Republic; 'brooked' — tolerated, endured; 'Th' eternal devil' — the devil himself, Satan; 'keep his state' — hold court, maintain his royal dignity and power; 'As easily as a king' — as readily as he would have tolerated a king (the sentence is ironic: he would have tolerated neither).

**Literal meaning:** The ancestral Brutus hated kingship so much that for him a king in Rome was as intolerable as the devil in Rome.

**Subtext:** This is the last and heaviest weight Cassius places on the scale: not Rome's opinion, not Caesar's weakness, not the equality of names, but *family*. He tells Brutus that his own blood has already decided the question. The appeal to "our fathers" makes the obligation hereditary and oral — something Brutus has heard at home since childhood — and therefore impossible to argue against without disowning his own name.

**Hidden meaning:** The sentence contains the whole conspiracy in miniature, and it contains the conspiracy's confusion too. The ancestral Brutus expelled a *king*, Tarquin; Caesar is not a king and has, we learn minutes later, refused a crown three times. Cassius's analogy smuggles in the conclusion that Caesar is a king already. Brutus will inherit this confusion in Act 2, reasoning that Caesar must be killed not for what he is but for what the crown "would" make him. The comparison of a king to "Th' eternal devil" is also telling: it treats monarchy not as a policy to be opposed but as an absolute evil to be exterminated, and that absolutism is what makes the murder, rather than opposition, seem to follow.

**Character psychology:** Cassius saves the ancestor for last because he has measured Brutus accurately: this is the appeal that will work. Brutus's reply confirms it. For the first time he does not deflect; he admits that he has "thought of this, and of these times". The chord Cassius strikes is pride of lineage disguised as duty — a chord Brutus himself will strike in his Act 2 soliloquy, where he recalls that his ancestors drove the Tarquin from the streets of Rome.

**Political meaning:** Rome's whole political identity rested on a founding act of expulsion: the republic was defined as the state that had no king. To invoke the first Brutus is to invoke the constitution itself. But the invocation also shows how the republican cause has shrunk to a matter of family memory. Cassius does not argue that the republic is good; he argues that the Brutuses are against kings. Politics has become genealogy.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Lucius Junius Brutus led the expulsion of the last king of Rome, Tarquin the Proud, traditionally in 509 BC, and became one of the first two consuls; Roman tradition made him the embodiment of republican liberty, and Marcus Brutus's family claimed descent from him (ancient and modern writers have doubted the descent, but the Romans of 44 BC treated it as fact and used it). Plutarch records that the people reminded Brutus of his ancestor by writings left on the praetor's tribunal and on the statue of old Brutus — the historical seed of the letters Cassius will forge. The "eternal devil" is a Christian idea placed in pagan Rome: an anachronism that Shakespeare's audience would not have noticed and that an examiner will be pleased to see you notice.

**Literary devices:** Historical allusion (to Lucius Junius Brutus); hyperbole and irony (the devil as easily tolerated as a king — meaning neither); anachronism ("Th' eternal devil"); the appeal to authority and tradition ("our fathers say"); the rhetorical climax, since this is the final and strongest point of a long speech; and, in the lines before it, apostrophe ("Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost") and the pun on Rome and "room enough".

**Effect:** The speech ends not with a flourish but with a quiet sentence about what the fathers used to say — and it is the quiet sentence that lands. The audience senses that Cassius has stopped performing and found the thing that will actually move Brutus.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the sentence that breaks Brutus's silence. For some fifty lines he has said almost nothing while Cassius talked; now he answers at length. Dramatically, the ancestor is the key that turns the lock.

**Foreshadowing:** Foreshadows the letters set up "with wax / Upon old Brutus' statue" in Scene 3; Brutus's own appeal to his ancestry in the orchard soliloquy; and the way the conspirators will present the murder as a re-enactment of the founding expulsion of the kings — a story the crowd accepts for exactly as long as it takes Antony to tell a better one.

**Scene connection:** Connects back to the weighing of names (the name Brutus now acquires its history) and forward to Cinna's wish in Scene 3 that Cassius could "win the noble Brutus to our party"; the ancestor is what makes Brutus's name worth winning.

**ICSE keywords:** allusion, ancestry, lineage, anachronism, irony, appeal to tradition, republican founding myth, incitement, climax.

**Exam application:** 'Cassius ends with the one appeal he has held in reserve: "There was a Brutus once" who would no more have "brooked" a king in Rome than "Th' eternal devil". By turning Brutus's ancestry into an obligation, he makes inaction look like a betrayal of blood — and it is this, not the stories of Caesar's weakness, that finally draws an answer from Brutus.'

::: history The first Brutus and the hatred of kings
Roman tradition held that in 509 BC Lucius Junius Brutus, a nephew of the king, led the revolt that drove out Tarquin the Proud after the outrage done to Lucretia, and that he swore the Romans never again to allow a king. He became one of the first two consuls and, in the most famous story about him, condemned his own sons to death for plotting to restore the monarchy. From then on 'rex' was the most hated word in Roman politics; to call a man a would-be king was to call for his removal. Marcus Brutus's family kept a statue of the founder among their ancestral images, and the Romans of 44 BC — as Plutarch tells it — left messages at that statue asking whether the living Brutus was asleep. Shakespeare knew all this from North's Plutarch, and he builds the whole appeal of this speech on it. Keep the two Brutuses separate in your answers: 'the ancestral Brutus' (Lucius Junius) and 'Brutus' (Marcus Junius, the character). Confusing them is a common error.
:::

## 7. Brutus Answers: The Guarded Reply and the Villager

### Brutus's guarded reply — "That you do love me, I am nothing jealous"

After all that, Brutus speaks. What he says is a masterpiece of controlled courtesy, and you must read it slowly, because every clause is doing two things at once: acknowledging Cassius and holding him off.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** That you do love me, I am nothing jealous. / What you would work me to, I have some aim. / How I have thought of this, and of these times, / I shall recount hereafter. For this present, / I would not, so with love I might entreat you, / Be any further moved.

> **Brutus:** What you have said / I will consider; what you have to say / I will with patience hear, and find a time / Both meet to hear and answer such high things.

**Simple English:** I have no doubt at all that you love me. I have some idea of what you are trying to persuade me to do. How I have thought about this matter, and about the times we live in, I will tell you later. For now — and I ask this of you as a friend — I would rather not be pressed any further. What you have said I will think over; what you still have to say I will listen to patiently; and I will find a time that is right both for hearing and for answering such weighty matters.

**Difficult words:** 'nothing jealous' — not at all doubtful, not suspicious (in Shakespeare's English 'jealous' often means 'suspicious, mistrustful'); 'work me to' — persuade me towards, manoeuvre me into; 'aim' — guess, conjecture; 'recount' — relate, tell; 'hereafter' — later; 'For this present' — for the present moment; 'so with love I might entreat you' — if I may ask you as a friend; 'moved' — urged, pressed (also, in the background, 'moved' emotionally); 'with patience' — patiently; 'meet' — fitting, suitable; 'high things' — weighty, serious, dangerous matters.

**Literal meaning:** Brutus assures Cassius of his trust, admits he understands the drift of Cassius's speech, says he has already been thinking about these matters, declines to be pressed further today, and promises to consider what has been said and to find a proper time to hear and answer the rest.

**Subtext:** Two words give the game away. "aim" — Brutus knows exactly what Cassius is driving at, and says so without saying it. "hereafter" — he does not refuse; he postpones. A man who intended to refuse would refuse. Brutus has, in effect, agreed to a second meeting on the subject of removing Caesar, while saying nothing that could be repeated against him. He also tells Cassius something Cassius badly wanted to know: that he had already "thought of this, and of these times" before Cassius said a word. The war within him, in other words, was about Caesar all along.

**Hidden meaning:** Look at the balance of the sentences — "What you have said / I will consider; what you have to say / I will with patience hear" — and at the pairing "Both meet to hear and answer". Brutus's mind works in symmetries, in weighed clauses; it is the mind of a judge, not a conspirator, and he will bring the same judicial balance to the orchard soliloquy and, fatally, to the decision to spare Antony. Notice also that he describes Cassius's project as "such high things": the phrase dignifies it. Brutus is already, in his diction, treating the overthrow of Caesar as a lofty matter rather than a crime.

**Character psychology:** This speech shows Brutus's two great qualities and his two great weaknesses in one breath. The qualities: honesty (he will not pretend he has not understood) and self-command (he will not be stampeded). The weaknesses: a tendency to postpone decisions he has already half made, and a trust in friends that he states as a point of honour — "I am nothing jealous" — at the very moment a friend is manipulating him. He is not deceived about Cassius's aim; he is deceived about Cassius's motive, and he never asks about it.

**Political meaning:** The speech is the republican conscience taking its time. It shows that Brutus will not act on a friend's resentment or a crowd's shout; he needs reasons, and he needs to supply them himself. That is admirable, and it is also the reason the conspiracy will take on Brutus's cast of mind: deliberate, principled, and blind to the practical.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch describes Brutus as a man who weighed the plot against Caesar long and carefully, torn between his obligations to Caesar, who had pardoned and promoted him, and the republican tradition of his family; he also records that Cassius had to approach Brutus more than once. Shakespeare's 'not today, but later' compresses that slow process into a single polite paragraph.

**Literary devices:** Antithesis and balanced clauses ("What you have said / I will consider; what you have to say / I will with patience hear"); formal, Latinate diction ("recount hereafter", "entreat", "meet"); understatement ("I have some aim"); the courteous conditional ("so with love I might entreat you"); the elevating phrase "high things".

**Effect:** After fifty lines of Cassius's heat, Brutus's coolness is a relief and a worry at once. The audience admires the restraint and notices that it is not a refusal. The scene's temperature drops, and the plot has nevertheless advanced.

**Dramatic significance:** This is the first commitment Brutus makes — a commitment to keep listening — and in a tragedy that is enough. Cassius knows it, as his reply shows. From here the question is no longer whether Brutus will be approached again but when.

**Foreshadowing:** "hereafter" foreshadows the night visit of the conspirators in Act 2 and the orchard soliloquy in which Brutus 'recounts' his thinking to himself; "find a time / Both meet to hear and answer" is fulfilled when the conspirators arrive at his house before dawn.

**Scene connection:** The earlier admission "with himself at war" is now explained: the war was about "these times". Cassius's soliloquy at the end of the scene will treat this reply as proof that Brutus's "honourable mettle may be wrought" — bent, worked — using the very verb Brutus used here, "work me to".

**ICSE keywords:** restraint, deliberation, postponement, antithesis, judicial mind, trust, dramatic irony, understatement.

**Exam application:** 'Brutus's reply is guarded but not a refusal: he admits "What you would work me to, I have some aim", confesses he has already "thought of this, and of these times", and merely asks not to be "any further moved" for the present. His courteous postponement is, in effect, consent to be approached again — the first step of his tragedy.'

### "Brutus had rather be a villager"

Brutus does not stop at postponement. He gives Cassius something to carry away — a sentence to "chew upon" — and it is the most committed thing he says in the whole scene.

**Original line / short passage:**

> **Brutus:** Till then, my noble friend, chew upon this: / Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us.

> **Cassius:** I am glad that my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus.

**Simple English:** Brutus: Until then, my noble friend, think this over: Brutus would rather be a simple country villager than call himself a citizen of Rome under the harsh conditions that these times are likely to impose on us. Cassius: I am glad that my feeble words have struck even this much of a spark from Brutus.

**Difficult words:** 'Till then' — until that later time; 'chew upon' — think over, ruminate on (the image is of slow digestion); 'had rather' — would rather, would prefer; 'villager' — an inhabitant of a village, a rustic with no civic standing; 'repute himself' — consider himself, call himself; 'a son of Rome' — a Roman citizen, with the pride the phrase implies; 'hard conditions' — harsh terms (as in the terms of a surrender or a contract); 'Is like to' — is likely to; 'lay upon us' — impose on us; 'weak words' — feeble speech (false modesty); 'struck… show of fire' — produced a visible spark, as steel struck on flint does; 'but thus much' — even this much, only this much.

**Literal meaning:** Brutus declares that he would rather give up Roman citizenship and live as a nobody in a village than be a Roman under the oppression that seems to be coming. Cassius expresses satisfaction that his words have drawn even this much passion from Brutus.

**Subtext:** This is as near as Brutus comes to a political statement in Act 1, and it is still oblique. He does not name Caesar; he says "these hard conditions" and "this time". But the meaning is unmistakable to Cassius and to us: Brutus regards the coming kingship as a condition under which Roman life would not be worth living as a Roman. For a man who has just been reminded of his ancestor, 'I would rather be a villager' is the voice of the republican tradition — citizenship under a king is not citizenship. Cassius's reply, meanwhile, is quietly jubilant beneath its modesty. 'Weak words' is a flourish; he knows they were not weak. "Fire" is what he wanted, and he has got a "show" of it.

**Hidden meaning:** Two images are hidden in the plain words. First, "chew upon this" turns the political thought into food — picking up, with grim appropriateness, Cassius's question about what "meat" Caesar feeds on. Brutus feeds Cassius a thought in return. Second, Cassius's "struck… fire" is an image of flint and steel: Brutus is the flint, hard and cold, from which a spark can be struck only by striking. The image concedes something important — that the fire is Brutus's own, latent in him, and that Cassius's role is only to strike. In his soliloquy minutes later Cassius will reach for a related image, Brutus's "honourable mettle", where 'mettle' means temperament and puns on 'metal'. The two images belong together: a metal that can be struck for fire, and worked into a new shape.

**Character psychology:** Brutus here shows the idealist's absolutism. He does not weigh degrees of oppression; he poses a stark either–or (villager or son of Rome) and declares his preference. The same cast of mind will make him see the choice about Caesar as absolute in Act 2 and will make him unable to compromise with Cassius in Act 4. Note too the third person once more — "Brutus had rather" — the public self speaking. Cassius, for his part, shows the skill of a man who knows when to stop. He does not press for more; he banks the spark.

**Political meaning:** The sentence defines what Roman citizenship meant to a republican: not residence in the city but participation as a free equal. Under a king, the "son of Rome" becomes a subject, no better than a "villager" — so one might as well be a villager honestly. This is the libertas of the Roman tradition speaking: freedom as the condition of living under laws one shares in making, rather than under the will of one man. Brutus has, without naming him, pronounced Caesar's rule incompatible with Roman life.

**Historical context:** HISTORICAL RECORD: Plutarch's Brutus, in the months before the Ides, was in fact Caesar's favoured praetor and designated for a consulship; the historical man's alienation was slower and more complex than this sentence suggests, and Shakespeare compresses it. The opposition of city and village had a special resonance for Romans, whose word for citizen (civis) and for the state (civitas) both came from the city; to leave the city was, in a sense, to leave politics altogether. In Shakespeare's England the word 'villager' also carried the sense of a person of no account, which sharpens the sacrifice Brutus says he would make.

**Literary devices:** Metaphor ("chew upon this"; "struck… show of fire"); antithesis ("villager" against "son of Rome"); illeism ("Brutus had rather"); euphemism or indirection ("these hard conditions", "this time" for Caesar's rule); ironic understatement in "my weak words"; the double meaning latent in "show of fire" — a genuine flash, but also only a 'show', an appearance.

**Effect:** Brutus's sentence lands with the weight of a vow, and the audience hears, for the first time, that the honourable man is on the move. Cassius's reply lets the audience share his satisfaction, which is uncomfortable, because it is the satisfaction of a successful fisherman.

**Dramatic significance:** This closes the first movement of the scene. The procession went out with Brutus unapproached; it returns with Brutus declared, in principle, against the coming kingship. In the space of one offstage race the conspiracy has acquired the man it needs. Caesar's return at this exact moment is Shakespeare's irony: the victim re-enters just as his destroyer has been won over.

**Foreshadowing:** "a son of Rome" under "hard conditions" foreshadows Brutus's justification of the murder to the crowd — that he loved Rome more than he loved Caesar — and his claim to have killed Caesar for Rome's liberty. The spark Cassius has struck foreshadows the "fire" imagery of the storm scene and the conflagration of Act 3, when the mob carries firebrands through the city. The 'villager' who would rather lose Rome than live under a king will, at Philippi, lose both Rome and his life.

**Scene connection:** Brutus's "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well", spoken after the first shout, has now hardened into "Brutus had rather be a villager": the scene traces the movement from divided feeling to stated principle. Cassius's soliloquy at the scene's end — "Well, Brutus, thou art noble; yet I see / Thy honourable mettle may be wrought" — is his private assessment of the "show of fire" he has just struck.

**ICSE keywords:** republicanism, citizenship, libertas, idealism, antithesis, illeism, metaphor (flint and fire; chewing), commitment, turning point.

**Exam application:** 'Brutus's parting declaration that he "had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome" under the coming "hard conditions" is his first political commitment in the play: without naming Caesar he pronounces life under a king not worth living as a Roman. Cassius's satisfied reply that his "weak words" have struck a "show of fire" confirms that the first movement of the seduction has succeeded.'

## 8. Where Part One Ends

Stop here and look back at the ground covered, because the pause is where the learning happens. When Caesar's procession left the stage, Brutus was a troubled man who wanted to be alone. When it returns, he has admitted a fear, confessed a love, defined his honour, heard his ancestor invoked and promised to hear more. Nothing has been decided; everything has been prepared. That is the pattern of a Shakespearean first act, and Scene 2 is its finest example in this play: a long conversation in which almost nothing happens and the whole tragedy becomes possible.

Three things you should now be able to do without notes. First, trace Cassius's sequence — hurt friend, mirror, shout, honour, Tiber, fever, Colossus, stars, names, ancestor — and say what each move was for. Second, state Brutus's position in his own words at three points: "with himself at war"; "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well"; "Brutus had rather be a villager". Third, explain why the Soothsayer's one sentence at the start of the scene changes how you hear Cassius's claim that men are "masters of their fates".

::: icse How this half-scene is used in examination answers
Be clear about what is examined where. Act 1 is the Class IX portion, so a direct extract from this half of the scene — the Soothsayer exchange, the mirror lines, the Tiber story, the Colossus, the names, the ancestral Brutus — belongs in Class IX internal papers, where the usual sub-questions ask who is speaking to whom and where, what has just happened, what a phrase means in your own words, what the lines reveal about the speaker, and what happens next. For the Class X board paper (Acts III–V), this material works as *evidence* rather than as the extract itself. When a question on Act 3 asks why Brutus joined the conspiracy, the honest answer reaches back to his admission here that he feared the people would "Choose Caesar for their king" and that he loved "The name of honour more than I fear death". When a question on Act 4 or 5 asks about Cassius's character or his change of heart about omens, "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars" is the line to set against his later fatalism. When a question asks what Antony means by Brutus's nobility, the mirror, the names and the ancestor explain why the conspirators wanted him. Never claim a question is certain to come; show the examiner that you know where every Act 3–5 motive began.
