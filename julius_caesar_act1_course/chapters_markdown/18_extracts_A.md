# Extract-Based Training (Part One): Scene 1 and the First Half of Scene 2

*This chapter turns everything you have learned about Act 1 into the one skill that examiners actually reward: reading a short passage and answering five graded questions on it. It takes six extracts from Scene 1 and the first half of Scene 2, works each one through a fixed thirteen-label analysis, then sets a full ICSE-style question with model answers and an explanation of exactly why each answer scores. You will also learn a 90-second method for an unseen extract. Work through the extracts in order; each one trains a move the next one assumes.*

## How This Chapter Trains You

An extract question hands you ten to fourteen lines and five sub-questions. In the 2025 ICSE board paper the drama extracts carried 16 marks, split [3], [3], [3], [3], [4]. The first sub-questions test whether you know where you are in the play; the middle ones test whether you understand the words and can explain why they are spoken; the last one asks you to judge, compare or respond. The skill is the same whether the extract comes from Act 1 or Act 5.

**EXAM-ORIENTED ADVICE:** Be clear about the syllabus split. Act 1 belongs to the Class IX portion of the ICSE syllabus, examined by your school, usually through 'reference to the context' questions. The Class X board paper examines Acts III, IV and V. So the six extracts in this chapter are not board extracts. They matter for two honest reasons. First, the technique of reading an extract is identical, and Act 1 passages are the ideal training ground because you already know the story. Second, the board paper constantly assumes Act 1: the 2025 paper, for instance, asked candidates what 'the work the ides of March begun' referred to, which is unanswerable without the Soothsayer, the Lupercal and the conspiracy of Act 1. Every Act 3 question about why Brutus joined, why Cassius hates Caesar or why the crowd is fickle is really an Act 1 question in disguise.

### The thirteen labels and the five levels

Every extract below is worked through the same thirteen labels. They are not a ritual; each one answers a question an examiner is silently asking. The table shows how they map onto the five levels this book teaches.

| Label | Level | What the examiner is really testing |
|---|---|---|
| Context, Speaker, Listener, Before, After | Level 1 — What happened? | Can you place the lines precisely and know what surrounds them? |
| Meaning | Level 2 — What does it mean? | Can you turn Shakespeare's English into your own clear English? |
| Subtext, Character, Device | Level 3 — Why did Shakespeare write it this way? | Can you see what is not said, what the words reveal, how the effect is built? |
| Theme, Historical context, Scene connection | Level 4 — How does it connect to the whole play? | Can you link a moment to the play's ideas, its sources and its later scenes? |
| Keywords | Level 5 — How do I use it in an ICSE answer? | Do you own the precise vocabulary that makes an answer sound like literature? |

Do the labels in order when you practise. A student who jumps to 'theme' before securing 'before' and 'after' writes clever answers about the wrong moment, and loses marks for accuracy that no amount of insight can win back.

::: insight How to read an unseen extract in 90 seconds
Trained readers do not read an extract from the first word to the last and then hope. They interrogate it in four passes, and the whole process takes about a minute and a half.

**First pass (0–15 seconds) — the labels.** Read only the speaker labels and any names inside the speech. Who is talking, to whom, and who is named? If Pompey is named, you are in Scene 1; if a shout is heard, you are outside the Lupercal games in Scene 2; if the ides are named, the Soothsayer is near.

**Second pass (15–35 seconds) — the hinge.** Every extract has one line the paper will hang a question on: usually an image (feathers, mirror, Colossus) or a proverb-like statement (the fault is not in our stars). Find it and underline it. This is where the 'explain' question and the 'device' question will come from.

**Third pass (35–60 seconds) — the edges.** Look at the first and last lines and ask what must have just happened and what must happen next. The 'before' and 'after' marks live at the edges.

**Fourth pass (60–90 seconds) — the margin note.** Write three words in the margin: one character quality, one theme, one device. You now have the skeleton of all five answers before you write a sentence. Only then do you read the questions, because you read them as someone who already knows what the passage is for.
:::

::: icse How marks are distributed across a five-part extract question
In the 2025 paper the pattern was [3][3][3][3][4]. Read the pattern as a ladder. The two or three 3-mark questions at the top are usually recall and understanding: where are we, what has just happened, what does a phrase mean. A 3-mark question in the middle is often application or analysis: why does the speaker say this, what does it reveal about him, what is the tone. The final 4-mark question is evaluative or comparative: how far do you agree, which argument was strongest, what does the passage prepare us for. A 3-mark answer needs three distinct, correct points; a 4-mark answer needs four, or three developed points with a supported judgement. Never write one long paragraph for a 3-mark question and one line for the 4-mark one. Nothing in this box is a promise about a future paper; it is a description of a verified paper and the habits that fit it.

## Extract 1 — The Tribunes Meet the Cobbler

::: extract Extract 1 — Act 1 Scene 1, the opening lines of the play
> **Flavius:** Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!  
> Is this a holiday? What, know you not,  
> Being mechanical, you ought not walk  
> Upon a labouring day without the sign  
> Of your profession?—Speak, what trade art thou?
>
> **Carpenter:** Why, sir, a carpenter.
>
> **Marullus:** Where is thy leather apron and thy rule?  
> What dost thou with thy best apparel on?—  
> You, sir, what trade are you?
>
> **Cobbler:** Truly, sir, in respect of a fine workman, I am but, as you would say, a cobbler.
>
> **Marullus:** But what trade art thou? Answer me directly.
>
> **Cobbler:** A trade, sir, that I hope I may use with a safe conscience, which is indeed, sir, a mender of bad soles.
:::

**Context:** These are the first words of the play. The setting is a street in Rome on a public holiday. Working men have put on their best clothes and come out to see Julius Caesar return in triumph; Shakespeare's Rome is celebrating the victory over the sons of Pompey and, on the same day, keeping the feast of Lupercal. Two tribunes, Flavius and Marullus, are trying to clear the streets. Nothing has been explained to the audience yet; we learn who is who, and what the holiday is for, by watching this quarrel.

**Speaker:** Flavius opens; Marullus takes over the questioning; the Carpenter and the Cobbler answer. Note the division of labour between the tribunes: Flavius gives orders, Marullus interrogates. Both are officials, and both are angry.

**Listener:** The commoners on stage, and behind them the theatre audience, who are in exactly the same position as the crowd: idle, festive, and about to be told that their holiday is a disgrace.

**Before:** Nothing precedes this exchange in the play. What precedes it in the story is Caesar's victory at Munda in Spain over the sons of Pompey, the triumph granted for it, and the death of Pompey himself years earlier. All of this the tribunes will pour out in a moment, but here it is only implied by the words holiday and triumph.

**After:** The Cobbler keeps sliding out of Flavius's grip with puns: he begs him "be not out with me", adds "Yet if you be out, sir, I can mend you", claims "all that I live by is with the awl" and calls himself "a surgeon to old shoes". Flavius finally asks the useful question, "But wherefore art not in thy shop today?", and the Cobbler tells the truth: "we make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph." That single sentence releases Marullus's furious speech about Pompey (Extract 2) and the tribunes' plan to strip Caesar's images (Extract 3).

**Meaning:** In plain English, Flavius says: 'Get out of here, go home, you lazy people. Is this a holiday? Do you not know that as working men you are not allowed to walk about on a working day without wearing the tools and clothes of your trade? You, what is your job?' The Carpenter answers straight. Marullus then demands to know why he is not wearing his leather apron and carrying his rule (a measuring stick), and why he has his best clothes on. The Cobbler's first answer is a joke at his own expense: compared with a really fine craftsman, he is only what you would call a cobbler, a word that in Shakespeare's English meant both a shoe-mender and a clumsy bungler. Told to answer directly, he says his trade is one he can practise with a clear conscience, because he mends bad soles. Difficult words: mechanical (a manual worker, someone who works with his hands), apparel (clothing), rule (a measuring rule), in respect of (compared with), directly (plainly, without evasion).

**Subtext:** The tribunes are not really angry about aprons. The clothes are a pretext for a political objection that they cannot yet say aloud to a crowd: that celebrating this triumph means celebrating the defeat of Romans by a Roman, and that a man who is cheered like a king soon becomes one. The Cobbler's evasions are not stupidity. He understands perfectly that he is being bullied, and he answers a bullying question with a slippery one. When he says he works with a safe conscience, he is quietly claiming the moral high ground: his trade harms no one, unlike, perhaps, the trade of politics. The exchange dramatises a Rome in which those who should speak for the people are shouting at the people, and the people are answering back with wit rather than obedience.

**Character:** Flavius is peremptory (commanding in an abrupt way): four imperatives in his first line and a half. Marullus is the sharper questioner, but he too resorts to insult when the Cobbler resists. The Carpenter is the plain, obedient citizen. The Cobbler is the scene's most intelligent presence: self-mocking, unhurried, unafraid, and entirely in control of the words. Shakespeare gives the wit to the working man and the temper to the officials.

**Theme:** The extract opens three of the play's great themes at once. Public opinion and the crowd: Rome's people are already seen as fickle, dressing up for the man who beat their former favourite. Authority and liberty: the tribunes' power to order citizens off the street is the republic's power, and the crowd's cheerful disobedience is its weakness. Appearance versus reality: the argument is literally about clothing, about what a man wears and what he is, and this question of surfaces will run through the whole play.

**Device:** The most important device is the contrast of prose and verse. Flavius and Marullus speak blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter, ten syllables with a rising rhythm); the Carpenter and Cobbler speak prose. Shakespeare uses this contrast to mark class and also temperature: verse for public authority and passion, prose for street-level realism and humour. Within the prose the Cobbler puns: soles and souls sound alike, so a mender of bad soles is also a mender of bad souls, a joke that turns him into a kind of priest to the tribunes. Flavius's opening is built from imperatives and a rhetorical question ("Is this a holiday?"), which is not a request for information but a rebuke.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Tribunes of the plebs were Roman officers created, by tradition, in 494 BC to protect ordinary citizens against the aristocracy; they were sacrosanct and could veto legislation. That is the irony of the opening: the people's protectors are scolding the people. The real tribunes Gaius Epidius Marullus and Lucius Caesetius Flavus did clash with Caesar in 44 BC, removing a diadem that had been placed on his statue and arresting men who hailed him as king; Caesar had them deposed. Caesar's Spanish triumph, celebrated in October 45 BC for the victory at Munda, was resented because it celebrated a victory over fellow Romans. Shakespeare compresses that triumph and the Lupercalia of 15 February 44 BC into one day. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** The rule that a working man must wear the sign of his trade on a working day sounds more like Shakespeare's London than ancient Rome; Shakespeare habitually dresses Roman commoners in Elizabethan habits so that his audience will recognise themselves.

**Scene connection:** The crowd that dresses up here is the crowd Casca will describe hooting and clapping at Caesar's refusal of the crown later in Scene 2, the crowd Antony will turn against the conspirators in Act 3, and the mob that tears Cinna the poet apart. The tribunes who clear the streets here are, by the end of Scene 2, reported as "put to silence" for pulling scarves off Caesar's images: the first hint that opposing Caesar has a cost. And Flavius's fear that Caesar will keep Rome "in servile fearfulness" is the seed of Cassius's argument to Brutus that Romans have become underlings.

**Keywords:** exposition; tribunes; plebeians; mechanicals; prose versus blank verse; pun; class tension; fickle crowd; peremptory; evasion; comic relief with political weight; sumptuary detail; anachronism.

Read the extract and answer the following questions.

**(i)** Where does this scene take place, and on what occasion? Who are Flavius and Marullus, and why are they angry with the men they meet? [3]

**(ii)** Explain in your own words what Flavius means by "Being mechanical, you ought not walk / Upon a labouring day without the sign / Of your profession". What do Marullus's questions about the "leather apron" and "best apparel" add to the argument? [3]

**(iii)** The Cobbler calls himself "a mender of bad soles". Explain the pun, and show, with reference to what the Cobbler says immediately after the extract, how he continues to play with words. [3]

**(iv)** Contrast the way the tribunes speak with the way the commoners speak in this extract. What does Shakespeare achieve through this contrast? [3]

**(v)** Flavius calls the commoners "idle creatures" and treats them as fools. How far does the rest of Act 1 justify the tribunes' low opinion of the Roman crowd? Support your answer with reference to the text. [4]

**Reference to the context (Class IX style)**

**(a)** "Hence! Home, you idle creatures, get you home!" Who speaks these words, to whom, and on what occasion? What tone does the line set for the play?

**(b)** "A trade, sir, that I hope I may use with a safe conscience". Who is speaking? Explain the phrase safe conscience and say what it suggests about the speaker's attitude to the man questioning him.

**Multiple-choice questions**

2. Flavius and Marullus hold the office of: (a) consul (b) senator (c) tribune (d) priest of the Lupercal

::: model Model answer (i)
The scene is set in a street in Rome. It is a public holiday: Caesar is returning to the city in triumph after defeating the sons of Pompey, and the feast of Lupercal is being kept on the same day. Flavius and Marullus are tribunes, elected officers whose duty is to protect the interests of the common people. They are angry because the working men have left their shops on what should be a working day, and are walking about in their best clothes without the tools or dress of their trades. Beneath this lies a deeper anger: the men are celebrating Caesar, whose growing power the tribunes fear.

**Why this answer earns marks:** It gives the three things the question asks for in three clear sentences: place and occasion (context), the office of the two speakers (recall with the correct term tribune), and the reason for their anger. It then adds the one piece of insight that lifts a full-mark answer, the political motive behind the complaint about clothes, without wandering into the later speech.
:::

::: model Model answer (ii)
Flavius is telling the men that, as manual workers, they have no right to be wandering the streets on a working day unless they are dressed for work and carrying the signs of their trade. In his view a holiday declared by the crowd for itself is no holiday at all. Marullus sharpens the point with specific details: the Carpenter should have on his leather apron and should be carrying his rule, the measuring stick of his craft, but instead he is wearing his best clothes. The two questions turn a general rebuke into a piece of evidence. The men's fine clothes prove that they have deliberately chosen to treat Caesar's return as a festival, and it is this choice, not the clothes themselves, that the tribunes resent.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The first half is a genuine paraphrase in the student's own words, which is what an 'explain' question demands; simply repeating the quotation would earn nothing. The word mechanical is correctly glossed. The second half answers the second part of the question directly, explaining what the apron and rule add (specific evidence), and ends with a short piece of analysis about what the tribunes really object to.
:::

::: model Model answer (iii)
The pun rests on the identical sound of soles and souls. A cobbler mends the soles of shoes; but a mender of bad souls is a priest or a moral reformer. The Cobbler therefore answers an insulting question with a joke that makes him sound like a spiritual doctor to the very officials who are bullying him. He goes on in the same vein. When Flavius grows angrier, he says "be not out with me", meaning do not be angry, but adds "Yet if you be out, sir, I can mend you", where out now means having worn-out shoes. He says that "all that I live by is with the awl", playing on awl and all, and calls himself "a surgeon to old shoes" who can "recover" them, which means both to heal and to re-cover with new leather.

**Why this answer earns marks:** It explains the pun precisely (terminology: pun, two meanings named), it obeys the instruction to refer to the lines immediately after the extract, and it quotes short, accurate phrases rather than long chunks. Each quotation is followed by an explanation of the double meaning, which shows understanding rather than memory. Three developed points comfortably cover three marks.
:::

::: model Model answer (iv)
The tribunes speak in blank verse, the elevated rhythm Shakespeare gives to people of rank and to moments of passion; their lines are full of commands and angry questions, such as "Is this a holiday?" and "Speak, what trade art thou?". The commoners answer in prose, the everyday language of the street, and the Cobbler's prose is relaxed, witty and full of puns. Through this contrast Shakespeare marks the social gap between officials and workers, and at the same time reverses our expectations: the men with power lose their tempers, while the man without power keeps his head and controls the conversation with words. The contrast also sets the play's tone, beginning a tragedy about the fate of Rome with comedy from the very Romans whose opinion will decide that fate.

**Why this answer earns marks:** It names the device accurately (blank verse versus prose), supports the contrast with short quotations, and then answers the harder second part with two distinct effects, social and dramatic, rather than one. The final sentence connects the technique to the play as a whole, which is the move that separates a competent answer from an excellent one.
:::

::: model Model answer (v)
To a large extent Act 1 confirms the tribunes' poor view of the crowd, though not their contempt. Marullus reminds the people that they once climbed to "chimney tops" with their "infants in your arms" to cheer Pompey, and now "strew flowers" for the man who triumphs "over Pompey's blood"; their loyalty has simply changed sides with success. When rebuked, they "vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness", too ashamed to argue, which suggests they had not thought about what they were celebrating. Later in Scene 2 Casca reports the same crowd hooting, clapping and throwing up their sweaty nightcaps as Caesar refuses the crown, behaving, he says, as they do to the players in the theatre. The people respond to spectacle, not to principle. Yet the Cobbler's wit shows that individual commoners are far from stupid, and the tribunes' own aggressive behaviour hardly earns them the people's trust. Act 1 justifies the fear that the crowd can be swayed by anyone with a chariot or a crown; it does not justify calling them senseless.

**Why this answer earns marks:** A 4-mark evaluation needs a clear judgement, evidence from more than one place, and a balancing point. This answer gives a verdict in the first sentence, supports it with three pieces of evidence from Scene 1 and Scene 2 (quotation and connection), and then qualifies it with a counter-argument drawn from the extract itself. The closing sentence returns to the exact wording of the question, distinguishing fickle from senseless, which shows the examiner that the student is answering the question set and not a general one about the mob.
:::

::: model Model answers — reference to the context and MCQs
**(a)** Flavius, one of the two tribunes of the people, speaks these words to a group of working men in a Rome street on the day of Caesar's triumph, which coincides with the feast of Lupercal. The line is a string of commands and an insult; it sets a tone of public disorder and official irritation, and tells the audience at once that this Rome is a divided city in which the people and their officers want different things.

**(b)** The Cobbler is speaking to Marullus, who has ordered him to name his trade plainly. A safe conscience is a clear conscience: he means his work is honest and harms no one, so he need not be ashamed of it. The remark is quietly pointed. He implies that he, unlike some, has nothing to hide, and he turns a demand for information into a small assertion of his own respectability.

**Why these answers earn marks:** Both give speaker, listener and occasion in one sentence before explaining, which is the shape a 'reference to the context' answer must take. Each ends with one sentence of interpretation, so the answer shows understanding as well as recall.

**MCQ key:** 1 (b); 2 (c); 3 (d). Distractors to notice: (a) in question 1 is the modern meaning of mechanical, not Shakespeare's; (b) in question 3 is a half-truth, since the Lupercal is mentioned but the Cobbler names Caesar's triumph as the reason.

## Extract 2 — Marullus Remembers Pompey

::: extract Extract 2 — Act 1 Scene 1, Marullus turns on the crowd
> **Marullus:** Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home?  
> What tributaries follow him to Rome  
> To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?  
> You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!  
> O you hard hearts, you cruel men of Rome,  
> Knew you not Pompey? Many a time and oft  
> Have you climbed up to walls and battlements,  
> To towers and windows, yea, to chimney tops,  
> Your infants in your arms, and there have sat  
> The livelong day, with patient expectation,  
> To see great Pompey pass the streets of Rome.  
> And when you saw his chariot but appear,  
> Have you not made an universal shout,
:::

**Context:** Still the opening scene, moments after Extract 1. The Cobbler has finally given a straight answer to the tribunes' question about why the shops are shut: "we make holiday to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph." The word rejoice detonates this speech. Marullus, who has so far only interrogated, now delivers the play's first passionate verse oration.

**Speaker:** Marullus, tribune of the people.

**Listener:** The assembled commoners, with Flavius beside him. The speech is public, and that is its point: Marullus is trying to shame a crowd into remembering.

**Before:** Flavius has asked "But wherefore art not in thy shop today?" and "Why dost thou lead these men about the streets?"; the Cobbler has joked that he leads them about "to wear out their shoes, to get myself into more work", then admitted the truth about Caesar's triumph.

**After:** Marullus finishes the picture of the old days, when the people's shout was so loud "That Tiber trembled underneath her banks / To hear the replication of your sounds / Made in her concave shores?" He then hammers the contrast with a triple question: "And do you now put on your best attire? / And do you now cull out a holiday? / And do you now strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood?" He dismisses them with orders to go home, kneel and "Pray to the gods to intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude." Flavius adds a gentler command to weep into the Tiber, and the commoners leave without a word.

**Meaning:** 'Why celebrate? What conquest has he brought home? What conquered peoples, paying tribute, walk behind him to Rome as chained prisoners decorating his chariot? You blocks of wood, you stones, you things with even less feeling than that! You hard-hearted, cruel Romans, did you not know Pompey? Time and again you climbed up walls and battlements, towers and windows, even chimney tops, carrying your babies, and sat there all day waiting patiently just to watch great Pompey pass through the streets. And the moment his chariot came into view, did you not all shout together…' Difficult words: tributaries (subject peoples or rulers who pay tribute to a conqueror), captive bonds (the chains of prisoners of war), grace (adorn), senseless (without the power to feel), livelong (the whole, long), replication (echo), concave shores (hollowed, curving river banks).

**Subtext:** The first two questions are an accusation in disguise. A triumph was meant to display foreign captives and foreign wealth; there are none here, because Caesar has defeated Romans, the sons of Pompey. Marullus cannot say 'this triumph is illegal' to a festive crowd, so he asks them to notice what is missing from the procession. The reminder of Pompey is also a charge of betrayal: the crowd has transferred its love to the man who destroyed its old hero. But the speech contains an irony Marullus does not intend. His own picture of the people climbing chimneys to watch a chariot proves that they have always loved the spectacle more than the man. They cheered Pompey's chariot; now they cheer Caesar's. The very memory he uses to shame them shows that they cannot be relied on by either side.

**Character:** Marullus is passionate, eloquent and brave, and he has a sense of history that the crowd lacks. He is also a partisan: his grief for Pompey is personal, and his contempt for the crowd is open. He attacks the people he ought to be winning over, calling them stones and cruel men, and he ends by predicting divine punishment for them. He is the republic at its most sincere and least skilful.

**Theme:** Public opinion and its fickleness; ingratitude as a political sin; the illegitimacy of power won over fellow citizens in civil war; the contest between memory (Pompey) and spectacle (Caesar's chariot). Beneath all of these lies the play's central anxiety: a republic whose survival depends on a crowd that changes its mind.

**Device:** Marullus begins with a barrage of rhetorical questions, which demand agreement rather than information. He turns to invective (violent abuse) and apostrophe (direct address) in "You blocks, you stones", a metaphor that reduces men to lifeless objects, then intensifies it: they are "worse than senseless things", because stones at least cannot be ungrateful. The list of vantage points is a climax, an ascending order from walls to battlements to towers to windows to chimney tops, so that the crowd's devotion seems to rise physically before our eyes. The detail of infants in arms is pathos, an appeal to feeling. In the lines that follow, the personified Tiber trembling at the echo is hyperbole, and the repeated "And do you now" is anaphora, the repetition of an opening phrase, which turns the contrast between then and now into a drumbeat.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** A Roman triumph was granted by the Senate for victory over a foreign enemy. The procession moved from the Campus Martius through the city to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline hill: captives in chains and the spoils of war went before, and the general rode in a four-horse chariot wearing a purple-and-gold toga and a laurel wreath. A triumph for a civil-war victory was considered improper, and Plutarch records that Caesar's Spanish triumph of October 45 BC, which celebrated the defeat of Pompey's sons at Munda, was resented for exactly this reason. Pompey the Great had celebrated three triumphs and was for years the most popular man in Rome; he was defeated by Caesar at Pharsalus in 48 BC and murdered in Egypt shortly afterwards. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** Shakespeare places this triumph on the day of the Lupercalia, which historically fell four months later; the compression lets the tribunes' grievance and the crown-offering collide in a single afternoon.

**Scene connection:** The appetite for spectacle Marullus describes is exactly what Casca reports later in Scene 2, when the crowd claps and hisses Caesar "as they use to do the players in the theatre". The fickleness will become the engine of Act 3, where the same crowd is turned by Brutus and then by Antony within minutes. Pompey's name keeps returning: the conspirators gather at Pompey's Porch in Scene 3, and Caesar's death in Act 3 is staged in Pompey's shadow. Marullus's contempt for the people also anticipates Cassius's outburst in Scene 3 that Rome has become trash and rubbish for serving as fuel to Caesar's fire: the republicans of this play never quite respect the republic's citizens.

**Keywords:** rhetorical question; invective; apostrophe; metaphor; climax; anaphora; hyperbole; personification; pathos; fickle; ingratitude; tributaries; triumph; civil war; partisan; Pompeian.

::: model Model answer (i)
The speaker is Marullus, one of the two tribunes of the people, and he is addressing the crowd of working men whom he and Flavius have stopped in a street of Rome. The occasion is the public holiday for Caesar's triumphal return after defeating the sons of Pompey, which falls on the feast of Lupercal. The Cobbler has just admitted that the men have shut their shops in order to see Caesar and to rejoice in his triumph, and it is the idea of rejoicing at such a victory that provokes Marullus's anger.

**Why this answer earns marks:** Speaker, listener and occasion are given precisely (context and recall). The trigger for the speech is identified from the text, with the key word rejoice picked out, so the examiner sees that the student has located the extract exactly and understood why it begins with the question Wherefore rejoice.

::: model Model answer (ii)
Marullus asks what conquered peoples are walking behind Caesar to Rome as prisoners in chains, decorating his chariot as trophies of victory. In a proper Roman triumph the general displayed foreign captives and plunder to prove that he had enlarged the power of Rome. Marullus's question expects the answer none, because Caesar has not conquered a foreign enemy; he has defeated fellow Romans, the sons of Pompey, in a civil war. The question is therefore an accusation in disguise. Without ever saying that the triumph is improper or that Caesar is dangerous, Marullus invites the crowd to see for themselves that there is nothing to celebrate, and that the man they are cheering has spilt Roman blood.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The paraphrase is accurate and glosses the two hard words (tributaries, captive bonds). The answer then explains the historical logic of a triumph, which is needed to see why the question wounds, and finally answers the second part directly, explaining what is hidden and why it is an accusation. Understanding, context and analysis are all present.

::: model Model answer (iii)
Marullus describes a people so devoted to Pompey that they would climb to any height and wait any length of time for a glimpse of him. Two details stand out. First, the rising list of places, from "walls and battlements" up to "chimney tops": the climax makes the crowd's eagerness seem to grow taller as he speaks, and the comic exaggeration of the chimney tops mocks how far they were once willing to go. ::: model Model answer (iv)
The first device is the rhetorical question. Marullus opens with a volley of them: "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home?" and so on. They are not requests for information but challenges; each one forces the crowd to supply an answer that condemns them, so that they seem to accuse themselves. The second device is apostrophe combined with metaphor: in "You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things!" he addresses the people directly and reduces them to lifeless objects, then intensifies the insult by saying they are worse than stones, since stones cannot be ungrateful. The direct address makes the attack personal and inescapable, and the metaphor strips the crowd of the very humanity, memory and feeling, that he is about to appeal to when he recalls Pompey.

**Why this answer earns marks:** Two devices are correctly named (terminology), each is quoted, and each is explained in terms of its effect on the listener, which is what the word strengthens in the question demands. The answer does not stop at identification, the commonest way to lose marks on a device question; it says what the device does.

::: model Model answer (v)
Marullus's speech comes closer to proving his charge than he might wish, though not in the way he means. Immediately after it the commoners do not defend themselves; as Flavius observes, they "vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness". Their silence suggests that they had never thought about what they were celebrating, and that they can be shamed out of a mood as easily as they were talked into it. Later in Scene 2 Casca describes the same crowd shouting at every refusal of the crown, throwing up their sweaty nightcaps, and then forgiving Caesar "with all their hearts" when he faints, behaving, in Casca's words, like an audience at a play. Their reactions are instant and emotional, driven by spectacle rather than judgement. Yet senseless is the wrong word. The Cobbler shows quick intelligence, and the crowd's love of Pompey, which Marullus himself recalls, was real while it lasted. The truth Act 1 establishes is that the people feel strongly but remember briefly, which makes them dangerous rather than stupid. Marullus's speech proves their fickleness; it disproves the claim that they have no feeling at all.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The verdict is stated at once and then argued. The answer uses the immediate aftermath (connection to the lines after the extract), a second piece of evidence from Scene 2 (quotation and connection), and a counter-argument from the extract and the Cobbler. It distinguishes between fickle and senseless, which is the precise judgement the question invites, and it closes with a balanced conclusion in one sentence.

## Extract 3 — Flavius and the Feathers of Caesar's Wing

::: extract Extract 3 — Act 1 Scene 1, the closing lines of the scene
> **Flavius:** They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness.  
> Go you down that way towards the Capitol.  
> This way will I. Disrobe the images  
> If you do find them decked with ceremonies.
>
> **Marullus:** May we do so?  
> You know it is the feast of Lupercal.
>
> **Flavius:** It is no matter. Let no images  
> Be hung with Caesar's trophies. I'll about  
> And drive away the vulgar from the streets;  
> So do you too, where you perceive them thick.  
> These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing  
> Will make him fly an ordinary pitch,  
> Who else would soar above the view of men  
> And keep us all in servile fearfulness.
:::

**Context:** The end of Scene 1. The commoners have slunk away without a word after Marullus's tirade and Flavius's order to go and weep into the Tiber. For the first time the two tribunes are alone on stage, and the tone changes from public scolding to private planning.

**Speaker:** Flavius, with one anxious interruption from Marullus.

**Listener:** Each other. There is no crowd to persuade now, so what we hear is what the tribunes actually think and intend.

**Before:** Flavius has just watched the crowd leave and remarked "See whe'er their basest mettle be not moved", meaning: see whether even their low-grade spirit is not stirred by shame. That line is the hinge between the two halves of the scene.

**After:** The scene ends and the stage fills at once with Caesar's procession for the Lupercal, the very feast Marullus has just named. We never see the tribunes again. Near the end of Scene 2 Casca reports, almost in passing, that "Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence." The plan we hear made here is carried out, and it destroys the men who make it.

**Meaning:** 'They have disappeared, struck dumb by guilt. You go down that way towards the Capitol, and I will go this way. Strip the statues if you find them decorated with festive ornaments.' Marullus asks whether they are permitted to do this, since it is the sacred feast of Lupercal. Flavius replies: 'That does not matter. No statue is to be hung with tokens of Caesar's victories. I will go about and clear the common people out of the streets; you do the same wherever you find them gathered thickly. Caesar's power is like a bird's feathers that are still growing; pluck them out now and he will fly at an ordinary height, whereas otherwise he would soar so high that men could no longer see him, and keep us all in the fear of slaves.' Difficult words: tongue-tied (unable to speak), images (statues), decked with ceremonies (decorated with ceremonial ornaments such as scarves or garlands), trophies (tokens or memorials of victory), the vulgar (the common people), pitch (in falconry, the height to which a hawk climbs before it stoops on its prey), servile (slave-like).

**Subtext:** Flavius's plan is symbolic politics. He believes that if you strip the decorations from Caesar's statues you diminish Caesar, as if power were made of scarves. Marullus's hesitation is partly religious, since interfering with a sacred festival is sacrilege, and partly fearful; Flavius's "It is no matter" tells us how far his hostility outruns his caution. The falconry image betrays something the tribunes may not fully realise about themselves: they do not accuse Caesar of any crime. Their fear is that he will rise beyond the sight of ordinary men, beyond scrutiny, beyond control. That is a republican fear of altitude, not a moral judgement of the man. It is also an admission that Caesar is by nature a bird of prey who "would soar"; only artificial clipping can keep him low.

**Character:** Flavius is decisive, bold and unafraid of religious scruple; he is also politically naive, mistaking ornaments for power and making an enemy of the crowd he needs. Marullus, so fiery a moment ago, is here the cautious one, which shows that even the republic's defenders are divided about how far they dare go. Together they are the play's first portrait of opposition to Caesar: sincere, courageous, and doomed.

**Theme:** Liberty and tyranny, stated for the first time in the play's own vocabulary, servile fearfulness against the freedom of Romans. The theme of checking power early, before it is too high to reach, which Cassius will turn into a whole philosophy. Appearance versus reality, in the tribunes' faith that removing the appearance of kingship will remove the thing. Religion against politics, in Marullus's scruple. And fear, the emotion that Caesar's rise produces in almost everyone who watches it.

**Device:** The governing device is an extended metaphor drawn from falconry: feathers, wing, fly, pitch and soar all belong to the hawk. Caesar is the bird, his honours are his growing plumage, and the tribunes are the falconers who mean to clip him. The image is vertical: the whole danger is expressed as height, and the whole remedy as bringing him down to the level of other men. There is a pun in the earlier line on mettle and metal, spirit and base material. The scene closes on a sententious, summarising statement rather than a rhyme, a common Shakespearean way of ending a scene, and it carries dramatic irony, because the audience knows that Caesar will indeed be brought down, though not by tribunes and not by plucking feathers.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** The Lupercalia, held on 15 February, was an ancient festival of purification and fertility in which priests called Luperci ran through the city striking bystanders, especially women, with strips of goat-hide; a new college of Luperci had recently been created in Caesar's honour. The historical tribunes Marullus and Caesetius Flavus removed a diadem from Caesar's statue on the Rostra and arrested men who hailed him as king; Caesar had them deposed from office. Plutarch adds that the people hailed the tribunes as Brutuses, after the founder of the republic. Historically this happened in January 44 BC, before the Lupercalia; Shakespeare fuses the two events. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** Falconry was an aristocratic sport in Shakespeare's England, and pitch is an English technical term; the image is Elizabethan, and the audience would have felt its precision.

**Scene connection:** Marullus's mention of Lupercal is the bridge into Scene 2, which opens with Caesar arranging the "holy chase". The images decked with ceremonies become the scarves Casca reports, and the report of the tribunes being silenced is the first hard evidence in the play that Caesar punishes opposition, evidence that Brutus and Cassius hear together. Flavius's picture of a Caesar who soars above the view of men is answered within a scene by Cassius's Colossus who bestrides the world while petty men peep about beneath him; the tribune's feathers and the conspirator's giant are two images of the same fear. And "servile fearfulness" is the first sounding of the note Cassius will strike in Scene 3 when he swears that "Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius."

**Keywords:** extended metaphor; falconry imagery; iconoclasm; symbolic politics; sacrilege; scruple; servile; republican; dramatic irony; foreshadowing; sententious close; trophies; images; the vulgar.

## Extract 4 — Caesar, Antony and the Soothsayer

::: textnote About the length of this extract
This passage is a little longer than a board extract usually is, because the three moments named in it, the fertility instruction, the warning and the dismissal, form one dramatic unit and are best trained together. In an examination you would be given ten to fourteen lines from within it; the labels and questions below would apply to any such window.

::: extract Extract 4 — Act 1 Scene 2, the opening of the scene
> **Caesar:** Forget not, in your speed, Antonius,  
> To touch Calpurnia, for our elders say  
> The barren, touchèd in this holy chase,  
> Shake off their sterile curse.
>
> **Antony:** I shall remember.  
> When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed.
>
> **Caesar:** Set on, and leave no ceremony out.
>
> **Soothsayer:** Caesar.
>
> **Caesar:** Ha! Who calls?
>
> **Casca:** Bid every noise be still. Peace, yet again!
>
> **Caesar:** Who is it in the press that calls on me?  
> I hear a tongue shriller than all the music  
> Cry 'Caesar.' Speak. Caesar is turned to hear.
>
> **Soothsayer:** Beware the ides of March.
>
> **Caesar:** What man is that?
>
> **Brutus:** A soothsayer bids you beware the ides of March.
>
> **Caesar:** Set him before me. Let me see his face.
>
> **Cassius:** Fellow, come from the throng. Look upon Caesar.
>
> **Caesar:** What sayst thou to me now? Speak once again.
>
> **Soothsayer:** Beware the ides of March.
>
> **Caesar:** He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass.
:::

**Context:** The opening of Scene 2, moments after the tribunes have left. A flourish of trumpets and the stage fills: Caesar enters with his train, Antony stripped for the race, Calpurnia, Portia, Decius, Cicero, Brutus, Cassius, Casca and a crowd, among whom stands a Soothsayer. It is the feast of Lupercal, and Antony is about to run the ritual course. This is our first sight of Caesar, and Shakespeare shows him doing two things in quick succession: giving a domestic order in public, and receiving a warning in public.

**Speaker:** Caesar dominates; Antony, Casca, Brutus and Cassius each speak a line; the Soothsayer speaks four words, twice.

**Listener:** Antony and Calpurnia are addressed, but the true audience is the whole crowd. Everything Caesar says here is said to be overheard.

**Before:** Caesar has twice called "Calpurnia", and Casca has silenced the crowd with "Peace, ho! Caesar speaks." Caesar has told his wife to "Stand you directly in Antonius' way / When he doth run his course."

**After:** A sennet sounds and the whole procession leaves for the games. Only Brutus and Cassius remain, and Cassius opens the conversation of Extract 5 by asking "Will you go see the order of the course?" While they talk, off-stage, Antony offers Caesar the crown three times; we learn of it only from Casca's report later in the scene.

**Meaning:** Caesar tells Antony not to forget, as he runs, to touch Calpurnia, because the old belief is that childless women touched during this sacred race are freed from the curse of barrenness. Antony answers that he will remember, and adds that whatever Caesar commands is done. Caesar orders the procession forward with instructions to omit no part of the ritual. A voice calls his name; he asks who it is, Casca calls for silence, and Caesar says he hears one voice, sharper than all the festival music, crying his name, and that he has turned to listen. The Soothsayer tells him to beware the fifteenth of March. Caesar asks who the man is; Brutus repeats the warning; Caesar demands to see the man's face; Cassius brings him forward. Caesar asks him to speak again, hears the same four words, and dismisses him as a dreamer before ordering the procession on. Difficult words: speed (haste; here, the running itself), elders (people of former times), barren (childless), holy chase (sacred race), sterile curse (the curse of childlessness), set on (go forward), press (crowd), shriller (more piercing), soothsayer (a foreteller of the future, literally a truth-sayer), ides (the fifteenth day of March), dreamer (a man who sees visions; a fantasist).

**Subtext:** Caesar's first public act announces before all Rome that his wife is barren. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many readers take this as a hint of dynastic thinking, a ruler who wants an heir; the text does not say so, and leaves the motive open. What is certain is that Caesar treats a private matter as a public ceremony, and that Antony's reply is the language of a servant, not of a fellow consul. It tells us how power now works in Rome: Caesar speaks, and things happen. The Soothsayer episode is then a study in performance. Caesar hears one thin voice through the music, which suggests a man alert to threat; he demands to see the face, which suggests curiosity, even unease; he asks to hear the warning again, which suggests he has taken it in. Then, having listened in full view of the crowd, he dismisses it. He cannot be seen to fear, so he performs not fearing. The dismissal is as much for the onlookers as for himself.

**Character:** Caesar is commanding, ceremonious and image-conscious; he refers to himself by name, as if Caesar were a public office he occupies. He is superstitious enough to insist on every detail of a fertility rite, yet dismisses a warning about his own life, a contradiction the play will return to. Antony is devoted, athletic and obedient, the loyal lieutenant. The Soothsayer is calm, nameless and unmoved: he does not argue, he repeats. Casca plays the officious herald, silencing the crowd for the man he will be the first to stab. Brutus is the one who relays the warning to Caesar, and Cassius the one who brings the Soothsayer face to face with him: the two future conspirators literally deliver the prophecy.

**Theme:** Fate and free will, in the first prophecy of the play and the first refusal to heed it. Superstition and omens, and the inconsistency with which Caesar treats them. Appearance versus reality, in the gap between the public Caesar who fears nothing and the private man who turns to listen. Power and obedience, in Antony's reply. Leadership, in the question of whether a great man can afford to be seen taking advice from a stranger in a crowd.

**Device:** The dominant device is dramatic irony: the audience knows what the ides of March will bring, so Caesar's confident dismissal is heard against knowledge he lacks. The warning itself is foreshadowing, made heavier by repetition, since the Soothsayer says the identical words twice and Brutus repeats them a third time. Caesar's third-person self-reference, "Caesar is turned to hear", is sometimes called illeism; it separates the man from his public role. There is contrast between the loud festival music and the single shrill voice that cuts through it, an aural image of truth interrupting celebration. The dismissal ends in anticlimax: one word, "Pass", closes an episode that the audience knows to be the most important warning in the play.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** The Lupercalia was held on 15 February. The Luperci ran through the city striking bystanders, especially women, with strips of goat-hide, which was believed to promote fertility; Antony, consul in 44 BC, ran as one of them. Calpurnia had married Caesar in 59 BC and the marriage was childless; Caesar's only legitimate child, Julia, had died in 54 BC. Plutarch and Suetonius both record that a seer named Spurinna warned Caesar of a danger that would come no later than the ides of March. The ides were one of the three fixed marker days of the Roman month, falling on the fifteenth of March, May, July and October and on the thirteenth of the other months; they were sacred to Jupiter. Caesar was killed on 15 March 44 BC. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** Shakespeare makes the seer anonymous, moves the warning into a public crowd, and fuses it with the Lupercalia, so that celebration and doom occupy the same minute of stage time.

**Scene connection:** Antony's promise of instant obedience prepares for Caesar's later request in this scene that Antony tell him truly what he thinks of Cassius, and for Antony's loyalty after Caesar's death in Act 3. Caesar's dismissal of the Soothsayer is the first in a series: in Act 2 he will brush aside Calpurnia's dream and the priests' warnings, and on the ides he will meet the Soothsayer again. The barren Calpurnia of this extract is the frightened Calpurnia of Act 2. Casca's cry for silence is the first thing this man does for Caesar; the last is to strike him. And the warning about the ides is the thread that the Class X paper picks up in Act 5, when the conspirators speak of the work begun on the ides of March.

**Keywords:** dramatic irony; foreshadowing; omen; prophecy; soothsayer; ides; Lupercal; fertility rite; illeism; public persona; superstition; dismissal; anticlimax; fate versus free will.

## Extract 5 — The Mirror and the Oath of Honour

::: textnote About the shape of this extract
The mirror conversation and Brutus's oath of honour are separated by about thirty lines of Cassius's talk, so this extract is printed as two windows with the gap marked. An examination would give you one continuous window of ten to fourteen lines from either half; the labels and the questions below are written so that they serve whichever window you are given. Note also a small edition variant in the gap: where Cassius denies being "a common laughter", some editions print 'laugher'; the sense in both is a man who is a general joke, not to be taken seriously.
:::

::: extract Extract 5 — Act 1 Scene 2, Brutus and Cassius alone after the procession has left
> **Cassius:** Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?
>
> **Brutus:** No, Cassius, for the eye sees not itself  
> But by reflection, by some other things.
>
> **Cassius:** 'Tis just.  
> And it is very much lamented, Brutus,  
> That you have no such mirrors as will turn  
> Your hidden worthiness into your eye,  
> That you might see your shadow.

*(A little later, as a flourish of trumpets and a shout are heard from the games off-stage.)*

> **Brutus:** What means this shouting? I do fear the people  
> Choose Caesar for their king.
>
> **Cassius:** Ay, do you fear it?  
> Then must I think you would not have it so.
>
> **Brutus:** I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well.  
> But wherefore do you hold me here so long?  
> What is it that you would impart to me?  
> If it be aught toward the general good,  
> Set honour in one eye and death i' th' other  
> And I will look on both indifferently;  
> For let the gods so speed me as I love  
> The name of honour more than I fear death.
:::

**Context:** Scene 2, in the same public place, immediately after Caesar's procession has swept off to the Lupercal games and Caesar has dismissed the Soothsayer. For the first time in the play two men are alone and speaking privately, and the two men are Brutus and Cassius. Everything that follows in the tragedy begins in this conversation. Shakespeare gives it to us in real time while, off-stage and unseen, Antony is offering Caesar a crown; the shouts that interrupt the talk are the sound of that offer.

**Speaker:** Cassius leads in the first window; Brutus speaks the longest and most revealing lines in the second. The balance matters. Cassius asks questions and supplies images; Brutus, believing himself guarded, supplies the two confessions Cassius needs.

**Listener:** Each other, with no one else on stage. This is the play's first fully private exchange, so the audience hears both men without their public manners. Brutus, however, is still partly performing, for himself as much as for Cassius; his language stays formal and careful even here.

**Before:** Cassius asked Brutus whether he would go and watch "the order of the course"; Brutus refused, saying "I am not gamesome; I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony." Cassius then complained that Brutus has lately withheld "that gentleness / And show of love as I was wont to have." Brutus replied that the trouble is in himself, that he is "Vexèd" by "passions of some difference", private thoughts that may have spoilt his manners, and asked Cassius to read nothing more into his coldness than that "poor Brutus, with himself at war, / Forgets the shows of love to other men." Cassius seized on that: he says he has "much mistook" Brutus's mood and has therefore kept buried "Thoughts of great value, worthy cogitations". The mirror question is how he begins to dig them up. Between the two windows, Brutus asks warily "Into what dangers would you lead me, Cassius", and Cassius promises that he, as Brutus's "glass", will "modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of", and insists that he is no flatterer who fawns on men and then slanders them.

**After:** Cassius answers the oath at once: "I know that virtue to be in you, Brutus, / As well as I do know your outward favour. / Well, honour is the subject of my story." He has taken Brutus's own word and made it his title. He then declares "I was born free as Caesar; so were you", and tells the two stories that fill the next thirty lines: the swimming match in the Tiber, when Caesar cried "Help me, Cassius, or I sink!" and Cassius carried him out as Aeneas carried Anchises from Troy; and the fever in Spain, when "this god did shake" and begged for drink "As a sick girl." A second shout brings Extract 6, the Colossus speech.

**Meaning:** Cassius asks Brutus whether he can see his own face. Brutus answers, correctly and a little pedantically, that he cannot: the eye does not see itself except by reflection, in a mirror or in other surfaces. Cassius agrees, and regrets that Brutus has no mirror of the right kind, one that would show him his own hidden worth, so that he could see his reflection as others see it. In the second window Brutus hears the shout and says he fears the people are choosing Caesar as king. Cassius pounces: 'You fear it? Then I must take it that you do not want it.' Brutus admits he does not want it, though he loves Caesar well, and asks Cassius to come to the point. Then he states his terms: if what Cassius has to say concerns the public good, he will look at honour with one eye and death with the other and regard both without preference, because, as surely as he hopes the gods will favour him, he loves the name of honour more than he fears dying. Difficult words: lamented (regretted, mourned), worthiness (merit, value), shadow (here, a reflected image), impart (tell, communicate), aught (anything), the general good (the welfare of the whole community), indifferently (impartially, without favouring either), speed (prosper, favour), the name of honour (the reputation and idea of honour).

**Subtext:** Cassius's mirror is a trap disguised as a compliment. A mirror shows you what is there; Cassius proposes to show Brutus what Cassius wants him to see, and to call it Brutus's hidden self. The image is well chosen for its victim. A vain man would be flattered to be told he is admired; Brutus is not vain, so Cassius flatters his modesty instead, telling him he undervalues himself. The word shadow is quietly double-edged: it means reflection, but it also means something less than the real thing, and the play will ask whether the Brutus that Cassius reflects is the real man or his shadow. Then the shout does Cassius's work for him. Notice that Cassius never says the word king; Brutus does. The sound from the games makes Brutus confess a fear he had kept to himself, and Cassius turns the confession into an admission of political will with one deft question. Brutus's "yet I love him well" is the whole tragedy in five words, a man already divided before anyone has asked him to act. His oath is meant as a defence, a way of saying 'I will hear you only on honourable terms', but it hands Cassius the key, because now Cassius knows exactly which word opens Brutus. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** many readers have noticed that Brutus loves "The name of honour", not simply honour, and have read in it a man more attached to the idea of himself as honourable than to the hard reality of right action. The text supports this reading without compelling it; a generous reader hears only the idiom of the time.

**Character:** Brutus is reserved, courteous and philosophical; he answers a loaded question with a piece of Stoic-sounding wisdom about the eye and reflection, and he refuses to be led anywhere without conditions. He is also already troubled, already fearing kingship, already loving the man he fears. His honour is sincere and his self-knowledge is incomplete, which is exactly the combination a manipulator needs. Cassius is patient, observant and endlessly adaptive. He has clearly been waiting for an opening; he tests with the mirror, retreats when Brutus asks about dangers, denies being a flatterer in a speech that is itself flattery, and then lets the shout speak. He is also, in his own way, sincere: his hatred of Caesar's elevation is real, and his claim to be Brutus's true glass is not wholly false, since he does see in Brutus a republican who has not yet admitted what he is.

**Theme:** Honour, named here for the first time as Brutus's governing value and immediately adopted by Cassius as a weapon. Appearance versus reality, in the mirror, the shadow and the veiled look of a man at war with himself. Self-knowledge and its limits, in Brutus's correct statement that no one sees himself unaided and his failure to ask who is holding the glass. Friendship and manipulation, which the extract refuses to separate. Republicanism and the fear of kingship, voiced by Brutus, not Cassius. The private man and the public role, since Brutus's love for Caesar is personal and his fear of Caesar is civic.

**Device:** The governing device is the extended metaphor of the mirror: face, eye, reflection, mirrors, shadow and later glass all belong to it, and Cassius's offer to be the glass turns metaphor into a plan of action. Brutus's reply is an aphorism, a general truth compactly stated, which Shakespeare often gives him to mark his reflective habit of mind. The off-stage flourish and shout are a dramatic device of sound: an unseen event punctuates the dialogue, and each interruption advances Cassius's argument more than any speech could. Brutus's oath is built on antithesis, honour against death, one eye against the other, and on the balance of "look on both indifferently". There is dramatic irony throughout, since the audience watches the trap being laid and watches Brutus walk towards it. Cassius's "Ay, do you fear it?" is an echo question, repeating the victim's own word back to him, a classic technique of persuasion.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** Marcus Brutus was pardoned by Caesar after siding with Pompey at Pharsalus, was made governor of Cisalpine Gaul and then urban praetor for 44 BC, the more prestigious of the two praetorships, and had close family ties to the Stoic-leaning Cato, whose daughter Porcia he married. Cassius, who had married Brutus's sister, received the lesser praetorship, and ancient sources count this among his grievances against Caesar. Plutarch records that anonymous messages urging Brutus to act appeared on his praetor's chair and his ancestor's statue; Shakespeare will turn these into the forged letters Cassius plans at the end of the scene. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** The mirror was a favourite Renaissance figure for self-knowledge and for moral instruction; a mirror in a book title promised a warning picture in which readers would see themselves. Shakespeare's audience would have caught the double sense at once: Cassius offers a glass, and a glass can be an honest reflection or a flattering portrait.

**Scene connection:** The oath is the hinge on which the whole scene turns, because Cassius's reply, "honour is the subject of my story", shows him reshaping his argument around Brutus's word. At the scene's end, alone, Cassius states the method in cold blood: Brutus's "honourable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed", and the letters he will throw in at Brutus's window will be paper mirrors, "all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name". Brutus's fear that the people "Choose Caesar for their king" is confirmed within minutes by Casca's report of the crown. The divided love of "yet I love him well" is the seed of Brutus's orchard soliloquy in Act 2, of his claim in the Forum in Act 3 that he loved Rome more than Caesar, and of the ghost that visits him in Act 4; and the honour that Cassius praises here is the honour Brutus will throw back at him in their quarrel in Act 4, when the subject of Cassius's story has become the standard by which Brutus judges him.

**Keywords:** extended metaphor; mirror and glass; reflection; shadow; aphorism; antithesis; echo question; off-stage sound; dramatic irony; flattery disguised as candour; honour; the general good; divided loyalty; manipulation; self-knowledge; Stoic.

> Why should that name be sounded more than yours?  
> Write them together, yours is as fair a name;  
> Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well;  
> Weigh them, it is as heavy; conjure with 'em,  
> 'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar.'  
> Now, in the names of all the gods at once,  
> Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed  
> That he is grown so great? Age, thou art shamed!  
> Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!
:::

**Context:** Still Scene 2, still the two men alone, a few minutes after Extract 5. Cassius has told his two stories against Caesar, the near-drowning in the Tiber and the fever in Spain, and has ended on his amazement that "A man of such a feeble temper should / So get the start of the majestic world / And bear the palm alone." A second shout and flourish come from the games. Brutus says "Another general shout! / I do believe that these applauses are / For some new honours that are heaped on Caesar." Cassius answers with this speech, the most famous passage in Act 1.

**Speaker:** Cassius, uninterrupted. Brutus says nothing for the whole speech and for the eleven lines that follow it, and his silence is part of the drama; we watch him listen.

**Listener:** Brutus alone. But the speech is shaped for two ears at once: for Brutus the Roman, who is told that Rome has shrunk to one man, and for Brutus the descendant, who is told that his name is as good as Caesar's.

**Before:** The Tiber and fever stories were personal: a Cassius who saved Caesar's life and watched him shake with fever, now forced to bow if Caesar "carelessly but nod on him". Brutus's remark about "new honours" turns the talk from the man to the city, and Cassius follows him there.

**After:** Cassius goes on without pause. He asks when any age "since the great flood" was famous for only one man, and plays on the city's name: "Now is it Rome indeed, and room enough / When there is in it but one only man." Then he reaches for the ancestor: "There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king." Only then does Brutus speak, and his reply is cautious and dignified: "That you do love me, I am nothing jealous. / What you would work me to, I have some aim." He asks not to be pressed further for the present, promises to consider what has been said, and leaves Cassius with a sentence to "chew upon": "Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us." Cassius is satisfied: "I am glad that my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus." Caesar then returns with his train, "The angry spot" glowing on his brow, and the scene moves to Caesar's judgement of Cassius and Casca's report.

**Meaning:** 'Why, man, Caesar straddles this cramped world like the Colossus, and we small men walk about between his enormous legs, peering around for a place to be buried without honour. There are moments when men control their own destinies. The blame, dear Brutus, does not lie in the stars under which we were born; it lies in ourselves, that we accept the position of inferiors. Brutus and Caesar: what is so special about the word Caesar? Why should that name be proclaimed more loudly than yours? Write the two side by side, and yours is as handsome a name; speak them, and yours fits the mouth just as well; weigh them, and yours is just as heavy; use them in a spell, and Brutus will raise a ghost as quickly as Caesar will. Now, by all the gods together, what food does this Caesar of ours eat that he has grown so huge? Our age should be ashamed. Rome, you have lost your stock of noble families.' Difficult words: bestride (stand with a leg on each side of), Colossus (a giant statue; here the famous one at Rhodes), petty (small, insignificant), peep about (peer around timidly), underlings (inferiors, subordinates), sounded (proclaimed, spoken aloud), become (suit), conjure (summon spirits by magic), start (raise, rouse), meat (food in general), breed (stock, lineage).

**Subtext:** The speech has three movements, and each attacks Brutus from a different side. The Colossus humiliates: it makes Caesar not merely great but monstrously disproportionate, and makes every other Roman, Brutus included, a small creature scuttling about beneath him. The word dishonourable is aimed with care, since Brutus has just sworn that he loves honour more than life; Cassius is telling him that under Caesar even his grave will be shameful. The lines about the stars then turn humiliation into responsibility. If the cause of Rome's condition were fate, nothing could be done and no one would be to blame; Cassius insists that the cause is human, which means that the cure is human too. He includes himself, "ourselves", "we", so the reproach does not sound like an accusation of Brutus but like a shared confession. The third movement is the subtlest: the game with the two names. Cassius never says 'you should rule instead of Caesar'; he says only that there is nothing in the sound or weight of the name Caesar that makes it superior, and lets Brutus feel the implication. The verb conjure reaches past the living Brutus to the dead one, the ancestor whose name could indeed raise a spirit in Rome, and Cassius will name him openly a few lines later. The closing exclamations turn personal envy into civic grief, so that the speech ends not with Cassius's wound but with Rome's. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** whether the envy or the grief is the truer motive is one of the oldest debates about the play; the extract contains evidence for both, and an honest answer says so.

**Character:** Cassius is a brilliant persuader and a passionate republican, but a passion for equality shades here into resentment at being outshone, and the speech lets us hear both. He is quick, physical in his imagination, scornful and grand by turns, and he is at his most eloquent when he is most personal. Brutus, silent throughout, is characterised by what he does not do: he does not interrupt, protest, or defend Caesar, and when he finally speaks he admits that he has some idea what Cassius is driving at. The listener who lets such a speech run to its end has already half consented. Caesar, absent, is characterised too, as a giant who has grown by eating something no one else was offered, an image that makes his greatness seem unnatural rather than earned.

**Theme:** Fate and free will, in the play's most quoted statement that men, not stars, make their condition, spoken in the same scene as a prophecy that will prove true. Power and its disproportion, the republic's horror at a city that holds only one man. Honour, in the "dishonourable graves" that await those who submit. Jealousy and the politics of names, since the speech argues that reputation is arbitrary and can be redistributed. Republican nostalgia, in the lament for an age that has lost "the breed of noble bloods". Individual against state, since Cassius's case is that one individual has swallowed the state.

**Device:** The opening is a simile, Caesar "Like a Colossus", developed into a visual image of giant legs and tiny men, and heightened by hyperbole; the world itself becomes "narrow" beside him. "Men at some time are masters of their fates" is a sententia or aphorism, and the two lines on the stars are an antithesis, our stars against ourselves, which makes the argument feel as balanced as a law. The passage on the names is a sustained tricolon with anaphora-like parallel imperatives, "Write them", "Sound them", "Weigh them", followed by a fourth, "conjure with 'em", so that the names are tested in four ways like coins and found equal. There is a metaphor of magic in conjure and "start a spirit", and a metaphor of unnatural feeding in "Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed", which makes Caesar's growth bestial. The last two lines are apostrophe, direct address to the abstractions Age and Rome, and exclamations that lift the voice from argument to lament; "breed of noble bloods" is alliterative and bitterly ironic, since Cassius himself belongs to that breed. Underneath everything runs rhetorical questioning, five questions in nineteen lines, each designed to be answered by the listener in Cassius's favour. In the lines immediately after, the pun on Rome and room, which many editors note were pronounced alike in Shakespeare's day, caps the speech with a bitter joke.

**Historical context:** **HISTORICAL RECORD:** The Colossus of Rhodes was a huge bronze statue of the sun-god Helios set up at the harbour of Rhodes in about 280 BC and counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world; it had fallen in an earthquake long before Caesar's time. The picture of it straddling the harbour mouth with ships passing between its legs is a medieval legend, not ancient fact, and Shakespeare's image depends on that legend. Lucius Junius Brutus led the expulsion of King Tarquin the Proud in 509 BC, by tradition the founding act of the Roman Republic, and Marcus Brutus claimed descent from him; this is the Brutus who could, in Cassius's words, "start a spirit". In February 44 BC Caesar had been made dictator for life, breaking the old six-month limit on the office, and had already held unprecedented honours; the shouts off-stage, in Plutarch's account, greeted Antony's offer of a diadem at the Lupercalia. Cassius had been pardoned by Caesar after the civil war and made praetor, but received the less prestigious praetorship while Brutus received the urban one, and ancient writers treat this as a grievance. Plutarch also reports that Caesar said he feared not fat, long-haired men but pale, thin ones, meaning Brutus and Cassius, which Shakespeare dramatises moments after this speech. Historically, too, Cassius followed Epicurean philosophy, which denied that the gods or the stars govern human affairs; the line about the stars sits naturally in his mouth. **LITERARY INTERPRETATION:** the talk of astrology ("our stars") is Elizabethan rather than Roman in flavour, and the audience of 1599 would have heard a familiar debate about whether the heavens fix a man's fortune.

**Scene connection:** The Colossus answers Flavius's hawk at the end of Scene 1: there Caesar was to "soar above the view of men", here he towers over them, and both images turn the fear of Caesar into a problem of height and scale. Cassius's "dishonourable graves" picks up Brutus's own oath about honour and death from Extract 5 and threatens him with the one thing he said he did not fear, a death without honour. The claim that the fault is in ourselves is answered in the same scene by Caesar's judgement that "Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous.", a judgement that is itself a kind of fate closing in. In Scene 3 Cassius repeats the method with Casca, describing a man "no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action, yet prodigious grown", and he turns the argument about names into action with the forged letters that will praise Brutus's name and glance at Caesar's ambition. Brutus's reply, that he would rather be a villager than call himself a son of Rome under such conditions, is his first step; the orchard soliloquy of Act 2, where he reasons his way to Caesar's death, is the second. And the confident denial of the stars returns with heavy irony on the battlefield in Act 5, where Cassius admits that he has come partly to credit the omens he once dismissed, and dies on his birthday.

**Keywords:** simile; Colossus; hyperbole; sententia; aphorism; antithesis; fate versus free will; tricolon; anaphora; rhetorical question; apostrophe; exclamation; metaphor of conjuring; metaphor of feeding; underlings; republican nostalgia; envy versus principle; lineage; pun.

::: question Extract 6 — ICSE-style questions
**(i)** Who is speaking, to whom, and where? What has just been heard, and how has the listener explained it? [3]

**(ii)** Explain the image of the Colossus. Who are the "petty men", and what does Cassius mean by "dishonourable graves"? [3]

**(iii)** Explain the lines "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings." How does this view of human life compare with the Soothsayer's warning earlier in the scene? [3]

**(iv)** What does Cassius do with the two names Brutus and Caesar in this extract? Identify the devices he uses and explain what he hopes to achieve. [3]

**(v)** Cassius has been described as 'a patriot driven by envy'. Using this extract and the stories about Caesar that come just before it, say how far you agree with this description. [4]

**Reference to the context (Class IX style)**

**(a)** "Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" Who says this, and in what circumstances? Name the figure of speech and explain what the speaker is lamenting.

**(b)** "Men at some time are masters of their fates." Who is speaking to whom? Explain the statement and say why it is important to the speaker's argument.

**Multiple-choice questions**

1. The Colossus to which Cassius compares Caesar was: (a) a Roman general (b) a giant statue at Rhodes (c) a god of war (d) a mountain near Rome
2. In the extract, underlings means: (a) children (b) soldiers (c) inferiors or subordinates (d) rebels
3. Immediately after this extract, Cassius reminds Brutus of: (a) the Soothsayer's warning (b) the death of Pompey (c) his ancestor who would not tolerate a king in Rome (d) the storm over the city

::: model Model answer (i)
Cassius is speaking to Brutus in a public place in Rome on the feast of Lupercal. The two men are alone; Caesar and his followers have gone to the games, and Cassius has been working on Brutus's discontent, telling him how he once saved Caesar from drowning in the Tiber and watched him shake with fever in Spain. A second shout and a flourish of trumpets have just been heard from the games. Brutus has explained the noise as applause for some new honours being heaped upon Caesar, and it is this remark that prompts Cassius's speech.

**Why this answer earns marks:** Speaker, listener and place are given at once (context), the situation is fixed by reference to what Cassius has just been saying (recall and connection), and the question's two final parts, what was heard and how Brutus explained it, are both answered with Brutus's own phrase. The answer ends by linking the remark to the speech, which shows the student understands why the extract begins as it does.
:::

::: model Model answer (ii)
Cassius compares Caesar to the Colossus, the giant statue at Rhodes which legend pictured standing astride a harbour so that ships sailed between its legs. Caesar, he says, straddles the whole world in the same way, and the world has become narrow, too small for anyone but him. The petty men are all other Romans, Cassius and Brutus included, reduced to the size of insects walking about between the giant's legs. The dishonourable graves are the shameful deaths that await such men: if they live out their lives as Caesar's inferiors, the only thing left for them to find is a place to be buried without honour. The phrase is aimed at Brutus, who has just declared that he loves honour more than he fears death; Cassius is warning him that under Caesar he will lose the one and still suffer the other.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The image is explained in both halves, the statue and what it stands for (terminology: simile, explained). The petty men are identified, with the important detail that Cassius includes himself. The dishonourable graves are glossed accurately and then connected to Brutus's oath a few lines earlier, which is the analytical point that lifts the answer above a glossary.
:::

::: model Model answer (iii)
Cassius means that the blame for Rome's condition does not lie in destiny, in the position of the stars at a man's birth, which astrologers believed fixed his fortune. It lies in men themselves: Romans are inferiors because they have agreed to be inferiors. The statement is a claim for free will, and it serves Cassius's purpose exactly, because if the fault is human then the remedy is human too, and Brutus cannot excuse inaction by calling Caesar's greatness fate. The view stands in sharp contrast to the Soothsayer's warning. The Soothsayer speaks as the voice of fate: a fixed day is coming, and the only choice is whether to heed it. Cassius speaks as the voice of will: nothing is fixed, and men make their own fates. Shakespeare places the two voices in the same scene so that the audience, who know what the ides will bring, can hear the irony. The conspirators will act as free men and will walk straight into the day the Soothsayer named.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The lines are paraphrased with the key cultural idea, astrology, explained (understanding). The function of the statement in Cassius's argument is stated (analysis). The comparison asked for is made directly, fate against will, with both positions characterised fairly, and the answer closes by naming the dramatic irony that results from putting them together (terminology and connection).
:::

::: model Model answer (iv)
Cassius sets the two names side by side and tests them as if they were coins. He asks what is so special in the word Caesar, and why it should be proclaimed more loudly than Brutus. Then he puts the names through four trials in a sustained parallel structure: written together, Brutus looks as fair; spoken, it suits the mouth as well; weighed, it is as heavy; used in a spell, Brutus will raise a spirit as quickly as Caesar. The devices are rhetorical question, parallelism or tricolon with repeated imperatives, and a metaphor of magic in the word conjure. His aim is to make Caesar's supremacy look arbitrary, a matter of mere sound, so that Brutus will feel that nothing but his own submission separates him from Caesar. He also hopes to wake the ancestor: the name Brutus can start a spirit because it belongs to the man who drove out Rome's kings, and Cassius names him openly a few lines later.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The question asks what Cassius does, which devices he uses and why; the answer gives all three in order. The devices are named precisely and illustrated from the text rather than merely listed, and the purpose is explained at two levels, the immediate one of making supremacy look arbitrary and the deeper one of invoking the ancestor, with a connection to the lines that follow the extract.
:::

::: model Model answer (v)
The description fits Cassius closely, provided that both halves of it are taken seriously. The patriot is real. In this extract his grief is for Rome, not only for himself: the age is shamed, the city has lost its breed of noble bloods, and the argument about the stars is a call to Romans to recover their freedom by their own will. He includes himself among the underlings and the petty men, which is the language of shared responsibility rather than private spite. Yet the envy is just as real, and it shows most clearly in the stories that precede the extract. Cassius dwells on the moment Caesar cried for help in the Tiber and on the fever in Spain when this god shook and begged for drink like a sick girl, and his fury is that so feeble a man has got the start of the majestic world and must now be bowed to. His complaint is not that Caesar is unjust but that Caesar is no better than Cassius, and the questions about the names, which prove only that Caesar is not superior, continue the same thought. The two motives are not in competition; they feed each other. Cassius hates Caesar's elevation because he is a republican and because he is Caesar's equal who has been made to feel small, and his eloquence comes from the wound. I agree with the description, with one refinement: envy supplies his energy and patriotism supplies his argument, and Brutus, who hears only the argument, is persuaded by the one while being moved by the other.

**Why this answer earns marks:** The answer treats the quotation in the question as a claim to be tested, not accepted. It supplies evidence for patriotism from the extract and evidence for envy from the preceding stories, as the question requires, with accurate paraphrase of both. It then does the hardest thing a 4-mark evaluation asks, relating the two motives to each other rather than merely listing them, and ends with a personal judgement stated in a single precise sentence that returns to Brutus and the extract's purpose.
:::

::: model Model answers — reference to the context and MCQs
**(a)** Cassius says this to Brutus, when the two are alone during the Lupercal games, at the climax of his speech about Caesar's monstrous greatness. The line is an apostrophe, a direct address to the city as if it were a person, and an exclamation. Cassius laments that Rome, once famous for producing many great men in every generation, has lost its stock of noble families and now holds only one man; the republic of equals has shrunk to a single giant and a crowd of underlings.

**(b)** Cassius is speaking to Brutus. He means that there are times when men hold their destiny in their own hands and can shape it by their actions. The statement is central to his argument because he must persuade Brutus that Caesar's rise is not fate but the result of Roman submission; if men can master their fates, then Brutus can and must act. It also tells us about Cassius himself, a man who refuses to believe in powers beyond human control, and it sets up the play's long argument between free will and the prophecy of the ides.

**Why these answers earn marks:** Each fixes speaker, listener and moment, then names the device where asked, then explains meaning and purpose. Answer (b) goes one step further by relating the line to the speaker's character and to the play's theme, which is how a 'reference to the context' answer shows understanding beyond recall.

**MCQ key:** 1 (b); 2 (c); 3 (c). In question 3, option (a) is a common error, because students remember the Soothsayer from the same scene; but it is the ancestor Brutus, not the Soothsayer, whom Cassius invokes to close his argument.

## What These Six Extracts Have Trained

Look back at the six and you will see that they are not six random passages but a graded course. Extracts 1 and 2 taught you to read a public quarrel: who has authority, who has wit, what the crowd is for. Extract 3 taught you to unpack a single governing image, the hawk, and to see a scene's close as a promise about the future. Extract 4 taught you to follow a sequence of reactions step by step and to hear dramatic irony. Extract 5 taught you to watch a conversation as a contest, and to notice which speaker supplies the key words. Extract 6 taught you to break a long speech into movements and to weigh motive against argument. Those are the six skills a drama extract question tests, in any act.
