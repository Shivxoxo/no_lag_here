Every writer must treat this file as binding. Where you are unsure of a fact not stated here, either verify it yourself (WebFetch a reliable source) or phrase it as an interpretation / "scholars suggest". NEVER invent quotations, line numbers, historical dates, or ICSE rules.

## 1. THE PLAY TEXT (PRIORITY 1)
- Canonical text for quotation: `ACT1_FOLGER.txt` (Folger Shakespeare Library edition; line numbers are Folger numbers). A second public-domain text with British/Globe-edition spellings is in `ACT1_MIT.txt` (MIT/Moby) and `act1_gutenberg.txt` (Project Gutenberg).
- QUOTE ONLY WORDS THAT APPEAR IN THESE FILES. Quote short passages (1–6 lines). For longer passages, paraphrase.
- Spelling policy: use British spellings as found in the MIT/Gutenberg text where Folger has American ones (honour, labouring, colour, favour, offence, humour). Both are Shakespeare's text; only spelling differs. Keep Folger's wording otherwise.
- Line numbers: DO NOT print line numbers in the study book at all. ICSE editions number lines differently from Folger. Refer to positions as "early in the scene", "after Caesar's procession leaves", "the Soothsayer exchange", etc.
- Name spellings vary by edition. The tribune is "Marullus" (Folger, MIT, Gutenberg) and "Murellus" (Folio 1.1, Oxford/Arden editions). Caesar's wife is "Calpurnia" (MIT/Gutenberg, most ICSE editions) and "Calphurnia" (Folger/Folio). Use "Marullus" and "Calpurnia" consistently and note the variants ONCE early in the book. "Decius Brutus" is Shakespeare's name (via North's Plutarch) for the historical Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus. Antony is "Antony"; Caesar calls him "Antonius" in 1.2.
- Casca's report of the crown-offering in 1.2 is REPORTED (narrated), not staged. The Soothsayer speaks to Caesar in 1.2; Caesar calls him "a dreamer". Cicero appears in 1.2 (silent; Casca says he spoke Greek) and in 1.3 (speaks with Casca, then exits before Cassius enters). Cinna enters in 1.3 after Casca joins Cassius. Cassius tells Casca that Metellus Cimber, Decius Brutus and Trebonius are among those waiting at "Pompey's porch". In 1.3 Cassius gives Cinna letters to place in the praetor's chair, throw in at Brutus's window, and set up with wax on old Brutus's statue. Casca's "Greek to me" line: "but, for mine own part, it was Greek to me."
- Scene 1 speakers: Flavius, Marullus, Carpenter, Cobbler (Folger names them "Carpenter"/"Cobbler"; MIT calls them "First Commoner"/"Second Commoner"). Scene 1 commoners speak PROSE; tribunes speak VERSE. The Cobbler puns: soles/souls, "be not out with me… if you be out… I can mend you", "all that I live by is with the awl", "withal", "recover" (re-cover), "surgeon to old shoes", "as proper men as ever trod upon neat's leather".
- Key 1.1 verse: Marullus's "Wherefore rejoice? What conquest brings he home? / What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels? / You blocks, you stones, you worse than senseless things! / O you hard hearts, you cruel men of Rome, / Knew you not Pompey?" … "That Tiber trembled underneath her banks / To hear the replication of your sounds / Made in her concave shores?" … "And do you now put on your best attire? / And do you now cull out a holiday? / And do you now strew flowers in his way / That comes in triumph over Pompey's blood?" … "Pray to the gods to intermit the plague / That needs must light on this ingratitude." Flavius: "See whe'er their basest mettle be not moved. / They vanish tongue-tied in their guiltiness." … "Disrobe the images / If you do find them decked with ceremonies." Marullus: "May we do so? / You know it is the feast of Lupercal." Flavius: "It is no matter. Let no images / Be hung with Caesar's trophies." … "These growing feathers plucked from Caesar's wing / Will make him fly an ordinary pitch, / Who else would soar above the view of men / And keep us all in servile fearfulness."
- Key 1.2 lines (Folger wording; apply British spelling): Caesar: "Forget not, in your speed, Antonius, / To touch Calpurnia, for our elders say / The barren, touchèd in this holy chase, / Shake off their sterile curse." Antony: "I shall remember. / When Caesar says 'Do this,' it is performed." Caesar: "Set on, and leave no ceremony out." Soothsayer: "Beware the ides of March." Caesar: "He is a dreamer. Let us leave him. Pass." Brutus: "I am not gamesome; I do lack some part / Of that quick spirit that is in Antony." Cassius: "Brutus, I do observe you now of late; / I have not from your eyes that gentleness / And show of love as I was wont to have." Brutus: "Vexèd I am / Of late with passions of some difference, / Conceptions only proper to myself, / Which give some soil, perhaps, to my behaviors" … "poor Brutus, with himself at war, / Forgets the shows of love to other men." Cassius: "Tell me, good Brutus, can you see your face?" Brutus: "No, Cassius, for the eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things." Cassius: "And it is very much lamented, Brutus, / That you have no such mirrors as will turn / Your hidden worthiness into your eye, / That you might see your shadow." … "And since you know you cannot see yourself / So well as by reflection, I, your glass, / Will modestly discover to yourself / That of yourself which you yet know not of." … "Were I a common laughter" (Folger has "laughter"; MIT/Gutenberg "laugher") … "then hold me dangerous." [Flourish and shout.] Brutus: "What means this shouting? I do fear the people / Choose Caesar for their king." Cassius: "Ay, do you fear it? / Then must I think you would not have it so." Brutus: "I would not, Cassius, yet I love him well." … "If it be aught toward the general good, / Set honor in one eye and death i' th' other, / And I will look on both indifferently; / For let the gods so speed me as I love / The name of honor more than I fear death." Cassius: "I know that virtue to be in you, Brutus, / As well as I do know your outward favor. / Well, honor is the subject of my story." … "I was born free as Caesar; so were you; / We both have fed as well, and we can both / Endure the winter's cold as well as he." Tiber anecdote: "Caesar said to me 'Dar'st thou, Cassius, now / Leap in with me into this angry flood / And swim to yonder point?'" … "But ere we could arrive the point proposed, / Caesar cried 'Help me, Cassius, or I sink!'" … "I, as Aeneas, our great ancestor, / Did from the flames of Troy upon his shoulder / The old Anchises bear, so from the waves of Tiber / Did I the tirèd Caesar. And this man / Is now become a god, and Cassius is / A wretched creature and must bend his body / If Caesar carelessly but nod on him." Fever in Spain: "He had a fever when he was in Spain, / And when the fit was on him, I did mark / How he did shake. 'Tis true, this god did shake. / His coward lips did from their color fly, / And that same eye whose bend doth awe the world / Did lose his luster. I did hear him groan, / Ay, and that tongue of his that bade the Romans / Mark him and write his speeches in their books, / 'Alas,' it cried 'Give me some drink, Titinius' / As a sick girl." … "Ye gods, it doth amaze me / A man of such a feeble temper should / So get the start of the majestic world / And bear the palm alone." [Shout. Flourish.] Brutus: "Another general shout! / I do believe that these applauses are / For some new honors that are heaped on Caesar." Cassius: "Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus, and we petty men / Walk under his huge legs and peep about / To find ourselves dishonorable graves. / Men at some time are masters of their fates. / The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings. / 'Brutus' and 'Caesar'—what should be in that 'Caesar'? / Why should that name be sounded more than yours? / Write them together, yours is as fair a name; / Sound them, it doth become the mouth as well; / Weigh them, it is as heavy; conjure with 'em, / 'Brutus' will start a spirit as soon as 'Caesar.' / Now, in the names of all the gods at once, / Upon what meat doth this our Caesar feed / That he is grown so great? Age, thou art shamed! / Rome, thou hast lost the breed of noble bloods!" … "There was a Brutus once that would have brooked / Th' eternal devil to keep his state in Rome / As easily as a king." Brutus: "That you do love me, I am nothing jealous. / What you would work me to, I have some aim." … "Brutus had rather be a villager / Than to repute himself a son of Rome / Under these hard conditions as this time / Is like to lay upon us." Cassius: "I am glad that my weak words / Have struck but thus much show of fire from Brutus." Brutus (on Caesar's return): "The angry spot doth glow on Caesar's brow, / And all the rest look like a chidden train. / Calpurnia's cheek is pale, and Cicero / Looks with such ferret and such fiery eyes / As we have seen him in the Capitol, / Being crossed in conference by some senators." Caesar to Antony: "Let me have men about me that are fat, / Sleek-headed men, and such as sleep a-nights. / Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look. / He thinks too much. Such men are dangerous." Antony: "Fear him not, Caesar; he's not dangerous. / He is a noble Roman, and well given." Caesar: "Would he were fatter! But I fear him not. / Yet if my name were liable to fear, / I do not know the man I should avoid / So soon as that spare Cassius. He reads much, / He is a great observer, and he looks / Quite through the deeds of men. He loves no plays, / As thou dost, Antony; he hears no music. / Seldom he smiles, and smiles in such a sort / As if he mocked himself and scorned his spirit / That could be moved to smile at anything. / Such men as he be never at heart's ease / Whiles they behold a greater than themselves, / And therefore are they very dangerous. / I rather tell thee what is to be feared / Than what I fear; for always I am Caesar. / Come on my right hand, for this ear is deaf, / And tell me truly what thou think'st of him." Casca's report: "Why, there was a crown offered him; and, being offered him, he put it by with the back of his hand, thus, and then the people fell a-shouting." … "he put it by once; but for all that, to my thinking, he would fain have had it. Then he offered it to him again; then he put it by again; but to my thinking, he was very loath to lay his fingers off it. And then he offered it the third time. He put it the third time by, and still as he refused it the rabblement hooted and clapped their chopped hands and threw up their sweaty nightcaps and uttered such a deal of stinking breath because Caesar refused the crown that it had almost choked Caesar, for he swooned and fell down at it." … "'twas not a crown neither; 'twas one of these coronets" … "He fell down in the marketplace and foamed at mouth and was speechless." Brutus: "'Tis very like; he hath the falling sickness." Cassius: "No, Caesar hath it not; but you, and I, / And honest Casca, we have the falling sickness." Casca on the crowd: "If the tag-rag people did not clap him and hiss him, according as he pleased and displeased them, as they use to do the players in the theater, I am no true man." … Caesar "plucked me ope his doublet and offered them his throat to cut." … "Marullus and Flavius, for pulling scarves off Caesar's images, are put to silence." … Cicero "spoke Greek" … "it was Greek to me." Cassius (of Casca): "he was quick mettle when he went to school." Brutus: "So is he now in execution / Of any bold or noble enterprise, / However he puts on this tardy form. / This rudeness is a sauce to his good wit, / Which gives men stomach to digest his words / With better appetite." Brutus: "Till then, my noble friend, chew upon this: / Brutus had rather be a villager…" Cassius's soliloquy: "Well, Brutus, thou art noble; yet I see / Thy honorable mettle may be wrought / From that it is disposed. Therefore it is meet / That noble minds keep ever with their likes; / For who so firm that cannot be seduced? / Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus. / If I were Brutus now, and he were Cassius, / He should not humor me. I will this night, / In several hands, in at his windows throw, / As if they came from several citizens, / Writings, all tending to the great opinion / That Rome holds of his name, wherein obscurely / Caesar's ambition shall be glancèd at. / And after this, let Caesar seat him sure, / For we will shake him, or worse days endure."
- Key 1.3 lines: Casca: "Are not you moved, when all the sway of earth / Shakes like a thing unfirm?" … "I have seen tempests when the scolding winds / Have rived the knotty oaks" … "But never till tonight, never till now, / Did I go through a tempest dropping fire. / Either there is a civil strife in heaven, / Or else the world, too saucy with the gods, / Incenses them to send destruction." Casca's prodigies: "A common slave—you know him well by sight— / Held up his left hand, which did flame and burn / Like twenty torches joined; and yet his hand, / Not sensible of fire, remained unscorched." … "Against the Capitol I met a lion, / Who glazed upon me and went surly by / Without annoying me." … "a hundred ghastly women, / Transformèd with their fear, who swore they saw / Men all in fire walk up and down the streets." … "And yesterday the bird of night did sit / Even at noonday upon the marketplace, / Hooting and shrieking. When these prodigies / Do so conjointly meet, let not men say / 'These are their reasons, they are natural,' / For I believe they are portentous things / Unto the climate that they point upon." Cicero: "Indeed, it is a strange-disposèd time. / But men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves. / Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?" Casca: "He doth, for he did bid Antonius / Send word to you he would be there tomorrow." Cicero: "Good night, then, Casca. This disturbèd sky / Is not to walk in." Cassius: "Who's there?" Casca: "A Roman." Cassius: "Casca, by your voice." … Cassius: "For my part, I have walked about the streets, / Submitting me unto the perilous night, / And thus unbracèd, Casca, as you see, / Have bared my bosom to the thunder-stone; / And when the cross blue lightning seemed to open / The breast of heaven, I did present myself / Even in the aim and very flash of it." Casca: "But wherefore did you so much tempt the heavens? / It is the part of men to fear and tremble / When the most mighty gods by tokens send / Such dreadful heralds to astonish us." Cassius: "You are dull, Casca, and those sparks of life / That should be in a Roman you do want, / Or else you use not." … "Now could I, Casca, name to thee a man / Most like this dreadful night, / That thunders, lightens, opens graves, and roars / As doth the lion in the Capitol; / A man no mightier than thyself or me / In personal action, yet prodigious grown / And fearful, as these strange eruptions are." Casca: "'Tis Caesar that you mean, is it not, Cassius?" Cassius: "Let it be who it is. For Romans now / Have thews and limbs like to their ancestors. / But woe the while! Our fathers' minds are dead, / And we are governed with our mothers' spirits. / Our yoke and sufferance show us womanish." Casca: "Indeed, they say the senators tomorrow / Mean to establish Caesar as a king, / And he shall wear his crown by sea and land / In every place save here in Italy." Cassius: "I know where I will wear this dagger then; / Cassius from bondage will deliver Cassius. / Therein, you gods, you make the weak most strong; / Therein, you gods, you tyrants do defeat. / Nor stony tower, nor walls of beaten brass, / Nor airless dungeon, nor strong links of iron / Can be retentive to the strength of spirit; / But life, being weary of these worldly bars, / Never lacks power to dismiss itself. / If I know this, know all the world besides, / That part of tyranny that I do bear / I can shake off at pleasure." Casca: "So can I. / So every bondman in his own hand bears / The power to cancel his captivity." Cassius: "And why should Caesar be a tyrant, then? / Poor man, I know he would not be a wolf / But that he sees the Romans are but sheep; / He were no lion, were not Romans hinds. / Those that with haste will make a mighty fire / Begin it with weak straws. What trash is Rome, / What rubbish and what offal, when it serves / For the base matter to illuminate / So vile a thing as Caesar! But, O grief, / Where hast thou led me? I perhaps speak this / Before a willing bondman; then I know / My answer must be made. But I am armed, / And dangers are to me indifferent." Casca: "You speak to Casca, and to such a man / That is no fleering telltale. Hold. My hand. / Be factious for redress of all these griefs, / And I will set this foot of mine as far / As who goes farthest." Cassius: "There's a bargain made." … "I have moved already / Some certain of the noblest-minded Romans / To undergo with me an enterprise / Of honorable-dangerous consequence." … "Now know you, Casca, I have moved already…" … Cinna: "Yes, you are. O Cassius, if you could / But win the noble Brutus to our party—" Cassius: "Be you content. Good Cinna, take this paper, / And look you lay it in the praetor's chair, / Where Brutus may but find it; and throw this / In at his window; set this up with wax / Upon old Brutus' statue. All this done, / Repair to Pompey's Porch, where you shall find us." … "Three parts of him / Is ours already, and the man entire / Upon the next encounter yields him ours." Casca: "O, he sits high in all the people's hearts, / And that which would appear offense in us / His countenance, like richest alchemy, / Will change to virtue and to worthiness." Cassius: "Him and his worth and our great need of him / You have right well conceited. Let us go, / For it is after midnight, and ere day / We will awake him and be sure of him."
- Scholarly notes: Caesar's deafness ("this ear is deaf") has no basis in ancient sources; it appears to be Shakespeare's addition. Caesar's "falling sickness" (epilepsy) IS in Plutarch (and Suetonius); modern historians debate the diagnosis. The 1.2 crown scene follows Plutarch's Lupercalia account (Antony offered a diadem; the people applauded the refusals), but Shakespeare adds Casca's cynical reading that Caesar "would fain have had it".

## 2. HISTORICAL RECORD (PRIORITY 5 — verified against Wikipedia, Plutarch via livius.org/ISE, Folger, Shakespeare Navigators)
- Roman Republic founded traditionally 509 BC when Lucius Junius Brutus led the expulsion of King Tarquin the Proud (Tarquinius Superbus). Marcus Brutus claimed descent from him. Romans had a deep cultural hatred of the title "rex" (king).
- Consuls: two, elected annually, each could veto the other. Senate: council of ex-magistrates/aristocrats, the central organ of government. Tribunes of the plebs (created after the first plebeian secession, 494 BC): sacrosanct, could veto legislation, protected plebeians; ten in number by the late Republic. Dictator: an emergency office, traditionally limited to six months; Caesar's perpetual dictatorship broke that limit. Patricians (old aristocratic families) vs plebeians (everyone else; by the 4th century BC plebeians could hold all offices). Libertas (liberty) and res publica ("the public thing") were core republican values; SPQR = Senatus Populusque Romanus.
- Late Republic crisis: generals (Marius, Sulla, Pompey, Caesar) commanded armies loyal to themselves; Sulla's dictatorship (82–81 BC) set a precedent for military strongmen.
- Caesar: born 12/13 July 100 BC. First Triumvirate with Pompey and Crassus (60 BC); consul 59 BC; Gallic Wars 58–50 BC (Alesia 52 BC); crossed the Rubicon c. 10–11 January 49 BC; Pharsalus 9 August 48 BC (Pompey defeated); Pompey murdered in Egypt 28 September 48 BC; Thapsus 46 BC; four triumphs in 46 BC (Gaul, Egypt, Pontus, Africa); Munda 17 March 45 BC (defeated Pompey's sons Gnaeus and Sextus and Labienus; Gnaeus killed, Sextus escaped); Spanish triumph October 45 BC — resented because it celebrated victory over fellow Romans (Plutarch, Life of Caesar 56: "men did not think it meet for him to triumph"). Dictator 49 BC; dictator again 48/47; dictator for ten years 46 BC; dictator perpetuo (for life) February 44 BC. Consul for 44 BC with Mark Antony as colleague. Assassinated 15 March 44 BC in the Curia of Pompey (part of Pompey's theatre complex) — NOT on the Capitol; Shakespeare relocates it to the Capitol. Roughly 23 wounds; around 60 conspirators. The seer Spurinna (Plutarch/Suetonius) warned Caesar of danger coming "no later than the Ides of March".
- Lupercalia: 15 February; ancient purification and fertility festival; the Luperci (priests) ran through the city striking bystanders, especially women, with thongs of goat-hide (februa) to promote fertility; a third college of Luperci, the Juliani, was created in Caesar's honour in 44 BC; Antony ran as a Lupercus while consul. Plutarch: Antony offered Caesar a diadem wrapped with laurel; the crowd applauded feebly at the offering and loudly when Caesar refused; Caesar sent the diadem to the Capitol (to Jupiter). Historically the diadem was offered/refused TWICE in Plutarch; Shakespeare's Casca says THREE times.
- Tribunes Gaius Epidius Marullus and Lucius Caesetius Flavus (44 BC): removed a diadem placed on Caesar's statue on the Rostra and arrested people who hailed Caesar as "rex"; Caesar had them deposed from the tribunate (and, per Suetonius, removed from the Senate). Plutarch says the people hailed them as "Brutuses". Shakespeare: Casca says they "are put to silence". Historically the diadem-on-statue incident came BEFORE the Lupercalia (January 44 BC); Shakespeare places the tribunes' action on the same day as the triumph and the Lupercalia.
- Shakespeare COMPRESSES: the October 45 BC triumph and the 15 February 44 BC Lupercalia become a single day in 1.1–1.2; 1.3 is the night before the Ides (i.e., 14 March) — a month passes silently between 1.2 and 1.3 (Cicero asks "Comes Caesar to the Capitol tomorrow?").
- Ides: in the Roman calendar the Kalends (1st), Nones (5th/7th), and Ides (15th of March, May, July, October; 13th of other months) were the three fixed marker days; the Ides were sacred to Jupiter.
- Roman triumph: granted by the Senate; procession from the Campus Martius through the city to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline; captives in chains, spoils, the general in a four-horse chariot in purple-and-gold toga with a laurel wreath, soldiers following; a slave reminded him of his mortality; a triumph for a civil-war victory over Romans was regarded as improper — hence Marullus's "What tributaries follow him to Rome / To grace in captive bonds his chariot wheels?"
- Pompey (106–48 BC): three triumphs (79, 71, 61 BC), hugely popular; married Caesar's daughter Julia (died 54 BC); Crassus died at Carrhae 53 BC; civil war 49–45 BC; the Theatre of Pompey (55 BC) with its portico ("Pompey's porch" in the play) — Caesar was killed in the Curia of Pompey attached to it, beneath Pompey's statue.
- Brutus (c. 85–42 BC): son of Servilia (Caesar's long-time mistress); the rumour that Caesar was his father is rejected by modern scholars on chronology; nephew (by marriage) of Cato the Younger, Stoic-leaning; sided with Pompey in 49 BC, pardoned by Caesar after Pharsalus; governor of Cisalpine Gaul 46 BC; urban praetor 44 BC (the more prestigious praetorship — Cassius got the lesser one; ancient sources make this a grievance of Cassius); married Porcia (Cato's daughter); anonymous graffiti/messages urged him to act ("Brutus, are you asleep?" / "You are no true Brutus") — Shakespeare turns this into Cassius's forged letters; died by suicide after Philippi, October 42 BC.
- Cassius (c. 86–42 BC): quaestor under Crassus, survived Carrhae 53 BC and defended Syria; tribune 49 BC; commanded Pompey's fleet; pardoned by Caesar; praetor 44 BC; Epicurean (relevant to his scepticism about omens — yet in the play he reads the storm as a sign); married Brutus's sister Junia Tertia; instigator of the conspiracy; died by suicide at Philippi, 3 October 42 BC. Plutarch reports Caesar saying he did not fear fat, long-haired men but the pale, thin ones — meaning Brutus and Cassius.
- Antony (83–30 BC): served under Caesar in Gaul from 54 BC; tribune 49 BC (his vetoes for Caesar and flight to Caesar's camp gave Caesar a pretext); commanded Caesar's left at Pharsalus; consul with Caesar 44 BC; ran as a Lupercus and offered the diadem; reputation for revelry (Caesar: "He loves no plays, / As thou dost, Antony"); after the assassination delivered the funeral oration; Second Triumvirate 43 BC (Antony, Octavian, Lepidus).
- Casca: Publius Servilius Casca Longus, struck the first blow on the Ides; tribune 43 BC; died after Philippi. Cinna: Lucius Cornelius Cinna, praetor 44 BC, a conspirator (distinct from Cinna the poet, killed by the mob in 3.3). Decius Brutus = Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus (81–43 BC), Caesar's trusted officer, named in Caesar's will, persuaded Caesar to attend the Senate. Metellus Cimber = Lucius Tillius Cimber. Trebonius = Gaius Trebonius. Cicero (106–43 BC): great orator, not part of the conspiracy; killed on the triumvirs' orders December 43 BC. Calpurnia (b. c. 76 BC): daughter of L. Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, married Caesar 59 BC, childless; Plutarch records her ominous dream before the Ides.
- Shakespeare's source: Sir Thomas North's 1579 English translation of Plutarch's Parallel Lives (Lives of Caesar, Brutus, Antony). Play written/performed 1599 (Thomas Platter saw it at a Bankside theatre, almost certainly the Globe, 21 September 1599); first printed in the First Folio, 1623. Elizabethan context: Elizabeth I was 66 in 1599, unmarried, no named heir — succession anxiety; killing a monarch was treated as a grave sin (Tudor "Homilies" against rebellion); yet tyrannicide was debated by humanists. Do not overstate: say "audiences may have felt" rather than asserting what they felt.
- Aeneas: Trojan hero, legendary ancestor of the Romans; in Virgil's Aeneid Book 2 he carries his aged father Anchises from burning Troy. Colossus: the giant bronze statue of the sun-god Helios at Rhodes (c. 280 BC; one of the Seven Wonders); the picture of it straddling the harbour so ships passed between its legs is a medieval legend, which Shakespeare's image relies on.

## 3. ICSE / CISCE FACTS (PRIORITY 3 — verified against the ICSE 2025 Literature paper and CISCE 2027 prescribed-books information)
- Paper 2, Literature in English: 80 marks, 2 hours (+ 15 minutes reading time), plus 20 marks Internal Assessment (two or three assignments, not exceeding 1500 words in total, e.g. character analysis, thematic study, literary appreciation).
- 2025 board paper structure (verified from the paper): "The paper has four Sections. Section A is compulsory — All questions in Section A must be answered. You must attempt one question from each of the Sections B, C and D and one other question from any Section of your choice." Section A: Question 1, 16 one-mark MCQs across drama, prose and poetry. Section B: Drama (Julius Caesar) — extract-based questions worth 16 marks each; in the 2025 paper each had FIVE sub-questions marked [3],[3],[3],[3],[4]. Sub-questions typically ask: who/where/what has just happened; explain a phrase in your own words; why does the speaker say X; what is revealed about character; what happens next; a short evaluative/personal-response question ("Which argument had the greatest impact? Give a reason"). Section C prose, Section D poetry, also 16 marks each.
- SYLLABUS SPLIT (very important, state it honestly in the book): For the ICSE (Class X) board examination the prescribed portion of Julius Caesar is Acts III, IV and V. Acts I and II are prescribed for Class IX (internal school examinations, usually "reference to the context" questions). Therefore Act 1 is NOT directly examined in the Class 10 board paper — but (a) the Class 10 paper constantly assumes knowledge of Act 1 (e.g., the 2025 paper asked what "the work the ides of March begun" refers to,), (b) Act 1 supplies the motives, characters and foreshadowing that every Act 3–5 answer needs, and (c) MCQs and "what happened earlier" sub-questions frequently reach back to Act 1 context. Frame the "ICSE exam relevance" material accordingly: "how Act 1 knowledge earns marks in Act 3–5 questions", plus Class IX-style extract practice for Act 1 itself.
- CISCE's stated skills for literature answers (paraphrase, do not fabricate exact wording): recall of the text, understanding of meaning, application/interpretation, analysis of character/theme/device, evaluation/personal response supported by the text. Do NOT claim any question is "guaranteed" or predict the board paper.
